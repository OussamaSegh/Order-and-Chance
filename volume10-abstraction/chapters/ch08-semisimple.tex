\chapter{Semisimple Algebras and Induction}

\begin{center}\itshape
A permutation of three objects gives a $3\times3$ matrix. Every such matrix fixes the line of constant vectors and preserves the plane whose coordinates sum to zero. Can we find all the pieces of every representation, and build those pieces from smaller groups?
\end{center}

Take the six elements of $\mathfrak S_3$ as a basis of a vector space. Multiplying basis elements by the group law and extending by linearity gives the six-dimensional algebra $\C[\mathfrak S_3]$. Its multiplication looks complicated in this basis. A different description is much smaller:
\[
\C[\mathfrak S_3]\cong\C\times\C\times M_2(\C).
\]
The first two coordinates are the trivial and sign representations. The matrix coordinate is the two-dimensional representation. The dimensions fit: $6=1+1+4$. Wedderburn's theorem will explain why every finite group has a decomposition of this kind.

Burnside did not begin as a believer in matrices. In the preface to the 1897 edition of \emph{Theory of Groups of Finite Order}, he wrote: ``while, in the present state of our knowledge, many results in the pure theory are arrived at most readily by dealing with properties of substitution groups, it would be difficult to find a result that could be most directly obtained by the consideration of groups of linear transformations.''
Frobenius' work on characters changed the situation. Burnside himself used characters in 1904 to prove that every group whose order involves at most two primes is solvable. The 1911 preface withdrew the earlier judgement: ``the reason given in the original preface for omitting any account of it no longer holds good. In fact it is now more true to say that for further advances in the abstract theory one must look largely to the representation of a group as a group of linear substitutions.''

The characters from Volume~3 now have an algebra around them. Wedderburn's theorem describes that algebra; induction brings a representation from a subgroup to the whole group. These constructions will prove Burnside's theorem and identify a normal subgroup in a Frobenius permutation group. In both arguments, traces reveal something that was hard to see in the multiplication table.

Unless another field is named, representations are finite-dimensional over $\C$, and groups are finite. A representation of $G$ is the same thing as a left module over $\C[G]$: extend $g\cdot v$ linearly in $g$. The rings here can be noncommutative. All modules are unital left modules.

\section{Semisimple modules and Wedderburn's theorem}

The line and plane in the opening example have no smaller nonzero invariant pieces. We need a name both for such a piece and for a sum of pieces.

\begin{definition}\label{def:semisimple}
An $A$-module $M$ is \emph{simple} when $M\ne0$ and its only submodules are $0$ and $M$. It is \emph{semisimple} when it is a direct sum of simple modules; the empty sum is allowed, so $0$ is semisimple. A ring $A$ is semisimple when its left regular module ${}_AA$ is semisimple.
\end{definition}

Maschke's theorem (Volume~3, Theorem~11.3) gives a direct sum of simple modules for every finite-dimensional complex representation. In particular it applies to the regular module $\C[G]$, so this algebra is semisimple. We only need this finite-dimensional assertion.

There is another example you can see directly. In $M_n(D)$, where $D$ is a division ring and $n\ge1$, let $L_j$ be the matrices whose only possibly nonzero column is column $j$. Left multiplication preserves $L_j$, and
\[
M_n(D)=L_1\oplus\cdots\oplus L_n.
\]
Each $L_j$ is a copy of the column module $D^n$. Given a nonzero column $v$ and any column $w$, choose a nonzero coordinate $v_k$ and a matrix whose $k$th column is $wv_k^{-1}$ and whose other columns vanish. It sends $v$ to $w$. Thus a submodule containing one nonzero column contains every column: $D^n$ is simple.

Schur's lemma works for modules over any ring. A nonzero map between simple modules has zero kernel and full image, so it is an isomorphism. Consequently the endomorphisms of a simple module form a division ring. For a finite-dimensional complex module, an endomorphism has an eigenvalue $\lambda$; its difference from $\lambda I$ has a kernel and hence is zero. This recovers the scalar conclusion of Volume~3, Lemma~11.4.

We can recover a ring from the maps of its regular module. The order of multiplication needs care.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:endA}
For a unital ring $A$, right multiplication gives an isomorphism of rings
\[
A^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}_A(A),\qquad
 a\longmapsto R_a,\quad R_a(x)=xa.
\]
Here $A^{\mathrm{op}}$ has the same elements and addition as $A$, with multiplication reversed.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The map $R_a$ is left $A$-linear because $(bx)a=b(xa)$. Moreover
\[
(R_a\circ R_b)(x)=(xb)a=x(ba)=R_{ba}(x).
\]
Thus composition corresponds to $a*b=ba$, precisely the product of the opposite ring. Addition and the identity are preserved too.

If $R_a=R_b$, evaluate at $1$ to obtain $a=b$, proving injectivity. For surjectivity, let $\varphi:A\to A$ be left $A$-linear. Put $a=\varphi(1)$. For every $x\in A$,
\[
\varphi(x)=\varphi(x\cdot1)=x\varphi(1)=xa.
\]
So $\varphi=R_a$.
\end{proof}

On a direct sum of several copies of one simple module, an endomorphism is a matrix. Between different simple types, every component is zero. That is the whole mechanism behind the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Wedderburn, 1908]\label{thm:wedderburn}
Suppose that the left regular module of $A$ is a finite direct sum of simple modules. Then there are division rings $D_i$ and positive integers $n_i$ such that
\[
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^rM_{n_i}(D_i).
\]
If $A$ is a finite-dimensional complex algebra, each $D_i$ is $\C$. For the zero ring, the product is empty.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Collect isomorphic simple summands together. We obtain a left-module isomorphism
\[
A\cong S_1^{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_r^{n_r},
\]
where the $S_i$ are pairwise nonisomorphic. Put $E_i=\operatorname{End}_A(S_i)$. Schur's lemma says that $E_i$ is a division ring and that $\operatorname{Hom}_A(S_i,S_j)=0$ for $i\ne j$.

Write an endomorphism of the displayed direct sum as a matrix of component maps. All blocks between distinct simple types vanish. The block on $S_i^{n_i}$ is an arbitrary $n_i\times n_i$ matrix over $E_i$, with composition given by matrix multiplication. Hence Lemma~\ref{lem:endA} gives
\[
A^{\mathrm{op}}\cong\operatorname{End}_A(A)
\cong\prod_iM_{n_i}(E_i).
\]
Take opposite rings. Transposition reverses the order of the matrix factors and of the entries, giving
\[
M_n(E)^{\mathrm{op}}\cong M_n(E^{\mathrm{op}}).
\]
This proves the result with $D_i=E_i^{\mathrm{op}}$.

If $A$ is finite-dimensional over $\C$, every $S_i$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space. The eigenvalue argument above gives $E_i=\C$, and taking the opposite ring does not change $\C$. If $A=0$, there are no simple summands and every displayed product is empty.
\end{proof}

For a group algebra, we know exactly which matrix blocks occur. The characters also tell us how to project onto one block.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:wedderburnCG}
Let $V_1,\ldots,V_r$ represent the irreducible complex representations of $G$, with characters $\chi_i$ and dimensions $d_i$. The action maps give an algebra isomorphism
\[
\C[G]\longrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^r\operatorname{End}(V_i)
\cong\prod_{i=1}^rM_{d_i}(\C),\qquad
 a\longmapsto(\rho_i(a))_i.
\]
The centre is spanned by the conjugacy-class sums and becomes the product of the scalar matrices. Its element
\[
e_i=\frac{d_i}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi_i(g^{-1})g
\]
acts as the identity on $V_i$ and as zero on every $V_j$ with $j\ne i$. In particular the $e_i$ are the central idempotents selecting the individual blocks.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The action map preserves addition, multiplication and $1$. If $a$ acts as zero on every irreducible representation, Maschke's decomposition shows that it acts as zero on the regular representation. Applying it to $1\in\C[G]$ gives $a=0$, so the map is injective. Source and target have the same dimension, since
\[
|G|=\sum_i d_i^2
\]
by Volume~3, Corollary~11.9. It is therefore an isomorphism.

An element $\sum_ga_gg$ is central exactly when conjugation preserves its coefficients. This means that $a_g$ is constant on each conjugacy class, so the class sums are a basis of the centre. In the matrix product, a central element must be scalar in each block.

The coefficients defining $e_i$ are constant on conjugacy classes. Thus $\rho_j(e_i)=\lambda_jI$ by Schur's lemma. Character orthogonality (Volume~3, Theorem~11.8) gives
\[
\lambda_jd_j
=\frac{d_i}{|G|}\sum_g\chi_i(g^{-1})\chi_j(g)
=d_i\delta_{ij}.
\]
Here $\chi_i(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi_i(g)}$, as in Volume~3. Hence $\lambda_i=1$ and the other $\lambda_j$ vanish. In the product algebra, these coordinates are idempotent, mutually orthogonal and sum to $1$, so the same is true of the $e_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:wedderburnsmall}
For $\mathfrak S_3$ the degrees $1,1,2$ give
$\C[\mathfrak S_3]\cong\C\times\C\times M_2(\C)$.
For $\mathfrak S_4$ they are $1,1,2,3,3$, giving the blocks of Figure~\ref{fig:wedderburn}.

Over $\R$, division algebras need not reduce to the scalar field. The classification of finite-dimensional real division algebras, quoted here without proof, gives $\R$, $\C$ and Hamilton's quaternions $\mathbb H$ (Frobenius, 1878). In particular
\[
\R[\Z/3]\cong\R\times\C,
\qquad
\R[Q_8]\cong\R^4\times\mathbb H.
\]
For the first identity, factor $X^3-1=(X-1)(X^2+X+1)$ over $\R$ and use the Chinese remainder theorem. The second has a direct proof in Exercise~\ref{ex:rq8}; it does not require the quoted classification. Thus the quaternions of Volume~3, Chapter~12, occur inside a group algebra.
\end{example}

The block decomposition tells us what all representations are made from. It does not yet construct those pieces. A subgroup often gives the first useful construction.

\section{Induced representations}

Let $H\le G$ and let $W$ be a representation of $H$. Imagine putting one copy of $W$ over each right coset $Hx$. A function $f:G\to W$ records the vector in each copy, but its values inside a coset must agree with the original $H$-action.

\begin{definition}\label{def:induced}
The representation of $G$ induced from $W$ is
\[
\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW
=\{f:G\to W:\ f(hx)=h\cdot f(x)
\text{ for }h\in H, x\in G\}.
\]
Its action is $(g\cdot f)(x)=f(xg)$. Restriction, written $\operatorname{Res}_HV$, keeps the vector space of a $G$-representation $V$ and uses only the action of $H$.
\end{definition}

Right translation preserves the condition on $f$, because
$(g\cdot f)(hx)=f(hxg)=h\cdot f(xg)$.
It is a left group action: applying $g_2$ and then $g_1$ evaluates $f$ at $xg_1g_2$, which is the action of $g_1g_2$. This check prevents an easy reversal of conventions.

Choose representatives $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ of $H\backslash G$, where $m=[G:H]$. A function in the induced space is determined freely by $f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_m)$, so
\[
\dim\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW=m\dim W.
\]
For trivial $W=\C$, these are scalar functions on $H\backslash G$. The group permutes their basis indicator functions. Inverting coset representatives identifies this with the usual permutation representation on $G/H$. If $H=\{1\}$, it is the regular representation.

Induction is built to make one kind of map easy to describe. Evaluating a function at the identity extracts exactly the information needed for a map of $H$-representations.

\begin{theorem}[Frobenius reciprocity, 1898]\label{thm:frobrecip}
For a $G$-representation $V$ and an $H$-representation $W$, evaluation at $1$ gives an isomorphism
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_G(V,\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW)
\cong\operatorname{Hom}_H(\operatorname{Res}_HV,W),
\qquad T\longmapsto(v\longmapsto T(v)(1)).
\]
Thus induction is right adjoint to restriction. For characters, with the inner product convention of Volume~3,
\[
\langle\chi_V,\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\psi\rangle_G
=\langle\operatorname{Res}_H\chi_V,\psi\rangle_H.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Given a $G$-linear $T$, define $S(v)=T(v)(1)$. For $h\in H$,
\[
S(hv)=T(hv)(1)=(h\cdot T(v))(1)
=T(v)(h)=h\cdot T(v)(1)=hS(v).
\]
So $S$ is $H$-linear.

Conversely let $S:V\to W$ be $H$-linear and set $T(v)(x)=S(xv)$. The function belongs to the induced space, since $S(hxv)=hS(xv)$. It also depends $G$-linearly on $v$:
\[
T(gv)(x)=S(xgv)=T(v)(xg)=(g\cdot T(v))(x).
\]
Starting from $S$, evaluation at $1$ gives $S$ back. Starting from $T$, the reverse construction gives
$S(xv)=T(xv)(1)=(x\cdot T(v))(1)=T(v)(x)$.
Thus the two maps are inverse linear maps. They also commute with maps in $V$ and $W$, so this is the adjunction of Chapter~1.

Decompose $V$ and any second representation into irreducibles. Schur's lemma shows that the dimension of the intertwiner space is the sum of products of their multiplicities, which is their character inner product. Applying this observation on both sides proves the character identity.
\end{proof}

For $G=\mathfrak S_3$ and $H$ the stabiliser of one of three points, inducing the trivial representation gives $\C^3$. The constant line is invariant. Its complement $x_1+x_2+x_3=0$ is the two-dimensional representation from Volume~3, Example~11.12. Figure~\ref{fig:v10new-ch09-induction} separates the two pieces; their dimensions add to the coset count.

We can compute an induced character without writing its whole matrix. Only the cosets fixed by a group element can contribute to the trace.

\begin{proposition}[the induced character]\label{prop:indchar}
If $\psi$ is the character of $W$, then for $g\in G$,
\[
\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\psi(g)
=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{\substack{x\in G\\xgx^{-1}\in H}}
\psi(xgx^{-1}).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Use the coordinate description $f\mapsto(f(x_i))_i$. Write $x_ig=h_ix_{j(i)}$ with $h_i\in H$. The $i$th output coordinate is
\[
(g\cdot f)(x_i)=h_i f(x_{j(i)}).
\]
Thus a diagonal block occurs exactly when $j(i)=i$, or equivalently $x_igx_i^{-1}\in H$. Its trace is then $\psi(x_igx_i^{-1})$. Sum these traces over the fixed cosets.

To replace the coset sum by a sum over $G$, note that the elements of $Hx_i$ are $hx_i$ for $h\in H$. Their conjugates of $g$ are $H$-conjugate, so all $|H|$ values of $\psi$ agree. Each fixed coset is counted $|H|$ times, giving the denominator. At $g=1$ the formula becomes $[G:H]\psi(1)$, as the dimension calculation requires.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:dihedral}
Write $D_n=\langle r,s:r^n=s^2=1, srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\rangle$, of order $2n$, with $n\ge2$. Its rotation subgroup $H=\langle r\rangle$ has index $2$. Put $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}$ and $\psi_j(r^k)=\zeta^{jk}$.

No reflection is conjugate into $H$, so its induced character is zero. The two cosets of $H$ give, on a rotation,
\[
\operatorname{Ind}_H^{D_n}\psi_j(r^k)
=\zeta^{jk}+\zeta^{-jk}=2\cos(2\pi jk/n).
\]
For $0<j<n/2$, this is the character of a two-dimensional irreducible representation. It can be realised by a plane rotation through $2\pi j/n$ and a reflection. Exercise~\ref{ex:dihedralmackey} checks irreducibility and fills in the one-dimensional cases.
\end{example}

Induction from the same subgroup can have very different outcomes: one character gives an irreducible plane, another splits into lines. Double cosets keep track of the difference.

\section{Mackey's formula}

For $H,K\le G$, a \emph{double coset} is a set $HsK$. Two such sets are equal or disjoint, and they partition $G$. A function induced from $H$ can be separated according to these supports. Right translation by $K$ never leaves one double coset.

For a representative $s$, put $K_s=K\cap s^{-1}Hs$. This subgroup acts on $W$ by
$k\cdot_{(s)}w=(sks^{-1})w$; denote this representation by $W^{(s)}$. The intersection ensures that $sks^{-1}$ belongs to $H$, where the original action is defined.

\begin{theorem}[Mackey, 1951]\label{thm:mackey}
Choose one representative $s$ of each double coset in $H\backslash G/K$. Then
\[
\operatorname{Res}_K\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW
\cong\bigoplus_s\operatorname{Ind}_{K_s}^KW^{(s)}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $F_D$ be the induced functions supported on a double coset $D$. Extending a restriction by zero still satisfies $f(hx)=hf(x)$, because $HD=D$. The supports partition $G$, so
\[
\operatorname{Res}_K\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW=\bigoplus_DF_D.
\]
Each summand is $K$-stable because $DK=D$.

For $D=HsK$, define $\varphi(k)=f(sk)$. If $k_0\in K_s$, then
\[
\varphi(k_0k)=f(sk_0k)
=f((sk_0s^{-1})sk)
=(sk_0s^{-1})\varphi(k).
\]
This is exactly the covariance condition for $\operatorname{Ind}_{K_s}^KW^{(s)}$. Evaluation along $sK$ commutes with right translation by $K$.

It remains to show that this map is bijective. If $\varphi=0$, then $f(hsk)=h\varphi(k)=0$, proving injectivity. Given $\varphi$ in the smaller induced space, define
$f(hsk)=h\varphi(k)$ on $HsK$ and zero elsewhere. If $hsk=h'sk'$, then
\[
k'k^{-1}=s^{-1}h'^{-1}hs\in K_s.
\]
The covariance of $\varphi$ gives
$h'\varphi(k')=h'(h'^{-1}h)\varphi(k)=h\varphi(k)$.
Thus the definition is independent of the expression for an element of the double coset. It supplies the inverse and proves the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mackey's irreducibility criterion]\label{cor:mackeyirr}
The induced representation $\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW$ is irreducible exactly when $W$ is irreducible and, for every $s\notin H$, the representations $W^{(s)}$ and $\operatorname{Res}_{H_s}W$ have no common irreducible constituent. Here $H_s=H\cap s^{-1}Hs$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $W=0$, both the assertion that $W$ is irreducible and the assertion that its induced representation is irreducible are false. Suppose $W\ne0$. Apply reciprocity, Mackey with $K=H$, and reciprocity again:
\[
\langle\operatorname{Ind}W,\operatorname{Ind}W\rangle_G
=\sum_s\langle W^{(s)},\operatorname{Res}_{H_s}W\rangle_{H_s}.
\]
These are nonnegative integer multiplicity sums. For the double coset $H$, choose $s=1$; its contribution is $\langle W,W\rangle_H\ge1$, with equality precisely when $W$ is irreducible. The full norm is $1$ precisely when this first term is $1$ and all remaining terms vanish. By Volume~3, Corollary~11.9, norm $1$ is equivalent to irreducibility. A vanishing term means that the two representations have no common constituent. Choosing other representatives gives isomorphic summands, so the condition can be stated for every $s\notin H$.
\end{proof}

\section{Burnside's \texorpdfstring{$p^aq^b$}{paqb} theorem}

A character value is a sum of roots of unity, so it is an algebraic integer. Recall that this means a root of a monic polynomial in $\Z[X]$. Chapter~5, Corollary~\ref{cor:integralclosure}, shows that sums and products of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers.

We shall also use a small fact about rational numbers. If $a/b$ in lowest terms satisfies a monic equation of degree $m$, multiplying the equation by $b^m$ shows that $b$ divides $a^m$. Hence $b=1$. A rational algebraic integer is therefore an ordinary integer. This direct argument avoids any additional number-field prerequisite.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:omega}
Let $\chi$ be an irreducible character of degree $d$, and let $C$ be the conjugacy class of $g$. Both $\chi(g)$ and $|C|\chi(g)/d$ are algebraic integers.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $g$ has order $m$, the matrix $\rho(g)$ satisfies $X^m-1=0$. This polynomial has distinct complex roots, so $\rho(g)$ is diagonalizable and its eigenvalues are roots of unity. Each eigenvalue is integral over $\Z$, and their sum $\chi(g)$ is integral too.

For the second claim, let $c_C=\sum_{x\in C}x\in\C[G]$. It is central and hence acts on the irreducible representation by a scalar $\omega_C$. Taking traces gives
$\omega_C=|C|\chi(g)/d$.
For any two classes $C,D$ we have
\[
c_Cc_D=\sum_Ea_{CDE}c_E,
\qquad a_{CDE}\in\Z_{\ge0}.
\]
Indeed the coefficient of an element counts products $xy$ with $x\in C$, $y\in D$, and conjugation makes that count constant on each class $E$. Acting on the representation gives the same relation for the scalars $\omega_C$.

Consequently $M=\sum_E\Z\omega_E\subset\C$ is a finitely generated $\Z$-module closed under multiplication. It contains $1=\omega_{\{1\}}$. Chapter~5, Proposition~\ref{prop:integralcrit}(c), applied to this finite module, shows that each $\omega_C$ is integral over $\Z$.
\end{proof}

The denominator $d$ in this lemma matters. If a class size is coprime to it, a character value cannot sit strictly between zero and its maximal size.

\begin{lemma}[Burnside]\label{lem:burnsidevanish}
Under the preceding hypotheses, suppose $\gcd(|C|,d)=1$. Then $\chi(g)=0$ or $\rho(g)$ is a scalar matrix.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose integers $u,v$ with $u|C|+vd=1$. The number
\[
\alpha=\frac{\chi(g)}d=u\omega_C+v\chi(g)
\]
is an algebraic integer by Lemma~\ref{lem:omega}. If the eigenvalues of $\rho(g)$ are $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$, then $\alpha$ is their average and $|\alpha|\le1$.

If $|\alpha|=1$, equality in the triangle inequality forces all the unit complex numbers $\lambda_i$ to agree. Since $\rho(g)$ is diagonalizable, it is a scalar matrix.

Suppose instead that $|\alpha|<1$. All $\lambda_i$ belong to the cyclotomic field $L=\Q(\zeta_m)$, where $m$ is the order of $g$. This is Galois over $\Q$ by Volume~3, Proposition~10.9. Each automorphism $\sigma$ maps roots of unity to roots of unity. Thus
\[
|\sigma(\alpha)|
=\left|\frac1d\sum_i\sigma(\lambda_i)\right|\le1.
\]
The product $\beta=\prod_{\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)}\sigma(\alpha)$ is fixed by every Galois automorphism, so it is rational by Volume~3, Theorem~10.6. It is also an algebraic integer, since each factor satisfies the same monic equation as $\alpha$. Therefore $\beta\in\Z$.

One factor is $\alpha$, of absolute value strictly below $1$, and all the others have absolute value at most $1$. Hence $|\beta|<1$, forcing $\beta=0$. Some $\sigma(\alpha)$ is zero; an automorphism is injective, so $\alpha=0$ and $\chi(g)=0$.
\end{proof}

The strict inequality in the last paragraph is the reason for multiplying all conjugates. A single algebraic integer can have absolute value less than $1$ without vanishing; its other conjugates then have to compensate.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:primepowerclass}
If a finite group has a conjugacy class of size $p^k$, where $p$ is prime and $k\ge1$, then it is not simple.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $g$ lie in that class. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $G$ is simple. Since the class has more than one element, $G$ is nonabelian and $g\ne1$.

Column orthogonality for $g$ and $1$ (Volume~3, Corollary~11.11) says
\[
\sum_\chi\chi(1)\chi(g)=0.
\]
The trivial character contributes $1$. If every nontrivial character with $\chi(g)\ne0$ had degree divisible by $p$, this equality would give $1+p\gamma=0$ for an algebraic integer $\gamma$. But then $\gamma=-1/p$, contradicting the rational-integrality fact above.

Choose, therefore, a nontrivial irreducible character with $p\nmid d=\chi(1)$ and $\chi(g)\ne0$. Since $|C|=p^k$, Burnside's lemma makes $\rho(g)$ scalar. The kernel of $\rho$ is normal and is not all of $G$, because $\rho$ is nontrivial. Simplicity makes it trivial. Thus $\rho$ is faithful. For every $h\in G$, the scalar $\rho(g)$ commutes with $\rho(h)$, and faithfulness gives $gh=hg$. This would make the class of $g$ a singleton, the required contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Burnside, 1904]\label{thm:burnsidepq}
For primes $p,q$ and integers $a,b\ge0$, every group of order $p^aq^b$ is solvable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We induct on $|G|$, starting with the trivial group. If $p=q$, or if one exponent vanishes, $G$ is a prime-power group. Such a group is solvable by Volume~3, Proposition~5.10(c). We may therefore assume $p\ne q$ and $a,b>0$.

Choose a Sylow $q$-subgroup $Q$. Its centre contains some $g\ne1$ (Volume~3, Corollary~3.10). Since every element of $Q$ commutes with $g$,
\[
Q\le C_G(g),\qquad
|g^G|=[G:C_G(g)]\mid[G:Q]=p^a.
\]
If the class has size greater than $1$, Proposition~\ref{prop:primepowerclass} supplies a proper nontrivial normal subgroup $N$. If the class has size $1$, then $g$ is central. The subgroup $\langle g\rangle$ is normal and nontrivial; it is proper unless $G$ is cyclic. A cyclic group is already solvable.

In every remaining case, $N$ and $G/N$ have smaller orders involving only $p$ and $q$. Induction makes both solvable. A group with a solvable normal subgroup and solvable quotient is solvable (Volume~3, Proposition~5.10(b)), finishing the induction.
\end{proof}

The smallest nonabelian simple group, $A_5$, has order $60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$. Three different primes are present. Character-free proofs of Burnside's theorem came much later, through work of Goldschmidt, Bender and Matsuyama around 1970. Here the short route was the arithmetic of character values.

\section{Frobenius groups}

Consider the affine maps $x\mapsto ax+b$ of $\mathbb F_q$, where $a\ne0$. A map with $a\ne1$ fixes exactly one point, because $(1-a)x=b$ has a unique solution. A translation with $b\ne0$ fixes none. Thus no nonidentity map fixes two points. The translations, including the identity, form a normal subgroup: composing two of them adds their parameters.

Now let a finite group $G$ act transitively on a finite set $X$ with $|X|>1$, and assume that only the identity fixes two distinct points. Call this a \emph{Frobenius action}; the acting group is a Frobenius group in the convention used here. For a point stabiliser $H$, the condition is
\[
H\cap xHx^{-1}=\{1\}\quad(x\notin H).
\]
The subgroup $H$ is a \emph{complement}. We allow $H=1$, the regular-action case. The theorem below says that the affine example's subgroup of translations has an abstract counterpart.

\begin{theorem}[Frobenius, 1901]\label{thm:frobeniuskernel}
In such an action, let $N$ consist of the identity and the elements fixing no point. Then $N$ is a normal subgroup, $N\cap H=\{1\}$, and $G=NH$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $H=1$, the action is regular, every nonidentity element fixes no point, and $N=G$. The conclusions follow immediately. Suppose $H\ne1$ and put $m=[G:H]=|X|$.

\emph{Count the candidate subgroup.}
There are $m$ distinct point stabilisers. Their nonidentity parts are disjoint, because an element in two would fix two points. Each has $|H|-1$ nonidentity elements, so
\[
|N|=|G|-m(|H|-1)=m.
\]
Conjugation preserves the number of fixed points; hence $N$ is a union of conjugacy classes. If it is a subgroup, it will be normal. We shall prove closure by identifying it as an intersection of kernels.

\emph{Compute induced values.}
For any class function $\psi$ on $H$, Proposition~\ref{prop:indchar} gives zero on $N\setminus\{1\}$ and the value $m\psi(1)$ at $1$. For $h\in H\setminus\{1\}$, any $x$ with $xhx^{-1}\in H$ must lie in $H$: otherwise that nonidentity element belongs to $H\cap xHx^{-1}$. Thus the induced value at $h$ is $\psi(h)$.

\emph{Extend a nontrivial irreducible character.}
For such a character $\psi$ of $H$, define the class function
\[
\theta_\psi=operatorname{Ind}_H^G\psi
-\psi(1)\operatorname{Ind}_H^G1_H+\psi(1)1_G.
\]
It is an integer linear combination of characters. The preceding values show that it restricts to $\psi$ on $H$ and equals $\psi(1)$ throughout $N$. Its values on all other elements follow by conjugation. Therefore
\begin{align*}
|G|\langle\theta_\psi,\theta_\psi\rangle_G
&=m\psi(1)^2+m\sum_{h\ne1}|\psi(h)|^2\\
&=m\psi(1)^2+m(|H|-\psi(1)^2)=|G|.
\end{align*}
Write $\theta_\psi=\sum_i a_i\chi_i$ with integers $a_i$. Orthogonality gives $\sum_i a_i^2=1$. Exactly one coefficient is $1$ or $-1$; since $\theta_\psi(1)=\psi(1)>0$, its sign is positive. Thus $\theta_\psi$ is an actual irreducible character, not merely a difference of characters.

\emph{Find $N$ as an intersection of kernels.}
For a representation of degree $d$,
\[
\chi(g)=d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho(g)=I.
\]
To check the reverse implication in this equivalence, write $\chi(g)$ as a sum of $d$ unit eigenvalues. A sum equal to the positive real number $d$ forces every eigenvalue to be $1$; finite order makes the matrix diagonalizable. Merely having $|\chi(g)|=d$ would only make it scalar, so the positive value is needed here.

Let $K$ be the intersection of the kernels of the representations with characters $\theta_\psi$, over nontrivial irreducible $\psi$ of $H$. Every element of $N$ belongs to $K$. Conversely, if $g\in K\setminus N$, it fixes a point and is conjugate to some $h\in H\setminus\{1\}$. Normality of $K$ puts $h$ in $K$. Hence $\psi(h)=\psi(1)$ for every nontrivial irreducible $\psi$; the equality also holds for the trivial character. Thus $h$ acts trivially in every irreducible representation of $H$, and so in the regular representation. Left multiplication by $h$ fixes the basis vector $1$ only if $h=1$, a contradiction. We have proved $N=K$.

Finally $N\cap H=1$, because a nonidentity element of $H$ fixes the chosen point. Since $|N|=m$, the set $NH$ has $m|H|=|G|$ elements and equals $G$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{fig:v10new-ch09-affine} shows the counting step in $\mathbb F_5$. A map with slope different from $1$ belongs to exactly one point stabiliser; a nontrivial translation belongs to none. The theorem proves that the leftover set is a subgroup even when there is no affine coordinate available.

\begin{beauty}
The proof of Frobenius' theorem found a subgroup without multiplying two of its proposed elements. Instead it extended characters from a point stabiliser and took an intersection of kernels. This is a useful habit: a difficult subset can become manageable when it is the set on which several maps vanish.

A further theorem, quoted here, says that a Frobenius kernel is nilpotent. Thompson proved it in 1959. Neither that result nor the classification of real division algebras is needed for any proof in this chapter.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

An element of $\C[G]$ can now be read one irreducible block at a time. Induction gives a way to construct representations before their blocks are known; Mackey's formula tests the construction by separating its double-coset supports. In Burnside's argument, a class sum acted as an algebraic integer. In Frobenius' argument, a character norm equal to $1$ turned a difference of characters into a single irreducible character. These are two different ways in which linear algebra controls a group.

For $\mathfrak S_n$, knowing that irreducible blocks exist is not the last word. We can label every block by a partition and calculate its size. The next chapter begins with the permutation modules induced from products of smaller symmetric groups. A diagram of boxes will select one irreducible piece inside each of them.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:doublecosets}
Let $H,K\le G$. Prove that
$\langle\operatorname{Ind}_H^G1,\operatorname{Ind}_K^G1\rangle_G$
equals the number of double cosets $HsK$. For $X=G/H$ with $|X|\ge2$, deduce that the action is doubly transitive exactly when $\C^X$ is the direct sum of the trivial representation and one irreducible representation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:dihedralmackey}
Apply Mackey's criterion to Example~\ref{ex:dihedral}. Prove that $\operatorname{Ind}\psi_j$ is irreducible exactly when $\psi_j\ne\psi_{-j}$, and list all irreducible representations of $D_n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rq8}
Prove both algebra isomorphisms
\[
\R[Q_8]\cong\R^4\times\mathbb H,
\qquad \C[Q_8]\cong\C^4\times M_2(\C).
\]
Use the four characters of $Q_8/\{\pm1\}$ and the inclusion of $Q_8$ into the unit quaternions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:heisenberg}
For an odd prime $p$, let $G$ be the upper unitriangular $3\times3$ matrices over $\mathbb F_p$. Construct its $p^2+p-1$ irreducible representations: $p^2$ linear characters and $p-1$ representations of degree $p$. Obtain the latter by induction from the abelian subgroup whose $(1,2)$ entry is zero.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:affinefrob}
For the affine group of $\mathbb F_q$, identify the characters $\theta_\psi$ in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:frobeniuskernel} with the nontrivial characters of $G/N\cong\mathbb F_q^\times$. Prove that exactly one further irreducible character is needed, of degree $q-1$, and construct it by inducing a nontrivial character of the translation subgroup $N$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:centrefaithful}
Prove that the centre of a finite group with a faithful irreducible complex representation is cyclic. For every irreducible representation, faithful or not, prove that its degree divides $[G:Z(G)]$. For the divisibility, consider the representations of $G^m$ on $V^{\otimes m}$ and the central subgroup with coordinate product $1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v10new-ch09-s3idempotents}
Let $r=(123)$ and $t=(12)$ in $\mathfrak S_3$. Compute the central idempotents for the trivial, sign and standard representations in the basis $1,r,r^2,t,rt,r^2t$. Verify their sum and pairwise products directly using $trt=r^{-1}$. Find the dimension of each ideal $e_i\C[\mathfrak S_3]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v10new-ch09-normalinduction}
Let $H\trianglelefteq G$ be abelian and let $\psi$ be a linear character of $H$. Show that $\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\psi$ is irreducible exactly when the stabiliser of $\psi$ under conjugation is $H$. Apply this to $G=\mathbb F_7\rtimes\mathbb F_7^\times$, inducing from the translations. Compute the induced character at every translation and every element with slope different from $1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v10new-ch09-c3real}
Prove directly that $\R[\Z/3]\cong\R\times\C$. Under this map, identify the two central idempotents and their ideal dimensions over $\R$. Explain why the two-dimensional real simple summand becomes two different linear representations after extension of scalars to $\C$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v10new-ch09-primeclass}
In $A_4$, determine the conjugacy classes and their sizes. Find a proper nontrivial normal subgroup using the class of a double transposition and Proposition~\ref{prop:primepowerclass}. Then construct all irreducible characters of $A_4$ and check explicitly the algebraic-integer scalars $|C|\chi(g)/\chi(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:doublecosets}
\idea A trivial representation contributes one invariant vector in every Mackey summand.
\writeup Reciprocity and Mackey give
\[
\langle\operatorname{Ind}_H1,\operatorname{Ind}_K1\rangle_G
=\sum_{HsK}\langle\operatorname{Ind}_{K_s}^K1,1\rangle_K
=\sum_{HsK}1.
\]
For the last equality use reciprocity once more: the restriction of a trivial representation is trivial.

Take $K=H$. Orbits of the diagonal action on $G/H\times G/H$ correspond to $H$-orbits on the second coordinate after the first is moved to $H$. These are the double cosets $HsH$. Since the action is transitive, the invariant functions on $X$ are exactly the constant functions: the trivial representation occurs once. Write the rest as $\bigoplus_i m_iV_i$ with nontrivial irreducibles. Then the permutation character has squared norm $1+\sum_i m_i^2$. This is $2$ precisely when the remainder is one irreducible occurring once. For $|X|\ge2$, the diagonal and the distinct ordered pairs are both nonempty, so two orbits are exactly double transitivity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:dihedralmackey}
\idea There is only one double coset outside the normal rotation subgroup.
\writeup The double cosets are $H$ and $HsH$. Conjugation by $s$ replaces $r$ by $r^{-1}$, so $\psi_j^{(s)}=\psi_{-j}$. Mackey's criterion now gives irreducibility precisely when $2j\not\equiv0\pmod n$. The characters for $j$ and $-j$ induce to the same representation, by their values in Example~\ref{ex:dihedral}.

The distinct two-dimensional representations are therefore indexed by $1\le j<n/2$. For a linear character, $r$ must equal its inverse because $srs^{-1}=r^{-1}$; hence its value is $1$, or also $-1$ if $n$ is even. The value on $s$ can independently be $1$ or $-1$. There are two linear characters for odd $n$ and four for even $n$. The square sums are
\[
2+4\frac{n-1}{2}=2n\quad(n\text{ odd}),
\qquad
4+4\frac{n-2}{2}=2n\quad(n\text{ even}).
\]
They exhaust the regular representation, so no irreducible is missing. For $n=2$, the two-dimensional list is empty and the four lines describe the Klein four group.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rq8}
\idea Separate the part where the central group element $z$ acts as $1$ from the part where it acts as $-1$.
\writeup Set $e=(1+z)/2$ and $e'=(1-z)/2$ in $\R[Q_8]$. They are central, satisfy $e+e'=1$, $ee'=0$, and are idempotent. Thus the algebra is the product of the ideals $e\R[Q_8]$ and $e'\R[Q_8]$. Each has dimension $4$: choose one representative from each pair $\{g,zg\}$ to obtain a basis $eg$, respectively $e'g$.

In the first ideal, $z$ acts as $1$, so it is the group algebra of $Q_8/\langle z\rangle\cong(\Z/2)^2$. Its four real linear characters give $\R^4$. In the second, the map induced by $Q_8\subset\mathbb H^\times$ sends $e'$ to $1$ and has image containing $1,i,j,k$. It is surjective onto $\mathbb H$ and has the same real dimension $4$, so it is an isomorphism.

After extension of scalars to $\C$, send $i,j$ to
\[
I=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
They satisfy $I^2=J^2=-1$ and $IJ=-JI$. The four matrices $1,I,J,IJ$ are linearly independent and span $M_2(\C)$. Hence $\mathbb H\otimes_\R\C\cong M_2(\C)$, giving the claimed complex algebra and its degree sum $4\cdot1^2+2^2=8$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:heisenberg}
\idea Use the centre to distinguish the induced representations, then use the degree sum to prove completeness.
\writeup With the coordinates in the hint, multiplication is
\[
(a,b,c)(a',b',c')=(a+a',b+b',c+c'+ab').
\]
The centre is $Z=\{(0,0,c)\}$, and commutators range over all of $Z$. Therefore the linear characters are exactly the $p^2$ characters of $G/Z\cong\mathbb F_p^2$.

The subgroup $A=\{(0,b,c)\}$ is abelian, normal and of index $p$. Fix $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/p}$. For each $\lambda\in\mathbb F_p^\times$, let
$\psi_\lambda(0,b,c)=\zeta^{\lambda c}$.
Conjugation by $(a,0,0)$ changes this to $\zeta^{\lambda(c+ab)}$. For $a\ne0$, choosing $b=1$ shows that the character changes. Mackey's criterion therefore makes $\operatorname{Ind}_A^G\psi_\lambda$ irreducible of dimension $p$.

On a central element $(0,0,c)$ this representation acts as the scalar $\zeta^{\lambda c}$: the scalar is already prescribed on every induced coordinate, since the element is central. Distinct $\lambda$ give nonisomorphic representations. Their degree squares add to
\[
p^2+(p-1)p^2=p^3=|G|.
\]
Thus they, together with the linear characters, are the full list. The number is $p^2+p-1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:affinefrob}
\idea A slope gives a quotient character; the translations supply the remaining representation.
\writeup The slope homomorphism $G\to\mathbb F_q^\times$ has kernel $N$, the translations. For any nontrivial character $\psi$ of the cyclic group $H=\mathbb F_q^\times$, the character $\theta_\psi$ has degree $1$, equals $\psi(a)$ on the map $x\mapsto ax$, and equals $1$ on $N$. It is exactly $\psi$ pulled back through the slope map. These, with the trivial character, give $q-1$ linear characters.

There are $q$ conjugacy classes: the identity; one class of all nonzero translations, because scaling multiplies their parameters transitively; and one class for every slope $a\ne1$, because a translation moves its unique fixed point to any prescribed point. Thus exactly one irreducible character remains. Its degree $d$ obeys
$(q-1)+d^2=q(q-1)$, so $d=q-1$.

For a nontrivial additive character $\lambda$ of $\mathbb F_q$, conjugation by a slope $a\ne1$ changes it. Indeed invariance would give $\lambda((a-1)b)=1$ for every $b$; multiplication by $a-1$ is a bijection, forcing $\lambda$ to be trivial. Mackey's criterion proves that $\operatorname{Ind}_N^G\lambda$ is irreducible of degree $q-1$, so it is the remaining one. If $q=2$, the complement is trivial, and the same argument gives the second linear character of $G\cong\Z/2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:centrefaithful}
\idea Scalars detect the centre; taking tensor powers makes a possible denominator grow too quickly.
\writeup Schur's lemma sends every $z\in Z(G)$ to a scalar $\lambda(z)$. For a faithful representation this embeds $Z(G)$ into $\C^\times$. A finite subgroup of order $m$ in $\C^\times$ lies among the $m$th roots of unity, by Lagrange's theorem; that set is cyclic, and so is each subgroup. Thus the centre is cyclic.

We first prove locally the degree-divisibility fact needed for the second part. Character orthogonality and Lemma~\ref{lem:omega} give
\[
\frac{|G|}{d}
=\sum_C\frac{|C|\chi(c)}{d}\,\overline{\chi(c)},
\qquad c\in C.
\]
The right side is an algebraic integer, while the left side is rational. Hence $d\mid|G|$. This also appears in Volume~3, Exercise~11.8, but the computation here makes the dependence explicit.

Now put $Z=Z(G)$ and choose any integer $m\ge1$. The representation of $G^m$ on $V^{\otimes m}$ has character $\prod_i\chi(g_i)$ and squared norm $\prod_i\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=1$, so it is irreducible of degree $d^m$. Its central subgroup
\[
Z_0=\{(z_1,\ldots,z_m)\in Z^m:z_1\cdots z_m=1\}
\]
acts trivially, since the product of its scalar actions is $\lambda(z_1\cdots z_m)=1$. The subgroup has order $|Z|^{m-1}$, so the representation descends irreducibly to a group of order $|G|^m/|Z|^{m-1}$. Degree divisibility for this quotient gives
\[
|Z|\left(\frac{[G:Z]}d\right)^m\in\Z
\qquad(m\ge1).
\]
Write $[G:Z]/d=a/b$ in lowest terms with $b>0$. The displayed integrality says $b^m\mid|Z|$ for every $m$, because $a^m$ and $b^m$ are coprime. This forces $b=1$. Thus $d$ divides $[G:Z]$, whether or not $V$ is faithful.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch09-s3idempotents}
\idea The two linear characters select the symmetric and alternating averages; the third block is what remains.
\writeup In the notation of the hint,
\[
e_+=\frac{A+B}{6},\qquad e_-=\frac{A-B}{6},
\qquad e_0=1-\frac A3=\frac{2-r-r^2}{3}.
\]
The character formula in Corollary~\ref{cor:wedderburnCG} gives exactly these expressions: the standard character has values $2,-1,0$ on the identity, three-cycles and transpositions. The relations $A^2=3A$, $AB=BA=3B$, $B^2=3A$ show $e_+^2=e_+$, $e_-^2=e_-$ and $e_+e_-=0$. They also give $(A/3)^2=A/3$, so $e_0$ is idempotent and annihilates $e_\pm$. Their sum is $1$. The ideal dimensions are $1,1,4$, either from the matrix blocks or from the independent translates of these elements. They sum to $6$, as they must.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch09-normalinduction}
\idea All double-coset intersections are the same subgroup, so only conjugation of the inducing character matters.
\writeup Since $H$ is normal, $H_s=H$. Linear characters are irreducible, and two such characters have a common constituent exactly when they agree. Mackey's criterion says that no $s\notin H$ may fix $\psi$, which is exactly the stabiliser condition.

For the affine group over $\mathbb F_7$, choose $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$ and $\psi(b)=\zeta^b$ on translations. A nonidentity slope changes $\psi$ by multiplying its argument by that slope, so the stabiliser is $H$. The induced representation is irreducible of degree $6$. At the identity its character is $6$. At a translation by $b\ne0$, the six cosets yield
\[
\sum_{a\in\mathbb F_7^\times}\zeta^{ab}=-1,
\]
because the full geometric sum over $\mathbb F_7$ is zero. At an element with slope different from $1$, no conjugate belongs to the translation subgroup, so the induced character is zero. As a check, its squared norm is $(36+6)/42=1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch09-c3real}
\idea A real polynomial has one real root and one conjugate pair in this factorisation.
\writeup Let $u$ generate $\Z/3$. Every algebra element has the form $a+bu+cu^2$, so the algebra is $\R[X]/(X^3-1)$. The relatively prime factors $X-1$ and $X^2+X+1$ give the isomorphism
\[
f(u)\longmapsto(f(1),f(\zeta))\in\R\times\C.
\]
The second factor is $\C$ as a real algebra, since $\zeta$ is nonreal and satisfies the quadratic. The idempotents are $(1+u+u^2)/3$ and $1-(1+u+u^2)/3$, with ideals of real dimensions $1$ and $2$. After extension to $\C$, the quadratic splits into $(X-\zeta)(X-\bar\zeta)$, so the second ideal becomes $\C\times\C$. These are the two characters $u\mapsto\zeta$ and $u\mapsto\bar\zeta$. They combine into one simple real representation but remain distinct over $\C$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch09-primeclass}
\idea The double transpositions exhibit the normal subgroup; the action on four points gives the non-linear character.
\writeup The classes have sizes $1,3,4,4$: the identity, the three double transpositions, and the two classes of three-cycles. For a double transposition, its centraliser in $A_4$ is the Klein four group, giving class size $12/4=3$. For a three-cycle, its centraliser has order $3$, giving size $4$; the eight three-cycles therefore split into two classes. Proposition~\ref{prop:primepowerclass} proves nonsimplicity. Explicitly, the identity and the three double transpositions form the normal Klein four subgroup $V_4$; closure follows by multiplying any two distinct double transpositions.

The quotient $A_4/V_4\cong\Z/3$ gives three linear characters. Write $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$ and choose the two three-cycle classes to map to the two nonidentity quotient elements. Their rows are
\[
(1,1,1,1),\quad(1,1,\zeta,\zeta^2),
\quad(1,1,\zeta^2,\zeta).
\]
The four-point permutation character is $(4,0,1,1)$, so removing constants gives $(3,-1,0,0)$. Its squared norm is $(9+3)/12=1$, hence it is irreducible. The degree squares $1+1+1+9=12$ show completeness. For the three linear rows, multiplying values by class sizes yields algebraic integers immediately. For the degree-three row the scalars are $1,-1,0,0$. Thus the divisibility in Lemma~\ref{lem:omega} can be checked in every class here.
\end{solution}
