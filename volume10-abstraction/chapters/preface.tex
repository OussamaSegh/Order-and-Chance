\chapter*{Preface}

A map can tell us more than a list of elements. To understand a group, we ask which homomorphisms leave it or enter it. To understand an equation, we put its polynomials in an ideal. To understand a hole, we look at the maps in a chain complex. This book follows those questions until the same language begins to describe all three.

Volume~3 introduced groups, rings, fields and their representations. Here we compare the constructions between them. A free group, a product and a tensor product each solve a problem about maps. Categories let us state the problem; universal properties say what counts as its solution. Yoneda's lemma explains how the maps into an object determine that object up to isomorphism.

The first three chapters take this language from examples to working tools. After categories comes a concrete tensor chapter: Kronecker products, matrix equations and the seven multiplications in Strassen's algorithm. The third chapter builds tensor products of modules without choosing bases, then follows kernels and cokernels through the snake lemma. These constructions will be used throughout the book.

The commutative algebra begins with a question about infinitely many polynomial equations: can finitely many do the same job? Hilbert's basis theorem answers it. His Nullstellensatz connects solutions with ideals. Localisation lets us inspect a ring near a prime, and dimension measures chains of such primes. In Dedekind domains, ideals recover the unique factorisation that individual elements may lose. This is where the series proves the abstract ideal-factorisation theorem that Volume~13 applies to number fields.

Exactness can fail when we apply a construction to a sequence of modules. Homological algebra records the failure with $\operatorname{Tor}$ and $\operatorname{Ext}$. Group cohomology then interprets extensions and the carries in ordinary addition. A carry is a small calculation you can check before meeting the general cocycle equation.

The final three chapters return to symmetry. Wedderburn's theorem breaks a semisimple algebra into matrix pieces. Induction builds representations from a subgroup. Young diagrams organise the irreducibles of the symmetric group, and hook lengths count their dimensions. On compact groups, Haar measure replaces a finite average. Weyl's character formula finally turns moments of a random unitary trace into counts of permutations with a bound on their longest increasing subsequence. A subsequence may skip entries; it need not be a consecutive run.

The prerequisites are the algebra and character theory of Volume~3, topology and integration from Volume~7, Hilbert-space methods from Volume~8, and homology, matrix groups and Weyl integration from Volume~9. A cited result is used only after its proof in the printed series order. Some further results are quoted as pointers; the text marks them so that they are not mistaken for proofs supplied here.

Each chapter ends with graded exercises, useful hints and complete solutions that separate the idea from the writeup. Try an exercise before reading its hint. When a map or an ideal feels abstract, return to its smallest example and write out what each symbol does. The examples are part of the argument.

The next volume is \emph{Randomness: Martingales, Limits and Ergodicity}. Its questions concern limits of random processes, including a deck mixed by random transpositions. The representation theory developed here gives that shuffle its eigenvalues. The passage from those finite algebraic calculations to a limiting probability law is the next step.
