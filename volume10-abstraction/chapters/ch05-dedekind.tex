\chapter{Dedekind Domains}

\begin{center}\itshape
The equality $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ gives two different factorizations of a number. Can ideals restore uniqueness? In the right rings they do. A valuation records the multiplicity at each prime, and a class group records the obstruction to replacing every ideal by one generator.
\end{center}

An ideal is a set of ordinary ring elements, but it can carry arithmetic that no single element carries. Dedekind used this idea in the tenth supplement to the 1871 second edition of Dirichlet's \emph{Vorlesungen über Zahlentheorie}. He had edited his late friend's lectures; Dirichlet had died in 1859. The supplement replaced Kummer's ``ideal numbers'' by ideals and proved unique factorisation of nonzero ideals in rings of integers.

The series proves the general ideal-factorisation theorem here, in Theorem~\ref{thm:dedekind}. We will also prove that integral closure in a finite separable extension produces a Dedekind domain, and that $\sum e_if_i$ equals the extension degree. Volume~13 applies these results to number fields. No result from that later volume is needed to prove them.

Dedekind spent most of his career in Braunschweig. E.~T.~Bell tells a story about a calendar that printed 4 September 1899 as the date of his death. According to Bell, Dedekind wrote to say he had spent that day in good health, talking with his guest Georg Cantor. Dedekind lived until 1916, dying at 84. The story is Bell's account; the mathematics below does not depend on it.

Dedekind and Weber's 1882 work extended ideal theory to algebraic functions. Polynomial functions on a suitable curve then obey the same ideal arithmetic as algebraic integers. Noether's 1927 contribution identified the common hypotheses: Noetherian, integrally closed, and dimension one. Chapters~4 and~5 have supplied these three notions. Corry describes the saying attributed to Noether as mathematical lore: \emph{Es steht alles schon bei Dedekind}, ``it is all already in Dedekind''.

Rings are commutative with $1$. Unless another ring is named, $R$ is a domain and $K$ is its fraction field. A Dedekind domain will be required not to be a field; this convention keeps dimension one explicit.

\section{Fractional ideals}

Over $\Z$, the ideal $2\Z$ has no ideal inverse contained in $\Z$. Its inverse should be $\frac12\Z$. We therefore allow an ideal to have a common denominator.

\begin{definition}\label{def:fractional}
A \emph{fractional ideal} is a nonzero $R$-submodule $I\subseteq K$ with $dI\subseteq R$ for some $0\ne d\in R$. Its product with another fractional ideal $J$ is the submodule generated by all $xy$ with $x\in I$ and $y\in J$. Put
\[
 (R:I)=\{x\in K:xI\subseteq R\}.
\]
The ideal $I$ is \emph{invertible} if $IJ=R$ for some fractional ideal $J$.
\end{definition}

Products here consist of finite sums of pairwise products. Multiplying common denominators shows that $IJ$ is fractional, and the domain hypothesis makes it nonzero. Associativity, commutativity and the identity $IR=I$ follow by distributing finite sums.

Every nonzero ideal of $R$ is fractional. So is $xR$ for $x\in K^\times$, called a \emph{principal fractional ideal}; it has inverse $x^{-1}R$. In $\Z$, every fractional ideal is $(a/b)\Z$ with $a/b\ne0$, since multiplying by a denominator gives an ordinary principal ideal. If $R$ is Noetherian, the same denominator argument shows that every fractional ideal is finitely generated, even when it is not principal.

The module $(R:I)$ is itself fractional. It contains a nonzero common denominator of $I$. To bound its denominators, choose $0\ne a=r/s\in I$, with $r,s\in R$ nonzero. For $x\in(R:I)$, $xa\in R$, so $rx\in sR\subseteq R$. This supplies a common denominator. We can now justify the inverse notation and a local test for invertibility.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:invertible}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $IJ=R$, then $J=(R:I)$ and $I$ is finitely generated. We write $I^{-1}=(R:I)$.
\item The product $IJ$ is invertible if and only if each of $I$ and $J$ is invertible.
\item Over a local domain, a fractional ideal is invertible if and only if it is principal.
\item If $I$ is finitely generated, it is invertible if and only if $I_{\mathfrak m}$ is invertible over $R_{\mathfrak m}$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For (a), $J\subseteq(R:I)$ because $JI=R$. Conversely,
\[
 (R:I)=(R:I)IJ\subseteq RJ=J.
\]
Choose a finite expression $1=\sum_{i=1}^na_ib_i$ with $a_i\in I$, $b_i\in J$. For any $x\in I$, we have $x=\sum_i(xb_i)a_i$ with $xb_i\in R$. Thus the $a_i$ generate $I$.

For (b), if $(IJ)L=R$, then $I(JL)=R$ and $J(IL)=R$. These are valid fractional ideals by the product construction. Conversely, the product of the inverses of $I$ and $J$ inverts $IJ$.

For (c), principal fractional ideals have already been inverted. If $I$ is invertible, use the expression $1=\sum_i a_ib_i$ above. In a local ring not all $a_ib_i$ can lie in its maximal ideal. Choose a unit $u=a_ib_i$. For $x\in I$,
\[
 x=(xb_i)u^{-1}a_i\in a_iR,
\]
so $I=a_iR$.

For (d), an inverse remains an inverse after localisation. For the converse, put $J=(R:I)$. Finite generation gives the identity
\[
 J_{\mathfrak m}=(R_{\mathfrak m}:I_{\mathfrak m})
\]
inside $K$. One inclusion is immediate from the multiplication condition. For the other, write $I=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and suppose $xI_{\mathfrak m}\subseteq R_{\mathfrak m}$. For every $i$, choose $s_i\notin\mathfrak m$ with $s_i xa_i\in R$. The product $s=\prod_i s_i$ makes $sxI\subseteq R$, so $sx\in J$ and $x\in J_{\mathfrak m}$. If all $I_{\mathfrak m}$ are invertible, part (a) identifies their inverses with these colon modules. Hence $(IJ)_{\mathfrak m}=R_{\mathfrak m}$ for every maximal ideal. Applying the local-zero test to $R/IJ$ gives $IJ=R$.
\end{proof}

For a local ring, invertibility has become a one-generator condition. To understand those generators near a prime, we need a ring with one prime parameter.

\section{Discrete valuation rings}

In $\Z_{(p)}$, a nonzero fraction is $p^nu$ with $n\ge0$ and $u$ a unit. Every nonzero ideal is generated by a power of $p$. A discrete valuation ring abstracts this calculation.

\begin{definition}\label{def:dvr}
A \emph{discrete valuation ring}, or DVR, is a principal ideal domain with exactly one nonzero prime ideal.
\end{definition}

Let that prime be $\mathfrak m=(\pi)$. The ring is local, since every maximal ideal is a nonzero prime. The element $\pi$ is called a \emph{uniformiser}. Unique factorisation in a PID, from Volume~3, Theorem~6.14, says that every element of $K^\times$ has a unique form $u\pi^n$ with $u\in R^\times$ and $n\in\Z$. Define $v(u\pi^n)=n$ and set $v(0)=+\infty$. Then
\[
 v(xy)=v(x)+v(y),\qquad
 v(x+y)\ge\min(v(x),v(y)).
\]
For the second inequality, factor out the smaller power of $\pi$; the remaining sum lies in $R$. The convention at zero handles $x+y=0$. Thus $R=\{x\in K:v(x)\ge0\}$, and its nonzero ideals are $(\pi^n)$, $n\ge0$.

Examples are $\Z_{(p)}$, $\C[x]_{(x-a)}$, and $k[[t]]$. Their valuations measure divisibility by $p$, order of vanishing at $a$, and first nonzero power of $t$, respectively. The power-series assertion is worked out in Exercise~\ref{ex:powerseries} and will not be used before its solution.

The next criterion turns normality in dimension one into a uniformiser.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:dvr}
Let $(R,\mathfrak m)$ be a Noetherian local domain whose only nonzero prime is $\mathfrak m\ne0$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $R$ is a DVR;
\item $R$ is integrally closed;
\item $\mathfrak m$ is principal;
\item $\mathfrak m$ is invertible.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, (c) implies (a) in any Noetherian local domain with $\mathfrak m\ne0$, without assuming that it is the only nonzero prime.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{A DVR is normal.} Suppose $x=u\pi^n\in K^\times$ is integral over a DVR. If $n<0$, multiply a monic equation $x^k+\sum_{i<k}c_ix^i=0$ by $\pi^{-nk}$. It gives
\[
 u^k=-\sum_{i<k}c_iu^i\pi^{-n(k-i)}\in\mathfrak m.
\]
Each exponent on the right is positive, but the left side is a unit. This contradiction gives $n\ge0$, so $x\in R$. The element zero is already in $R$. This proves (a)$\Rightarrow$(b).

The equivalence (c)$\Leftrightarrow$(d) is the local part of Lemma~\ref{lem:invertible}.

\emph{A principal maximal ideal gives all ideals.} Suppose only that $R$ is a Noetherian local domain and $\mathfrak m=(\pi)\ne0$. First prove $\bigcap_{n\ge1}(\pi^n)=0$. If $x=\pi^ny_n$ for every $n$, cancellation gives $y_n=\pi y_{n+1}$. Thus $(y_1)\subseteq(y_2)\subseteq\cdots$ stabilises. For some $n$, write $y_{n+1}=cy_n=c\pi y_{n+1}$. Since $1-c\pi$ is a unit, $y_{n+1}=0$, and hence $x=0$.

For $x\ne0$, there is consequently a largest $n$ with $x\in(\pi^n)$. Write $x=\pi^nu$; maximality of $n$ gives $u\notin\mathfrak m$, so $u$ is a unit. For a nonzero ideal, choose the smallest exponent among its nonzero elements. The corresponding $\pi^n$ generates it. The ring is a PID, and the only nonzero prime ideal among these powers is $(\pi)$: if $n>1$, the product $\pi\pi^{n-1}$ belongs to $(\pi^n)$ but neither factor does. This proves (c)$\Rightarrow$(a) and the theorem's extra assertion.

\emph{Normality produces a principal maximal ideal.} Now assume (b) and the stated dimension-one hypothesis. Choose $0\ne x\in\mathfrak m$. The only prime of $R/(x)$ is $\mathfrak m/(x)$. Chapter~5's nilradical calculation therefore says that every element of this ideal is nilpotent. The ideal is finitely generated. If generators have zero powers with exponents $n_1,\ldots,n_k$, a product of $n_1+\cdots+n_k$ generators is zero, by counting repetitions. Thus $\mathfrak m^N\subseteq(x)$ for some $N\ge1$.

Take $N$ minimal, and choose $y\in\mathfrak m^{N-1}\setminus(x)$, allowing $\mathfrak m^0=R$. Put $z=y/x$. Then $z\notin R$ but $z\mathfrak m\subseteq R$. We claim $z\mathfrak m$ cannot lie inside $\mathfrak m$. If it did, express multiplication by $z$ on a finite generating list $m_1,\ldots,m_k$ of $\mathfrak m$ as a matrix $A$ over $R$. Then $(zI-A)m=0$ in $K^k$. The vector $m$ is nonzero because $\mathfrak m\ne0$, so $\det(zI-A)=0$. This is a monic equation over $R$ for $z$. Normality would put $z\in R$, a contradiction.

Therefore the ideal $z\mathfrak m\subseteq R$ contains an element outside $\mathfrak m$, a unit. It equals $R$. Multiplying by $z^{-1}$ gives $\mathfrak m=z^{-1}R$, proving (b)$\Rightarrow$(c).
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
The dimension hypothesis entered when $R/(x)$ was said to have just one prime. Compare $\C[x,y]_{(x,y)}$: it is normal, but its maximal ideal needs two generators. In $\Z_{(p)}$, try the proof with $x=p^2$ and $y=p$. The element $z=1/p$ is outside the ring, yet $z(p)$ is the whole ring. This is the multiplication that identifies the uniformiser.
\end{think}

\section{Dedekind domains}

The local theorem will apply at every nonzero prime if a domain is Noetherian, normal and one-dimensional. These hypotheses are the definition we need.

\begin{definition}\label{def:dedekind}
A \emph{Dedekind domain} is a Noetherian integrally closed domain that is not a field and whose nonzero prime ideals are all maximal.
\end{definition}

Every PID other than a field is Dedekind. It is Noetherian; it is a UFD and hence normal by Chapter~5's denominator argument; and each of its nonzero prime ideals is maximal. Rings of integers will give examples that are not PIDs. Theorem~\ref{thm:intclosure} will prove that claim later in this chapter; for now the theorem below needs only the definition.

\begin{theorem}[Noether, 1927]\label{thm:dedekind}
For a Noetherian domain $R$ that is not a field, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $R$ is Dedekind;
\item $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is a DVR for every nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$;
\item every fractional ideal is invertible;
\item every nonzero proper ideal is a product of prime ideals.
\end{enumerate}
When these conditions hold, the factorisation in (d) is unique up to rearrangement of its factors.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{From normality to the local description, (a)$\Rightarrow$(b).} An ideal of $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is generated by the image of its contraction to $R$: multiply a fraction in the ideal by its denominator to see this. Hence $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is Noetherian. Its primes are the primes of $R$ inside $\mathfrak p$, namely $0$ and $\mathfrak p$. It is also normal. Indeed, if $x\in K$ is integral over $R_{\mathfrak p}$, choose $s\notin\mathfrak p$ clearing all coefficients of a monic equation. Multiplication by the appropriate powers of $s$ gives a monic equation over $R$ for $sx$. Thus $sx\in R$, and $x\in R_{\mathfrak p}$. Theorem~\ref{thm:dvr} now applies.

\emph{The local description recovers normality, (b)$\Rightarrow$(a).} If $0\ne\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak m$ with $\mathfrak m$ maximal, the prime $\mathfrak pR_{\mathfrak m}$ of the DVR $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is nonzero and hence is its maximal ideal. Contracting gives $\mathfrak p=\mathfrak m$. Every nonzero prime is therefore maximal.

Inside $K$ we have $R=\bigcap_{\mathfrak m}R_{\mathfrak m}$. To verify the nontrivial inclusion, take $x$ in this intersection. The ideal $D=\{r\in R:rx\in R\}$ contains, for each $\mathfrak m$, a denominator outside $\mathfrak m$. Thus $D$ lies in no maximal ideal, so $D=R$ and $x\in R$. If $x$ is integral over $R$, it is integral over every $R_{\mathfrak m}$, each a normal DVR. The intersection identity then puts $x\in R$.

\emph{Invertibility and the local description, (b)$\Leftrightarrow$(c).} A fractional ideal over the Noetherian ring $R$ is finitely generated. Under (b), each of its localisations at a maximal ideal is a fractional ideal in a DVR, hence principal and invertible. Lemma~\ref{lem:invertible}(d) gives (c). Conversely, under (c), localise $\mathfrak p\mathfrak p^{-1}=R$ at a nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$. The maximal ideal of $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is invertible, and hence principal by the local lemma. The extra assertion of Theorem~\ref{thm:dvr} makes this local ring a DVR.

\emph{Invertibility gives factorisation, (c)$\Rightarrow$(d).} If the conclusion fails, choose a maximal counterexample ideal $I$ by Noetherianity. It is not prime, since a prime is already a one-factor product. Choose a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ strictly containing $I$. Multiplying inclusions by $\mathfrak m^{-1}$ gives
\[
 I\subseteq I\mathfrak m^{-1}\subseteq R.
\]
The first inclusion uses $R\subseteq\mathfrak m^{-1}$. Equality there would give $\mathfrak m^{-1}=R$ after cancellation by $I^{-1}$, contradicting $\mathfrak m\mathfrak m^{-1}=R$. Thus $I\mathfrak m^{-1}$ is a strictly larger ideal. It is proper, since equality with $R$ would give $I=\mathfrak m$. Maximality of $I$ makes $I\mathfrak m^{-1}$ a product of primes. Multiplication by $\mathfrak m$ then factors $I$, a contradiction.

\emph{Factorisation gives invertibility, (d)$\Rightarrow$(c).} We must earn cancellation rather than assume it. The argument has three parts.

First suppose $P_1\cdots P_r=Q_1\cdots Q_s$, where all ideals are nonzero primes and every $P_i$ is invertible. We prove that the lists agree up to rearrangement. If $r=0$, the product is $R$ and no proper prime can occur on the right, so $s=0$. Otherwise choose a minimal member $P_1$ among the $P_i$. Primeness and $Q_1\cdots Q_s\subseteq P_1$ put some $Q_j$ inside $P_1$. Reverse the product comparison to find some $P_i\subseteq Q_j$. Minimality gives $P_i=P_1$, and hence $Q_j=P_1$. Cancel this invertible factor and continue by induction.

Second, any invertible nonzero prime $P$ is maximal. Suppose $a\notin P$ but $P+aR\ne R$. Factor $P+aR=\prod_iP_i$ and $P+a^2R=\prod_jQ_j$ using (d). All these primes contain $P$. In the domain $\bar R=R/P$ this yields factorizations of the nonzero principal ideals $(\bar a)$ and $(\bar a^2)$. Each $\bar P_i$ is invertible, being a factor of the invertible ideal $(\bar a)$. The cancellation result just proved, applied in $\bar R$, shows that the list of $\bar Q_j$ is the list of $\bar P_i$ repeated twice. The quotient prime correspondence lifts this equality to $R$, giving
\[
 P+a^2R=(P+aR)^2\subseteq P^2+aR.
\]
For $x\in P$, write $x=y+az$ with $y\in P^2$. Since $az\in P$ and $a\notin P$, $z\in P$. Thus $P=P^2+aP=P(P+aR)$. Cancel the invertible ideal $P$, obtaining $R=P+aR$, the desired contradiction.

Third, take any nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$ and choose $0\ne b\in\mathfrak p$. Factor $bR=\prod_iP_i$. Each $P_i$ is invertible as a factor of the principal ideal $bR$, so the second part makes it maximal. Since their product lies in $\mathfrak p$, some $P_i\subseteq\mathfrak p$, and maximality forces $P_i=\mathfrak p$. Thus every nonzero prime is invertible. Every nonzero ideal is now invertible by (d), and every fractional ideal is a principal fractional multiple $d^{-1}(dI)$ of such an ideal. This proves (c).

Finally, when (c) holds, the cancellation argument in the first part applies to every prime factorisation. It proves uniqueness as well as existence.
\end{proof}

There is now an integer exponent at each nonzero prime:
\[
 I=\prod_{\mathfrak p}\mathfrak p^{v_{\mathfrak p}(I)},
 \qquad v_{\mathfrak p}(I)\in\Z,
\]
with only finitely many nonzero exponents. For an ordinary nonzero ideal the exponents are nonnegative; for a fractional ideal, write $I=d^{-1}(dI)$ and subtract the exponents of $dR$. Uniqueness of integral ideal factorisation makes this representation unique. The fractional ideals therefore form the free abelian group $\mathcal I(R)$ on the nonzero primes.

For an element $x\in K^\times$, the exponent of $\mathfrak p$ in $xR$ equals its DVR valuation in $R_{\mathfrak p}$. Every other prime becomes the unit ideal in this localisation. This turns containment into a coordinatewise comparison.

\begin{corollary}[to contain is to divide]\label{cor:contain}
For fractional ideals $I,J$ of a Dedekind domain, the following are equivalent: $J\subseteq I$; $J=IL$ for some nonzero ideal $L\subseteq R$; and $v_{\mathfrak p}(J)\ge v_{\mathfrak p}(I)$ for every nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$. Moreover,
\[
\begin{split}
 v_{\mathfrak p}(I+J)&=\min(v_{\mathfrak p}(I),v_{\mathfrak p}(J)),\\
 v_{\mathfrak p}(I\cap J)&=\max(v_{\mathfrak p}(I),v_{\mathfrak p}(J)).
\end{split}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $J\subseteq I$, multiply by $I^{-1}$ to get the ideal $L=I^{-1}J\subseteq R$, and $J=IL$. Such a product adds nonnegative exponents, proving the comparison. Conversely, if the exponents compare, $I^{-1}J$ is a product of nonnegative prime powers, hence an ordinary ideal $L$. Then $J=IL\subseteq I$.

The sum $I+J$ is the smallest fractional ideal containing both. Its exponents must be at most each of their exponents, and taking the coordinatewise minima gives exactly the smallest such containing ideal. The intersection is the largest ideal contained in both, so its exponents are their coordinatewise maxima. The intersection is nonzero: clear denominators and use that the product of two nonzero integral ideals lies in their intersection. It is therefore a fractional ideal as required.
\end{proof}

For $I=\prod_i\mathfrak p_i^{n_i}$ with distinct primes and $n_i\ge1$, the corollary gives $I=\bigcap_i\mathfrak p_i^{n_i}$. Each prime power is primary. Indeed, its radical is $\mathfrak p_i$, and if $ab$ has valuation at least $n_i$ but $a$ does not, $b$ has positive valuation; then $b^{n_i}$ belongs to the prime power. If $ab=0$, the domain hypothesis handles the zero factors directly. The decomposition is minimal because the intersection of the other prime powers has valuation $0$ at $\mathfrak p_i$, whereas this component has valuation $n_i$.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:assprimes}, the associated primes of $I$ are just the primes in this factorisation. No one contains another, since each is maximal. Thus a nonzero ideal in a Dedekind domain has no embedded associated primes. The zero ideal in a domain is prime; it is best treated separately rather than forced into the nonzero factorisation convention.

Here is the promised ring in which element factorisation fails. We include its normality proof now, so the example does not depend on a later theorem or exercise.

\begin{example}\label{ex:sixtwice}
In $R=\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, put $\mathfrak p=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$, $\mathfrak q=(3,1+\sqrt{-5})$ and $\bar{\mathfrak q}=(3,1-\sqrt{-5})$. This ring is Dedekind, and
\[
\begin{gathered}
 2R=\mathfrak p^2,\qquad 3R=\mathfrak q\bar{\mathfrak q},\\
 (1+\sqrt{-5})R=\mathfrak p\mathfrak q,\qquad
 (1-\sqrt{-5})R=\mathfrak p\bar{\mathfrak q}.
\end{gathered}
\]
None of these three primes is principal. The two element factorizations of $6$ regroup the same prime-ideal factors $\mathfrak p,\mathfrak p,\mathfrak q,\bar{\mathfrak q}$.
\end{example}

Write $w=\sqrt{-5}$. First verify the ring hypotheses. The ring is a finite free $\Z$-module on $1,w$, so it is Noetherian by Chapter~4, Proposition~\ref{prop:noethmodules}; every ideal is a submodule. It is integral over $\Z$, so Chapter~5's dimension theorem gives dimension $1$. It is not a field, since the norm $N(a+bw)=a^2+5b^2$ shows that $2$ has no inverse in it.

For normality, let $z=a+bw\in\Q(w)$ be integral over $R$. Transitivity makes it integral over $\Z$. Its conjugate is also integral, by conjugating a monic equation over $\Z$. Consequently $2a=z+\bar z$ and $a^2+5b^2=z\bar z$ are rational numbers integral over $\Z$, and hence integers, since $\Z$ is normal. Put $A=2a$ and $B=2b$. Then $A\in\Z$ and $A^2+5B^2=4N(z)\in4\Z$. In particular, $5B^2\in\Z$. If $B=c/d$ is reduced with $d>0$, then $d^2\mid5$, so $d=1$. Thus $B\in\Z$. Reducing $A^2+5B^2$ modulo $4$ forces both $A$ and $B$ even. Hence $a,b\in\Z$ and $z\in R$. This proves normality without invoking the later trace lemma.

The three quotient maps send $w$ to $1$ modulo $2$, to $-1$ modulo $3$, and to $1$ modulo $3$, respectively. Their kernels are exactly the displayed ideals, so their quotients are $\mathbb F_2,\mathbb F_3,\mathbb F_3$ and the ideals are prime. Direct multiplication gives all four identities. For instance, $\mathfrak p^2$ is generated by $4$, $2+2w$ and $-4+2w$. These are multiples of $2$, and subtracting the last from the second, then subtracting $4$, gives $2$. Similarly, $\mathfrak q\bar{\mathfrak q}$ contains $9$ and $6$, so contains $3$, and all its generators are multiples of $3$. The product $\mathfrak p\mathfrak q$ has all generators divisible by $1+w$ and contains $3(1+w)-2(1+w)=1+w$. Conjugation gives the last identity, since $\bar{\mathfrak p}=\mathfrak p$.

If $\mathfrak p=gR$, then $N(g)$ divides both $4$ and $6$. It is not $1$, since $\mathfrak p$ is proper, and cannot be $2$, since $a^2+5b^2=2$ has no integral solution. If $\mathfrak q$ or $\bar{\mathfrak q}$ were principal, the analogous divisibilities by $9$ and $6$ would force norm $3$, again impossible. This also proves their nonprincipality before we use their classes.

\section{The class group}

The group of fractional ideals contains all principal fractional ideals as a subgroup. Quotienting by it records which ideals still fail to have one generator.

\begin{definition}\label{def:classgroup}
For a Dedekind domain $R$, its \emph{class group} is $\operatorname{Cl}(R)=\mathcal I(R)/\mathcal P(R)$, where $\mathcal P(R)$ is the subgroup of principal fractional ideals. The order of this group, if finite, is the \emph{class number}.
\end{definition}

Two fractional ideals have the same class exactly when one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other. The exact sequence below distinguishes a unit element, a principal ideal and a trivial ideal class.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:classseq}
There is an exact sequence of abelian groups
\[
1\longrightarrow R^\times\longrightarrow K^\times
 \xrightarrow{\,x\mapsto xR\,}\mathcal I(R)
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(R)\longrightarrow1.
\]
For a Dedekind domain, the following are equivalent: $R$ is a PID; its class group is trivial; and $R$ is a UFD.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The inclusion of units is injective. The equality $xR=R$ holds exactly when both $x$ and $x^{-1}$ belong to $R$, which means $x\in R^\times$. The image of $K^\times$ is $\mathcal P(R)$, precisely the kernel of the quotient map, and that quotient map is surjective by definition. This proves exactness.

Trivial class group means that every nonzero fractional ideal, hence every nonzero ideal, is principal. The zero ideal is principal as well, so $R$ is a PID. Conversely, over a PID, multiply a fractional ideal by a common denominator; the resulting ideal is principal, making the original fractional ideal principal too. A PID is a UFD by Volume~3, Theorem~6.14.

For the remaining implication, suppose $R$ is also a UFD. In a nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$, choose a nonzero element and factor it into irreducibles. Primeness puts one irreducible $\pi$ in $\mathfrak p$. In a UFD the ideal $(\pi)$ is prime. It is nonzero, so Dedekind's dimension-one condition makes it maximal. Since $(\pi)\subseteq\mathfrak p$, equality follows. Every prime ideal is now principal, and unique ideal factorisation makes every nonzero ideal principal.
\end{proof}

In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, the class of $\mathfrak p$ is nontrivial and its square is trivial, because $\mathfrak p^2=2R$. We have therefore proved the presence of an element of order $2$. The stronger claim that this is the whole class group is a preview: Volume~13 proves it using geometry of numbers, and proves finiteness for every ring of integers. For general Dedekind domains there is no finiteness theorem of this kind. Claborn's 1966 result, quoted here rather than proved, says every abelian group can occur as a class group.

Even when an ideal is not principal, one extra generator always suffices. More strongly, we can prescribe the first nonzero generator.

\begin{proposition}[one and a half generators]\label{prop:twogen}
Let $I$ be a nonzero ideal in a Dedekind domain, and choose $0\ne a\in I$. There is $b\in I$ with $I=(a,b)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $a$ is a unit, $I=R$ and $b=0$ works. Otherwise list the finitely many prime divisors $\mathfrak p_1,\ldots,\mathfrak p_r$ of $aR$. Any prime dividing $I$ is on this list, because $aR\subseteq I$ reverses the valuation comparison. Write $n_i=v_{\mathfrak p_i}(I)$, allowing $n_i=0$.

Choose $b_i\in\mathfrak p_i^{n_i}\setminus\mathfrak p_i^{n_i+1}$, possible because distinct prime powers are distinct ideals. The ideals $\mathfrak p_i^{n_i+1}$ are pairwise coprime. Indeed, a maximal ideal containing one contains its radical $\mathfrak p_i$, hence equals it, so it cannot contain another. The ring Chinese remainder theorem, Volume~3, Theorem~6.9, gives $b\in R$ with $b\equiv b_i\pmod{\mathfrak p_i^{n_i+1}}$ for every $i$. Thus $v_{\mathfrak p_i}(bR)=n_i$. At all primes not on the list, $b$ has nonnegative valuation and $I$ has valuation zero. Therefore $b\in I$.

At each listed prime the minimum of the valuations of $a$ and $b$ is $n_i$, since $a\in I$. At every other prime the valuation of $a$ is zero. Corollary~\ref{cor:contain} consequently gives $v_{\mathfrak p}(aR+bR)=v_{\mathfrak p}(I)$ everywhere. Unique factorisation yields $(a,b)=I$.
\end{proof}

\section{Integral closure in a finite extension}

We can now produce new Dedekind domains from old ones. Extend the fraction field, and keep the elements satisfying monic equations over the old ring. A trace pairing bounds their denominators. First we need the trace calculation itself.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace}
Let $L/K$ be finite of degree $n$, and let $y\in L$ have minimal polynomial $f\in K[x]$ of degree $d$. The characteristic polynomial of multiplication $m_y\colon z\mapsto yz$ on $L$ is $f^{n/d}$. Its trace is denoted $\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}(y)$. If $R$ is integrally closed with fraction field $K$ and $y$ is integral over $R$, this characteristic polynomial belongs to $R[x]$ and $\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}(y)\in R$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose a basis $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_{n/d}$ of $L$ over $K(y)$; the tower law, Volume~3, Theorem~8.2, gives its length. For each $j$, the vectors $\ell_j,y\ell_j,\ldots,y^{d-1}\ell_j$ form one block in a $K$-basis of $L$. Multiplication by $y$ has the companion matrix of $f$ on each block. Its characteristic polynomial is therefore the product of $n/d$ copies of $f$.

If $y$ is integral, choose a monic $g\in R[x]$ vanishing at $y$. Division by the minimal polynomial gives $f\mid g$ in $K[x]$. In a splitting field, every root of $f$, counted with multiplicity, is a root of a monic polynomial over $R$, and so is integral. The coefficients of $f$ are signed elementary symmetric expressions in these roots. Closure of integral elements under sums and products makes them integral over $R$. They lie in $K$, so normality puts them in $R$. Thus $f^{n/d}\in R[x]$, and its coefficient of $x^{n-1}$, with its sign changed, is the trace in $R$. No separability assumption was needed for this lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:intclosure}
Let $R$ be Dedekind with fraction field $K$, and let $L/K$ be finite separable of degree $n$. Write $S$ for the integral closure of $R$ in $L$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item There is $\theta\in S$ with $L=K(\theta)$. The element
\[
 d=\det\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}(\theta^{i+j})\bigr)_{0\le i,j<n}
\]
is nonzero, belongs to $R$, and satisfies $dS\subseteq R[\theta]$.
\item The ring $S$ is a finitely generated $R$-module and is a Dedekind domain with fraction field $L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Choose a primitive integral element.} The domain $R$ is infinite, since a finite domain is a field. Hence $K$ is infinite. We give the primitive-element argument over this field; Volume~3, Theorem~8.20 states it for subfields of $\C$, and that statement alone would not cover positive characteristic.

For two separable elements $\alpha,\beta$, work in a common splitting field of their minimal polynomials. List their distinct roots as $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_j$, including $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Choose $c\in K\setminus\{0\}$ avoiding the finitely many values for which
\[
 \alpha_i+c\beta_j=\alpha+c\beta\quad\text{with }\beta_j\ne\beta.
\]
Each equation excludes at most one value, and $K$ is infinite. Put $\gamma=\alpha+c\beta$. Over $K(\gamma)$ the polynomials $m_\beta(T)$ and $m_\alpha(\gamma-cT)$ have $\beta$ as their only common root in the splitting field. Since $m_\beta$ has distinct roots, their monic greatest common divisor is $T-\beta$. Its coefficients belong to $K(\gamma)$ by the Euclidean algorithm, so $\beta\in K(\gamma)$ and then $\alpha=\gamma-c\beta\in K(\gamma)$. Repeating for a finite generating list of $L/K$ gives $L=K(\theta)$.

Clear denominators in a monic equation for this $\theta$: choose $0\ne r\in R$ with $r$ times every coefficient in $R$. Substituting $r\theta$ gives a monic equation whose coefficients are in $R$. Replacing $\theta$ by $r\theta$ leaves $K(\theta)$ unchanged and puts $\theta$ in $S$.

\emph{The trace matrix is nonsingular.} Let $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n$ be the distinct conjugates of $\theta$. Over a splitting field, multiplication by $\theta$ has these eigenvalues, and is diagonalizable because they are distinct. Multiplication by $\theta^m$ is the $m$th power of that map, so
$\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^m)=\sum_k\theta_k^m$. With $V=(\theta_k^i)_{1\le k\le n,\,0\le i<n}$, the trace matrix is $V^{\mathsf T}V$. The Vandermonde determinant gives
\[
 d=\det(V)^2=\prod_{k<\ell}(\theta_\ell-\theta_k)^2\ne0.
\]
This determinant formula was proved in Volume~1, Proposition~7.4. Its entries are in $R$ by the trace lemma, so $d\in R$.

\emph{Bound every denominator.} For $s\in S$, expand $s=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_j\theta^j$ over $K$. Each $s\theta^i$ is integral, so
\[
 \sum_j c_j\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^{i+j})
   =\operatorname{Tr}(s\theta^i)\in R
 \quad(0\le i<n).
\]
This linear system has determinant $d\ne0$. Cramer's rule puts every $dc_j$ in $R$, whence $ds\in R[\theta]$. This proves (a).

\emph{Verify the ring properties.} The inclusion
\[
 S\subseteq d^{-1}R[\theta]
    =\bigoplus_{j=0}^{n-1}R(d^{-1}\theta^j)
\]
places $S$ in a finite $R$-module. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $S$ is a finite $R$-module too. Its ideals are $R$-submodules, so every ascending ideal chain stabilises: $S$ is Noetherian. Every element of $L$ has a nonzero multiple by an element of $R$ integral over $R$, by the same clearing-denominators argument used above. Thus $L$ is the fraction field of $S$.

If an element of $L$ is integral over $S$, transitivity makes it integral over $R$, so it belongs to $S$ by its definition. Hence $S$ is normal. Finally, it is integral over $R$, and dimension is preserved. Thus $\dim S=1$; it is not a field and every nonzero prime is maximal. These are exactly the Dedekind conditions, proving (b).
\end{proof}

With $R=\Z$, this proves that the integral closure $\mathcal O_L$ in a number field is Dedekind. Characteristic zero supplies separability. With $R=\C[x]$, it constructs the normal function rings of finite algebraic covers of the line. For a squarefree cubic, we will verify the required integral closure directly in the final section.

There is an advanced extension worth separating from this proof. The Krull--Akizuki theorem, quoted here, still makes the integral closure Dedekind in a finite inseparable extension of its fraction field; finite generation as an $R$-module can fail. We neither prove nor use that extension. Separability in the theorem above is what made the trace determinant nonzero.

\section{How primes split}

Keep $R,K,L,S$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:intclosure}, with $[L:K]=n$. Fix a nonzero prime $\mathfrak p$ of $R$. Lying over ensures $\mathfrak pS$ is proper, and unique ideal factorisation gives
\[
 \mathfrak pS=\mathfrak Q_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak Q_g^{e_g}.
\]
These are exactly the primes of $S$ above $\mathfrak p$. Indeed, a prime containing $\mathfrak pS$ contracts to a prime containing the maximal ideal $\mathfrak p$, hence to $\mathfrak p$ itself; the converse is immediate. Its exponent $e_i\ge1$ is the \emph{ramification index}. The \emph{residue degree} is
\[
 f_i=[S/\mathfrak Q_i:R/\mathfrak p].
\]
It is finite because $S$ is finite as an $R$-module. These two numbers count different features: how many layers lie above a point, and how large the field on each layer is.

\begin{theorem}[the fundamental identity]\label{thm:fundid}
With these hypotheses and notation, $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^g e_i f_i=n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We compute the dimension of $S/\mathfrak pS$ over the field $k=R/\mathfrak p$ in two ways.

\emph{Count the prime-power layers.} Distinct maximal ideals have coprime powers, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives
\[
 S/\mathfrak pS\cong\prod_i S/\mathfrak Q_i^{e_i}.
\]
Fix $\mathfrak Q=\mathfrak Q_i$. Each layer $\mathfrak Q^j/\mathfrak Q^{j+1}$ for $0\le j<e_i$ is a vector space over $S/\mathfrak Q$, since $\mathfrak Q$ annihilates it. It has dimension one. To see this, choose $x\in\mathfrak Q^j\setminus\mathfrak Q^{j+1}$. There is no ideal strictly between these consecutive powers: its valuation at $\mathfrak Q$ would have to be an integer strictly between $j$ and $j+1$, and all other valuations must be zero. Thus $\mathfrak Q^{j+1}+xS=\mathfrak Q^j$, so the nonzero class of $x$ generates the layer. A nonzero vector generated by one element over a field gives dimension one.

The filtration of $S/\mathfrak Q^{e_i}$ by these $e_i$ layers therefore gives dimension $e_if_i$ over $k$. Dimensions add along a finite filtration, by extending bases across a short exact sequence. Summing over the product gives dimension $\sum_i e_if_i$.

\emph{Count a local basis.} Localise at $\mathfrak p$. The ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is a DVR. The module $S_{\mathfrak p}=(R\setminus\mathfrak p)^{-1}S$ is its integral closure in $L$: one direction follows by localising equations; for the other, clear their denominators outside $\mathfrak p$ to make a multiple integral over $R$. Theorem~\ref{thm:intclosure} makes this a finite torsion-free module over the PID $R_{\mathfrak p}$. By Volume~3, Theorem~7.6, it is free.

A module basis is linearly independent over $K$, because denominators in any $K$-linear relation can be cleared. It spans $L$ over $K$, because every element of $L$ has a nonzero $R$-multiple in $S$. Its length is therefore $n$. Reducing the basis modulo the maximal ideal gives
\[
 S_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak pS_{\mathfrak p}\cong k^n.
\]
Finally, $S/\mathfrak pS\to S_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak pS_{\mathfrak p}$ is an isomorphism. Every $t\notin\mathfrak p$ is already invertible on $S/\mathfrak pS$: choose $u\in R$ with $ut\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak p}$, and multiplication by $u$ gives its inverse. Localising therefore changes nothing. The same dimension is $n$, proving the identity.
\end{proof}

When $R=\Z$, this is the prime-degree count used in Volume~13, Chapter~4. In a Galois extension the primes above $\mathfrak p$ are permuted transitively, so their ramification indices and residue degrees are constant, giving $efg=n$. This transitivity statement is a preview, not a premise: Volume~13 proves it for number fields by an argument that also works over a Dedekind base. We have proved the sum formula without assuming transitivity.

The cubic in Figure~\ref{fig:cubicclass} has a function ring with the same factorisation properties as $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$. Before using that claim, we prove the ring hypotheses locally. Let $g\in\C[x]$ be a squarefree cubic, and set
\[
 A=\C[x,y]/(y^2-g(x)).
\]
Every element has a unique form $a(x)+b(x)y$ by division by the monic polynomial in $y$. The cubic $g$ is not a square in $\C(x)$: the order at any one of its simple roots is odd. Hence $y^2-g$ is irreducible over $\C(x)$ and $A$ is a domain with fraction field $\C(x)(y)$. It is Noetherian and integral over $\C[x]$, so it has dimension $1$.

To prove normality, suppose $\alpha=a+by$, with $a,b\in\C(x)$, is integral over $A$. Transitivity makes it integral over $\C[x]$. Conjugation $y\mapsto-y$ preserves this integrality, so both $2a=\alpha+\bar\alpha$ and $a^2-b^2g=\alpha\bar\alpha$ are integral over $\C[x]$. They belong to its fraction field, and this PID is normal. Thus $a\in\C[x]$ and $b^2g\in\C[x]$. If a prime polynomial divided the reduced denominator of $b$, its square would occur in the denominator of $b^2$; the squarefree $g$ could cancel at most one copy. This is impossible. Hence $b\in\C[x]$ and $\alpha\in A$. The ring is not a field, since evaluating at any complex point gives a proper maximal ideal. Thus $A$ is Dedekind. This proof also identifies it as the integral closure of $\C[x]$ in its fraction field.

The weak Nullstellensatz gives its maximal ideals $\mathfrak m_P=(x-a,y-b)$, one for each point $P=(a,b)$ with $b^2=g(a)$. For a nonvertical line $\ell=y-\lambda x-\mu$, the quotient is
\[
 A/\ell A\cong\C[x]/((\lambda x+\mu)^2-g(x)).
\]
The polynomial on the right has degree three. If its three roots are distinct, the quotient is a product of three copies of $\C$. Its ideal factorisation is therefore $\ell A=\mathfrak m_P\mathfrak m_Q\mathfrak m_R$. Equivalently, the quotient dimension is three and each of its three prime factors must occur once. For $g=x^3-x+1$ and $\ell=y+2x-1$, the roots are $0,1,3$, which verifies the three points drawn in the original figure.

For a vertical line $x=a$, the quotient is $\C[y]/(y^2-g(a))$. If $g(a)\ne0$, it has two distinct roots $b,-b$, yielding $(x-a)=\mathfrak m_P\mathfrak m_{P'}$. If $g(a)=0$, the two points coincide and the quotient is $\C[y]/(y^2)$. There is one prime factor, with exponent two, so the same formula reads $(x-a)=\mathfrak m_P^2$. This repeated-root case matters: it gives the square of a nonprincipal ideal in Exercise~\ref{ex:cubicnotprincipal}.

Passing to classes gives $[\mathfrak m_P][\mathfrak m_Q][\mathfrak m_R]=1$ for a line meeting three distinct points, and $[\mathfrak m_P][\mathfrak m_{P'}]=1$ for a vertical line. These are the relations behind chord-and-tangent addition on an elliptic curve. Volume~13, Chapter~15 proves that group law; here it is a preview of the geometric meaning of the factorisations. Sending $P$ to $[\mathfrak m_P]$ and the point at infinity $O$ to $1$ respects the displayed relations. We have not proved from them that this map is a bijection, and do not use such a claim. For the curve $y^2=x^3-x$, the ideal $(x,y)$ is nonprincipal but its square is $(x)$; the exercise gives an elementary norm obstruction to a generator.

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

At a single nonzero prime, a Dedekind domain is a DVR: one uniformiser counts every vanishing order. Putting these local counts together gives unique factorisation of ideals. The inverse of an ideal allows cancellation, while the class group measures the remaining obstruction to one generator. Two generators still suffice, even when the first nonzero one has already been chosen.

The trace pairing then constructs new examples in finite separable extensions. Its nonzero determinant gives one common denominator for every integral element. That finite-module bound supplies Noetherianity, and the same local basis counts a split prime: $\sum e_if_i=[L:K]$. These are the results Volume~13 will need for rings of integers. The squarefree cubic calculation shows why the same statements belong in algebraic geometry.

Exact sequences have stayed in the background while we studied the rings. Chapter~3 left two defects to measure: tensoring may lose injectivity, and $\operatorname{Hom}$ may lose surjectivity. We can now return to them. Chapter~7 constructs $\operatorname{Tor}$ and $\operatorname{Ext}$; the extension $0\to\Z/2\to\Z/4\to\Z/2\to0$ is one of the small examples those groups will classify.

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:zsqrt5}
\idea An ideal equality needs both containments. Norm divisibility then obstructs a single generator.
\writeup Put $w=\sqrt{-5}$. The square of $\mathfrak p$ has generators $4$, $2+2w$, $-4+2w$, all in $2R$. Their combinations give $6=(2+2w)-(-4+2w)$ and $2=6-4$, so $\mathfrak p^2=2R$.

The product $\mathfrak q\bar{\mathfrak q}$ has generators $9$, $3(1-w)$, $3(1+w)$ and $6$. They belong to $3R$, and $9-6=3$ belongs to their span. Thus $\mathfrak q\bar{\mathfrak q}=3R$.

The generators of $\mathfrak p\mathfrak q$ are $6$, $2(1+w)$, $3(1+w)$ and $(1+w)^2$. Each is a multiple of $1+w$, including $6=(1+w)(1-w)$. Their difference $3(1+w)-2(1+w)=1+w$ proves $\mathfrak p\mathfrak q=(1+w)R$. Conjugation preserves $\mathfrak p$, since $1-w=2-(1+w)$, and gives $\mathfrak p\bar{\mathfrak q}=(1-w)R$.

If $\mathfrak p=gR$, the element $g\in R$ divides $2$ and $1+w$. Multiplicativity of $N$ makes the positive integer $N(g)$ divide both $4$ and $6$. Norm $1$ would make $g$ a unit and $\mathfrak p=R$, contrary to the quotient $R/\mathfrak p\cong\mathbb F_2$. Norm $2$ would require $a^2+5b^2=2$, which has no integer solution. No generator exists.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:powerseries}
\idea The first nonzero coefficient determines a power of $t$; everything following that power is a unit.
\writeup Let $f=\sum_{i\ge0}a_it^i$ with $a_0\ne0$. Define an inverse $g=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nt^n$ recursively by
\[
 b_0=a_0^{-1},\qquad b_n=-a_0^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^na_i b_{n-i}.
\]
The coefficient equations then give $fg=1$. All sums defining individual coefficients are finite, so no convergence is involved. A nonzero series has a least nonzero coefficient, say at degree $n$, and is $t^n$ times a unit. The product of two nonzero series has nonzero leading coefficient, making the ring a domain.

For any nonzero ideal, take the least order among its nonzero series. Removing the unit factor shows the ideal contains $t^n$, and every other element is a multiple of it. Thus every ideal is principal. The only nonzero prime is $(t)$, since a higher power fails the prime test. The fraction field is $k((t))$, the formal Laurent series with finitely many negative powers: each fraction is a power of $t$ times a unit, and each Laurent series has this form. Its valuation is the least exponent with nonzero coefficient. Nonzero constants have valuation zero; set $v(0)=+\infty$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:semilocal}
\idea Use the finite list of primes to build an element with valuation one at a chosen prime and zero at every other one.
\writeup List the nonzero primes as $\mathfrak p_1,\ldots,\mathfrak p_r$. There is at least one, because a Dedekind domain is not a field. Choose $\pi_0\in\mathfrak p_1\setminus\mathfrak p_1^2$. The ideals $\mathfrak p_1^2,\mathfrak p_2,\ldots,\mathfrak p_r$ are pairwise coprime. The Chinese remainder theorem gives $\pi$ congruent to $\pi_0$ modulo $\mathfrak p_1^2$ and to $1$ modulo every other prime on the list. Thus its valuation is one at $\mathfrak p_1$ and zero at all other nonzero primes. Unique factorisation gives $\pi R=\mathfrak p_1$. The same construction makes every nonzero prime principal. Every nonzero proper ideal is a product of these primes and is therefore principal; the zero and unit ideals are principal too.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:notdedekind}
\idea Dimension one and normality are separate hypotheses, and the two examples lose different ones.
\writeup Hilbert's basis theorem makes $\C[x,y]$ Noetherian, and it is a domain. Its prime chain $0\subsetneq(x)\subsetneq(x,y)$ has length two, so it is not Dedekind. Let $\mathfrak m=(x,y)$. If it were invertible, it would become principal in $\C[x,y]_{\mathfrak m}$. But the quotient of its maximal ideal by its square has basis the classes of $x$ and $y$ over $\C$: linear terms survive and all terms of degree at least two vanish. Localisation does not change this quotient, since each denominator has a nonzero constant term and acts by that scalar. Corollary~\ref{cor:mingens} says two generators are necessary. Thus this nonzero prime is not invertible.

The ring $R=\Z[\sqrt{-3}]$ is a finite free $\Z$-module, hence Noetherian, and is a domain. The missing element $\omega=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ belongs to its fraction field and satisfies $\omega^2-\omega+1=0$, so normality fails. Write $w=\sqrt{-3}$ and $\mathfrak p=(2,1+w)$. Its quotient is $\mathbb F_2$, so it is a nonzero maximal prime. Its square is generated by $4$, $2(1+w)$ and $2(-1+w)$. The last generator is $2(1+w)-4$, giving $\mathfrak p^2=2\mathfrak p$. Invertibility would permit cancellation and imply $\mathfrak p=2R$. This is false, since $1+w\notin2R$. Hence $\mathfrak p$ is not invertible.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:circle}
\idea The norm to $\R[x]$ has even degree, while a generator of the point ideal would need norm of degree one.
\writeup Division by the monic relation gives a unique expression $a+by$ with $a,b\in\R[x]$. Conjugation $y\mapsto-y$ makes
$N(a+by)=a^2+b^2(x^2-1)$ multiplicative. The square of $\mathfrak m$ is generated by $(x-1)^2$, $(x-1)y$ and $y^2=-(x-1)(x+1)$, so it lies in $(x-1)$. Conversely,
\[
 x-1=-\tfrac12\bigl((x-1)^2+y^2\bigr)\in\mathfrak m^2,
\]
proving equality.

If $\mathfrak m=(g)$, then $N(g)$ divides $(x-1)^2=N(x-1)$ and $x^2-1=N(y)$. It therefore divides a constant multiple of $x-1$. A nonzero constant norm would make $g$ a unit, because $\bar g/N(g)$ would be its inverse. This is impossible for a generator of the proper ideal with quotient $\R$. Thus $N(g)$ would have degree one.

For any nonzero $a+by$, the possible leading degrees of its norm are $2\deg a$ and $2\deg b+2$. If they agree, their real leading coefficients are positive squares and cannot cancel; if they differ, the larger survives. The degree is therefore even, contradicting degree one. This argument is where the real coefficient field matters.

After extending scalars to $\C$, put $u=x+iy$. Its inverse is $x-iy$, and
\[
 x=(u+u^{-1})/2,\qquad y=(u-u^{-1})/(2i).
\]
These formulas give mutually inverse ring maps with $\C[u,u^{-1}]$. It is the localisation of the PID $\C[u]$ at powers of $u$, so every ideal is the extension of its principal contraction and is principal. In particular, the extended point ideal becomes principal. The discussion does not require the quoted computation of the whole real class group.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:cubicnotprincipal}
\idea Norm degrees rule out a generator; squarefree denominators establish normality.
\writeup Put $h=x^3-x$. For (a), division by the monic polynomial $Y^2-h$ gives the unique remainder $a+bY$ over $\C[x]$. Conjugation $y\mapsto-y$ is a ring automorphism, so $N(\alpha)=\alpha\bar\alpha$ is multiplicative. If $b\ne0$, the degrees $2\deg a$ and $2\deg b+3$ have different parity; they cannot cancel. Interpreting the absent term as having degree $-\infty$, we get
\[
 \deg N(a+by)=\max(2\deg a,2\deg b+3)
\]
for a nonzero element. A unit must have constant nonzero norm, forcing $b=0$ and $a$ constant. Every such constant is indeed a unit.

For (b), $\mathfrak m^2=(x^2,xy,y^2)\subseteq(x)$. Also $x=x^3-y^2\in\mathfrak m^2$, because $x^3=x\cdot x^2$ and $y^2$ are in that ideal. Thus $\mathfrak m^2=(x)$. If $\mathfrak m=(g)$, the norm $N(g)$ divides both $x^2$ and $-h=-x(x-1)(x+1)$, so it divides $x$. It cannot be constant, for that would make $g$ a unit while $A/\mathfrak m\cong\C$. It would have degree one, excluded by the degree formula in (a).

For (c), the simple roots of $h$ ensure that it is not a square in $\C(x)$, so the fraction field is the quadratic field $\C(x)(y)$. Let $\alpha=a+by$ in that field be integral over $A$. Since $A$ is integral over $\C[x]$, transitivity makes $\alpha$ integral over $\C[x]$. Its conjugate is integral too. Thus $2a$ and $a^2-b^2h$ are integral elements of $\C(x)$, and normality of the PID $\C[x]$ puts them in $\C[x]$. Hence $a\in\C[x]$ and $b^2h\in\C[x]$. A prime factor in a reduced denominator of $b$ would occur at least twice in the denominator of $b^2$, while $h$ contains it at most once. No such factor is possible, so $b\in\C[x]$ and $\alpha\in A$.

The squarefree hypothesis is doing a specific job. For $y^2=x^3$, the fraction $y/x$ satisfies $(y/x)^2=x$ but is not in the function ring. Its denominator is possible because the cubic has a repeated root. This is an obstruction to applying the Dedekind argument to that singular curve.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch06-arithmetic}
\idea Read each operation one valuation at a time, remembering that a larger exponent means a smaller ideal.
\writeup At $\mathfrak p$, the exponents of $I,J$ are $2,1$; at $\mathfrak q$ they are $1,3$. Every other exponent is zero. Hence
\[
 I+J=\mathfrak p\mathfrak q,\quad
 I\cap J=\mathfrak p^2\mathfrak q^3,\quad
 IJ=\mathfrak p^3\mathfrak q^4.
\]
Neither ideal contains the other, since the inequalities go in opposite directions at the two primes. Multiplying $xJ\subseteq I$ by $J^{-1}$ shows $xR\subseteq IJ^{-1}$, equivalent to $x\in IJ^{-1}$. Conversely membership in $IJ^{-1}$ gives the required containment. Thus $(I:J)=IJ^{-1}=\mathfrak p\mathfrak q^{-2}$.

Over $\Z$, $I=(12)$ and $J=(54)$. Their sum is $(6)$, their intersection $(108)$, their product $(648)$ and their fractional colon $(2/9)\Z$. For example, $x\cdot54\in12\Z$ exactly when $x\in(2/9)\Z$. Finally, $\mathfrak p^2$ and $\mathfrak q$ are coprime, and their product is their intersection, so
$R/I\cong R/\mathfrak p^2\times R/\mathfrak q$ by the Chinese remainder theorem. The numerical case gives $\Z/12\cong\Z/4\times\Z/3$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch06-projective}
\idea The finite expression for $1$ gives a surjection onto $I$ together with an explicit section.
\writeup Define $f\colon R^n\to I$ by $f(r_1,\ldots,r_n)=\sum_i r_i a_i$, and $g\colon I\to R^n$ by $g(x)=(b_1x,\ldots,b_nx)$. Every coordinate of $g(x)$ lies in $R$ because $b_i\in I^{-1}$. Both maps are $R$-linear, and $f(g(x))=x\sum_i a_ib_i=x$. Hence $f$ is onto, $g$ is a section, and the splitting lemma gives $R^n\cong\ker f\oplus I$. Chapter~3's projective criterion makes $I$ projective and finitely generated.

A nonzero principal fractional ideal $aR$ is free of rank one by multiplication by $a$. Conversely, if $I$ is free, tensor with $K$. The inclusion $I\subseteq K$ becomes an isomorphism after localisation at nonzero elements: for $0\ne a\in I$, every element of $K$ is a $K$-multiple of $a$. Thus $K\otimes_R I$ has dimension one, forcing a free basis of $I$ to have one element. That element generates $I$ as a fractional ideal, so it is principal. After localisation at any maximal ideal, invertibility remains true, and the local part of Lemma~\ref{lem:invertible} makes the ideal principal. Its nonzero generator gives a free rank-one module there. A nonprincipal invertible ideal therefore gives a projective module that is locally free but not globally free.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch06-gaussian}
\idea Factor the reduced quadratic and keep repeated factors as multiplicities.
\writeup The Gaussian integers are a Euclidean domain, hence a PID and Dedekind; their fraction field is quadratic over $\Q$. Since $(1+i)^2=2i$ and $i$ is a unit, $2S=(1+i)^2S$. Its prime quotient is $\mathbb F_2$, so $e=2$ and $f=1$.

Modulo $3$, the polynomial $T^2+1$ has no root: the squares in $\mathbb F_3$ are $0,1$. It is irreducible, so $S/3S=\mathbb F_3[T]/(T^2+1)$ is the field with nine elements. Thus $3S$ is prime, with $e=1$ and $f=2$.

Finally, $5=(2+i)(2-i)$. The quotient by $(2+i)$ sends $i$ to $-2=3$ modulo $5$, while the quotient by $(2-i)$ sends $i$ to $2$. These are distinct prime ideals with quotients $\mathbb F_5$. Each has $e=f=1$. The three sums are respectively $2\cdot1$, $1\cdot2$ and $1\cdot1+1\cdot1$, all equal to $2$.

The quotient modulo $2$ is $\mathbb F_2[T]/((T+1)^2)$. It has a single prime, the ideal of $T+1$, but a nonzero nilpotent class $T+1$. This extra layer records multiplicity. It cannot be replaced by two distinct primes, which would instead give a product of two fields with no nonzero nilpotents.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v10new-ch06-cusp}
\idea A nonnormal one-dimensional ring can have a prime whose product behaves as if it could be cancelled, although cancellation is unavailable.
\writeup The ring is generated by $t^2,t^3$ over $K$, so it is Noetherian. It is a domain as a subring of $K[t]$. It is integral over the polynomial subring $K[t^2]$, since $(t^3)^2=(t^2)^3$, and hence has dimension one. Evaluating at $t=0$ gives quotient $R/\mathfrak m=K$, making $\mathfrak m$ a nonzero maximal prime.

Its square has generators $t^4,t^5,t^6$. The first two generate $t^2\mathfrak m$, and $t^6=t^2t^4$ lies there, so $\mathfrak m^2=t^2\mathfrak m$. If $\mathfrak m$ were invertible, multiply by its inverse to obtain $\mathfrak m=t^2R$. But $t^3\notin t^2R$, since $t\notin R$: no polynomial in $t^2,t^3$ has a term of degree one. This contradiction proves noninvertibility.

The fraction field contains $t=t^3/t^2$, which is integral by $T^2-t^2=0$ but missing from $R$. Thus the failed condition is normality. Noetherianity and dimension one alone do not give Dedekind ideal factorisation or invertibility. The contradiction from cancellation pinpoints why the missing hypothesis matters.
\end{solution}
