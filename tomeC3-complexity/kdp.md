# Description

In 1936 Alan Turing imagined a clerk with a pencil and an endless strip of paper, and proved that some questions can never be answered by any machine. Five years earlier Kurt Gödel had shown that some true statements about whole numbers can never be proved. This book tells what came of these two discoveries: a theory of what can be computed and proved at all, and a theory of what can be computed quickly.

Twelve chapters. Finite automata and regular expressions, with the Myhill-Nerode theorem and minimisation. Grammars, parsing and pushdown automata. Turing machines, the universal machine and the halting problem. Undecidable problems in mathematics: Post's correspondence problem, word problems, tilings, Conway's FRACTRAN and Hilbert's tenth problem. The lambda-calculus, types and the correspondence between proofs and programs. Gödel's completeness and compactness theorems. Incompleteness, Tarski's undefinability and Goodstein's theorem. Time and space, hierarchies and Savitch's theorem. NP-completeness: the Cook-Levin theorem and Karp's reductions. Beyond NP: the polynomial hierarchy, PSPACE, counting and the exponential-time hypothesis. Randomness and interaction: interactive proofs, zero knowledge and probabilistically checkable proofs. Circuits and proof complexity: Razborov's lower bounds and the barriers to proving more.

Every theorem is proved in full, except for a few landmarks clearly labelled as quoted. Every chapter opens with a story and a question to think about, and has exercises graded by difficulty, each with a hint and a solution that first explains how to find the idea and then writes it up. Figures show the constructions, research papers come with reading guides, and every chapter ends with its sources.

Each chapter comes with a substantial Python notebook that builds the machines of the chapter and runs them: automata, Turing machines and busy beavers, lambda-terms, Gödel numbers, reductions checked by brute force, interactive protocols and minimal circuits.

The book is written for the pleasure of seeing why some things cannot be done. It is the third volume of the computing track of a series that goes from the foundations of number theory, algebra and probability to the edge of research.
