# Description

Three lines through the corners of any triangle, trisecting its angles, meet in the corners of an equilateral triangle, and nobody noticed until 1899. Circles packed into a circle can have whole-number curvatures forever. A chain of circles between two others closes up or never does, wherever you start. Six points on an ellipse always put three intersection points on one line. This book explains every one of these miracles, and many more.

It starts in the plane of Euclid, seen through complex numbers: the Euler line, the nine-point circle, Napoleon's and Morley's theorems. Groups of transformations explain why there are exactly seven patterns for a frieze and why no crystal has five-fold symmetry. Inversion in a circle turns tangent circles into parallel lines and proves Descartes' circle theorem and Steiner's porism. The projective plane, born from the perspective drawings of Renaissance painters, gives parallel lines a meeting point, and the theorems of Desargues and Pappus turn out to encode the laws of algebra. Conics, Pascal's hexagram, poles and polars, and Poncelet's porism follow, then quadratic forms and the straight lines hidden in curved surfaces, plane curves and Bezout's theorem, and the cubic curves whose points can be added like numbers, the starting point of the arithmetic of elliptic curves.

The last part widens the view: lattices and the densest packings of balls, from the hexagonal pattern of the plane to the miraculous lattice E8; the curved worlds of the sphere and of the hyperbolic plane, where the angles of a triangle no longer add up to pi; convex bodies, the isoperimetric inequality and Buffon's needle; and curvature, from the four vertices of every oval to the theorem of Gauss and Bonnet that ties the shape of a surface to its topology.

Every theorem is proved in full. Every picture is drawn from coordinates computed exactly, so what you see is true. Every chapter opens with a question, asks you to pause and guess before the key results, and ends with graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that explains how to find the idea, and with sources for the history and further reading.

The book is written for pleasure, in a storytelling style, for readers who know complex numbers, a little linear algebra and a little group theory. It comes with a free companion Python notebook for every chapter, with exercises first and solutions after, which tests the theorems on random examples and lets you explore: Apollonian gaskets, Poncelet polygons, finite planes, elliptic curves over finite fields, the 240 shortest vectors of E8, and needles dropped on a floor to measure pi.
