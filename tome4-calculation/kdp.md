# Description

In 1713 Jacob Bernoulli boasted that it had taken him less than half of a quarter of an hour to add up the tenth powers of the first thousand integers. In 1735 Euler announced that 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … = π²/6. In 1799 Gauss noticed that the arithmetic–geometric mean of 1 and √2 matched a constant he knew to eleven decimals, and wrote in his diary that a whole new field of analysis would open. This book is about that art: finding the exact value of a sum, a product or an integral, and, when none exists, an approximation with an error one can bound.

Its tools are finite calculus and generating functions; classic series and infinite products; integration tricks, including Feynman's favourite, differentiation under the integral sign; the Gamma and Beta functions; Fourier series, and Fourier analysis on finite groups, where it becomes the fast Fourier transform; the Fourier transform and Poisson summation; complex analysis and residues; the zeta function with its functional equation, and the precise sense in which 1 + 2 + 3 + … = −1/12; asymptotics, from Stirling's formula to the saddle point; and the arithmetic–geometric mean, which doubles the number of correct digits of π at every step.

Every result is proved in full. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas and has graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that explains how to find the idea, figures, and sources for further reading.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
