\chapter{Cuts, Mixing and Expansion}\label{ch:cheeger}
\begin{center}\itshape
A bottleneck has a small boundary; a Laplacian eigenvector helps us find it.
\end{center}

Two busy networks joined by three cables are connected. They are also easy to separate: cut those three cables. Mere connectedness misses the bottleneck. To measure it, compare the number of edges leaving a set with the number of vertices inside it. A small ratio means a large piece can be detached cheaply.

The matrix detects this weakness through a vector that is nearly constant on each side of the cut. Its coordinates change little across most edges, so its energy is small. Minimising that energy under a zero-sum constraint produces the second Laplacian eigenvalue. Cheeger's inequality compares it with the cheapest cut, and its proof gives a way to find a cut by sorting the eigenvector's coordinates.

Unless specified otherwise, take a finite simple $d$-regular graph with $n\ge2$ vertices. For its adjacency matrix $A$, define the \emph{Laplacian} $L=dI-A$. More generally the Laplacian of a nonregular graph is $D-A$, where $D$ is the diagonal matrix of degrees; this convention is needed for the path exercise. In either case,
\[
x^{\mathsf T}Lx=\sum_{uv\in E}(x_u-x_v)^2.
\]
Each edge is counted once. Expanding its square gives the identity, since the coefficient of $x_u^2$ is the degree of $u$. Thus $L$ is positive semidefinite and $L\mathbf1=0$. For a regular graph its eigenvalues in increasing order are $\mu_i=d-\lambda_i$, where $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n$ are the adjacency eigenvalues.

The Rayleigh argument from Theorem~\ref{linear:thm:courantfischer} has a particularly useful form here.

\begin{lemma}[Rayleigh]\label{expansion:lem:rayleigh}
Let a real symmetric matrix $M$ have eigenvalues $\nu_1\le\cdots\le\nu_n$ with an orthonormal eigenbasis $\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_n$. If a non-zero vector $x$ is orthogonal to $\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_{k-1}$, then
$x^{\mathsf T}Mx\ge\nu_kx^{\mathsf T}x$.
Equality is attained by $x=\psi_k$. In particular, for a graph Laplacian with $0=\mu_1\le\cdots\le\mu_n$,
\[
\mu_2=\min_{x\ne0,\ x\perp\mathbf1}\frac{x^{\mathsf T}Lx}{x^{\mathsf T}x}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $x=\sum_ic_i\psi_i$. Orthogonality gives $c_i=0$ for $i<k$, so
$x^{\mathsf T}Mx=\sum_{i\ge k}\nu_ic_i^2\ge\nu_k\sum_{i\ge k}c_i^2$.
Taking $x=\psi_k$ attains equality. For the Laplacian, choose $\psi_1=\mathbf1/\sqrt n$ and complete it to an eigenbasis, including any further zero eigenvectors. The case $k=2$ gives the formula even when the graph is disconnected.
\end{proof}

\section{Cutting a graph: Cheeger's inequality}\label{spectral:sec:cheeger}

For a vertex set $S$, put $\bar S=V\setminus S$ and let $e(S,\bar S)$ count edges crossing its boundary. The \emph{Cheeger constant}, or \emph{edge expansion}, is
\[
h(G)=\min_{\varnothing\ne S,\ |S|\le n/2}\frac{e(S,\bar S)}{|S|}.
\]
We always use the smaller side of a cut. Finding the exact minimum is NP-hard in general. The eigenvalue gives two bounds with no search through all subsets.

\begin{theorem}[Cheeger's inequality: Dodziuk, 1984; Alon and Milman, 1985]\label{spectral:thm:cheeger}
Every $d$-regular graph on at least two vertices satisfies
\[
\frac{\mu_2}{2}\le h(G)\le\sqrt{2d\mu_2}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{From a cut to a vector.} Choose a minimising set $S$, with $s=|S|\le n/2$, and centre its indicator:
$x=\mathbf1_S-(s/n)\mathbf1$.
It is non-zero and orthogonal to $\mathbf1$. Its coordinate difference across an edge is zero unless that edge crosses the cut, when its square is $1$. Also
\[
x^{\mathsf T}Lx=e(S,\bar S),\qquad
\|x\|^2=s(1-s/n)^2+(n-s)(s/n)^2=\frac{s(n-s)}n.
\]
The Rayleigh lemma therefore gives
$\mu_2\le ne(S,\bar S)/(s(n-s))\le2h(G)$.
This proves the lower bound.

\emph{From a vector to cuts.} Choose a $\mu_2$-eigenvector $x\perp\mathbf1$, $x\ne0$. Both positive and negative coordinates occur. Replace $x$ by $-x$ if needed so that its positive support has size at most $n/2$. Put $y_v=\max(x_v,0)$, a non-zero vector supported on that set.
For $y_v>0$, we have $y_v=x_v$ and $y_u\ge x_u$ for every neighbour, whence
\[
(Ly)_v=dy_v-\sum_{u\sim v}y_u\le dx_v-\sum_{u\sim v}x_u=\mu_2y_v.
\]
Multiplying by $y_v$ and summing proves $y^{\mathsf T}Ly\le\mu_2\|y\|^2$.

For $t\ge0$, let $S_t=\{v:y_v^2>t\}$. Every nonempty such set has at most $n/2$ vertices. The values of $t$ for which an edge $uv$ crosses its boundary form an interval of length $|y_u^2-y_v^2|$. The values for which a vertex belongs to $S_t$ have total length $y_v^2$. Integrating these finite step functions gives
\[
h(G)\|y\|^2\le\int_0^\infty e(S_t,\bar S_t)\dd t
=\sum_{uv\in E}|y_u^2-y_v^2|.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the last sum by
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{uv\in E}|y_u-y_v|(y_u+y_v)
&\le\sqrt{y^{\mathsf T}Ly}\,
 \sqrt{\sum_{uv\in E}(y_u+y_v)^2}\\
&\le\sqrt{\mu_2\|y\|^2}\,\sqrt{2d\|y\|^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For the second bound use $(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2$ and count each vertex in $d$ edges. Dividing by $\|y\|^2>0$ finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

The proof supplies a finite procedure called \emph{spectral partitioning}. Between consecutive distinct positive values of $y_v^2$, the set $S_t$ is unchanged. At least one nonempty such set has boundary ratio at most $\sqrt{2d\mu_2}$: otherwise summing the strictly larger ratios with their positive interval lengths would contradict the proved integral inequality. Sorting the coordinates leaves at most $n$ different \emph{sweep sets} to inspect.

The eigenvector is often called a \emph{Fiedler vector}. Fiedler studied $\mu_2$ as algebraic connectivity in 1973. Shi and Malik's 2000 image-segmentation work used a normalised cut problem on a graph of pixels. The shared idea is concrete: vertices with similar eigenvector coordinates can be grouped together.

The cube attains the lower bound. Its second Laplacian eigenvalue is $2$, by Example~\ref{linear:exa:spectra}, so $h(Q_n)\ge1$. A coordinate half-cube has one outgoing edge per vertex, giving $h(Q_n)=1$. Cycles show why the upper bound needs a square root: their expansion is of order $1/n$, whereas $\mu_2$ is of order $1/n^2$. Exercise~\ref{spectral:ex:cheegercycle} computes the constants.

\section{Expanders}

To control both positive and negative adjacency eigenvalues, put
\[
\lambda=\max(|\lambda_2|,|\lambda_n|).
\]
This means the operator norm on $\mathbf1^\perp$, including further copies of $d$ if the graph is disconnected. A graph with these parameters is an \emph{$(n,d,\lambda)$-graph}. Cheeger's lower bound says $h(G)\ge(d-\lambda_2)/2$. Thus a small $\lambda/d$ forces every small set to have many boundary edges.

For fixed $d$, a sequence with vertex counts tending to infinity and $\lambda\le(1-\varepsilon)d$, for some fixed $\varepsilon>0$, is a family of expanders with a two-sided spectral gap. Some authors use a one-sided gap $d-\lambda_2$ instead. The distinction matters for bipartite graphs: they have eigenvalue $-d$, so their non-lazy walks alternate between the two sides even when their cuts expand well.

The same centred indicators used for cuts also count edges between arbitrary sets.

\begin{lemma}[expander mixing lemma, Alon and Chung, 1988]\label{spectral:lem:mixing}
Let $G$ be an $(n,d,\lambda)$-graph. For $S,T\subseteq V$, let $e(S,T)$ count ordered adjacent pairs $(s,t)\in S\times T$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|e(S,T)-\frac{d|S||T|}{n}\right|
&\le\lambda\sqrt{|S||T|(1-|S|/n)(1-|T|/n)}\\
&\le\lambda\sqrt{|S||T|}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\mathbf1_S=(|S|/n)\mathbf1+x$ and $\mathbf1_T=(|T|/n)\mathbf1+y$. Both remainders are orthogonal to $\mathbf1$. Regularity cancels the cross terms, leaving
\[
e(S,T)=\mathbf1_S^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf1_T
=\frac{d|S||T|}{n}+x^{\mathsf T}Ay.
\]
On $\mathbf1^\perp$, the eigenbasis gives $\|Ay\|\le\lambda\|y\|$. Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the error by $\lambda\|x\|\|y\|$. Finally $\|x\|^2=|S|(1-|S|/n)$ and the analogous identity holds for $y$. Dropping the two factors at most $1$ gives the second bound.
\end{proof}

For an independent nonempty $S$, the ordered count $e(S,S)$ is zero. The lemma yields $d|S|^2/n\le\lambda|S|(1-|S|/n)$ and hence $|S|\le n\lambda/(d+\lambda)$ when $d>0$. Compare the least-eigenvalue bound in Theorem~\ref{linear:thm:hoffman}. Powers of $A$ give further consequences: short paths between all pairs of vertices and convergence of random walks, proved in Exercises~\ref{spectral:ex:diameter} and~\ref{spectral:ex:walkmixing}.

How small can the nonconstant eigenvalues be in a large regular graph? A pair of test vectors supported far apart gives a universal obstruction.

\begin{theorem}[Alon and Boppana, 1986; Nilli's proof, 1991]\label{spectral:thm:alonboppana}
Let $d\ge2$ and let $k$ be a non-negative integer. Suppose a $d$-regular graph contains two edges such that every endpoint of one is at distance at least $2k+2$ from every endpoint of the other. Then
\[
\lambda_2\ge2\sqrt{d-1}-\frac{2\sqrt{d-1}-1}{k+1}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Around the first edge $\{v_1,v_2\}$, let $V_i$ consist of vertices at distance $i$ from that edge. Set $a_i=(d-1)^{-i/2}$ and define $f$ to equal $a_i$ on $V_i$ for $0\le i\le k$, and zero elsewhere.
Every vertex in a positive layer has a neighbour in the preceding layer; the two vertices in $V_0$ are neighbours of one another. Each vertex therefore has at most $d-1$ neighbours in the next layer. Consequently $|V_{i+1}|\le(d-1)|V_i|$.
Only edges between successive layers contribute to the energy, including those leaving $V_k$. There are at most $(d-1)|V_i|$ edges from layer $i$ to layer $i+1$, so
\[
f^{\mathsf T}Lf\le\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(d-1)|V_i|(a_i-a_{i+1})^2
+(d-1)|V_k|a_k^2.
\]
Put $b=\sqrt{d-1}$. Since $(d-1)(a_i-a_{i+1})^2=(d-2b)a_i^2$, this becomes
\[
f^{\mathsf T}Lf\le(d-2b)\|f\|^2+(2b-1)|V_k|a_k^2.
\]
The layer masses $|V_i|a_i^2$ are non-increasing. Their last value is at most their average, giving $|V_k|a_k^2\le\|f\|^2/(k+1)$. Thus
$f^{\mathsf T}Lf\le\theta\|f\|^2$, where $\theta=d-2b+(2b-1)/(k+1)$.

Construct $g$ around the other edge in exactly the same way. Its energy obeys the same bound. The distance hypothesis puts the two supports at distance at least $2$, so they are disjoint and no edge joins them. Choose $c=(\sum_v f_v)/(\sum_v g_v)>0$. Then $h=f-cg$ is non-zero and orthogonal to $\mathbf1$. Disjointness and the absence of joining edges give
\[
h^{\mathsf T}Lh=f^{\mathsf T}Lf+c^2g^{\mathsf T}Lg
\le\theta(\|f\|^2+c^2\|g\|^2)=\theta\|h\|^2.
\]
The Rayleigh lemma gives $\mu_2\le\theta$, or $\lambda_2=d-\mu_2\ge d-\theta$, which is the claimed estimate.
\end{proof}

At most $2\sum_{i=0}^r(d-1)^i$ vertices lie within distance $r$ of an edge. For fixed degree, sufficiently large connected graphs therefore contain edge pairs at arbitrarily large distances. If a regular graph is disconnected, its repeated top eigenvalue already gives $\lambda_2=d\ge2\sqrt{d-1}$. In either case the theorem implies $\lambda_2\ge2\sqrt{d-1}-o(1)$ as the vertex count tends to infinity. Exercise~\ref{spectral:ex:moorebound} makes the size estimate explicit.

The number $2\sqrt{d-1}$ is the adjacency spectral radius of the infinite $d$-regular tree, proved in the next chapter. A connected $d$-regular graph is called \emph{Ramanujan} when every eigenvalue other than the trivial values $d$ and, if present, $-d$, has absolute value at most $2\sqrt{d-1}$. For a nonbipartite graph this says $\lambda\le2\sqrt{d-1}$ in our notation. Bipartite Ramanujan graphs keep the additional trivial eigenvalue $-d$; they do not satisfy that two-sided bound with our present $\lambda$.

Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak, and independently Margulis, constructed Ramanujan families using number theory in 1988. Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava later proved the existence of bipartite Ramanujan families in every degree. Friedman's 2008 theorem says that, for fixed $d\ge3$ and each $\varepsilon>0$, a uniformly random simple $d$-regular graph has $\lambda\le2\sqrt{d-1}+\varepsilon$ with probability tending to $1$ along admissible vertex counts. These results are quoted here; the next chapter develops the tree, constructions and graph zeta function behind them.

\begin{beauty}
A small cut gives a vector with little edge energy. An eigenvector with little energy gives a small cut. Cheeger's proof carries the information in both directions, using only coordinate differences, level sets and Cauchy--Schwarz. That is why an algebraic minimisation can return a useful partition of an actual network.
\end{beauty}

\section{The tree sets the scale}

A spectral gap controls three different tasks: finding a cut, comparing edge counts with their uniform average, and estimating a walk's remaining dependence on its start. The sign of the bottom eigenvalue matters for the last task; bipartite alternation survives even when the upper gap is large.

The obstruction $2\sqrt{d-1}$ suggests a new goal. Can finite graphs match the tree's nontrivial spectral bound? Chapter~\ref{ch:expanders} studies those graphs and the zeta function that expresses their bound as a graph version of the Riemann hypothesis.

\section{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{spectral:ex:cheegercycle}
Compute $h(C_n)$ and $\mu_2(C_n)$ exactly, obtaining $2/\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ and $2-2\cos(2\pi/n)$. Prove that no absolute constant $c$ can replace Cheeger's upper bound by $h(G)\le c\mu_2$ for all regular graphs.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{spectral:ex:diameter}
For a connected $(n,d,\lambda)$-graph with $\lambda<d$, prove $(A^t)_{uv}\ge d^t/n-\lambda^t$ for all vertices and non-negative integers $t$. Deduce a diameter bound of $\lfloor\ln n/\ln(d/\lambda)\rfloor+1$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{spectral:ex:walkmixing}
Assume $d>0$. A walk chooses a uniform neighbour at each step, and $p_t$ is its probability vector. For $u=\mathbf1/n$, prove $p_{t+1}=Ap_t/d$ and $\|p_t-u\|\le(\lambda/d)^t$ on an $(n,d,\lambda)$-graph. Allow any initial probability distribution.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{spectral:ex:petersenalpha}
Apply the mixing lemma to rule out an independent set of five vertices in the Petersen graph. Exhibit one with four vertices.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{spectral:ex:fiedlerpath}
For the path $P_n$ with $n\ge2$, show that its Laplacian eigenvalues are $2-2\cos(j\pi/n)$, with eigenvectors $x_v=\cos(j\pi(2v-1)/(2n))$, for $j=0,\ldots,n-1$ and $v=1,\ldots,n$. Describe the cut found by sweeping a Fiedler vector.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{spectral:ex:moorebound}
(a) Bound the number of vertices within distance $r$ of a fixed edge by $2\sum_{i=0}^r(d-1)^i$ in a $d$-regular graph with $d\ge2$. Prove equality if the girth is at least $2r+2$.
(b) If the graph has more than $2\sum_{i=0}^{2k+2}(d-1)^i$ vertices, find two edges at endpoint distance at least $2k+2$. Conclude that Theorem~\ref{spectral:thm:alonboppana} applies.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c06a}
Compute $h(K_n)$ and $\mu_2(K_n)$ for $n\ge2$. Determine when the lower Cheeger bound is an equality.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c06b}
On $C_4$ in cyclic order, let $P=(I+A/2)/2$ be the lazy transition matrix and start at vertex $1$. For each $t\ge1$, find the exact probability vector and its Euclidean distance from uniform. Compare with the usual walk's alternating eigenvector.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c06c}
For $y=(3,2,1,0)$ on the path with edges $12,23,34$, compute $\int_0^\infty|S_t|\dd t$ and $\int_0^\infty e(S_t,\bar S_t)\dd t$, where $S_t=\{v:y_v^2>t\}$. Check the two level-set identities directly, and list all nonempty sweep sets.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c06d}
Suppose a $d$-regular graph with $d>0$ has $\lambda_2<d$. For the lazy transition matrix $P=(I+A/d)/2$, prove
\[
\|p_t-u\|\le\left(1-\frac{d-\lambda_2}{2d}\right)^t\|p_0-u\|.
\]
Explain why this bound applies to connected bipartite graphs as well as nonbipartite ones.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:cheegercycle}
\idea An arc minimises the number of boundary edges, and the largest allowed arc minimises their ratio to size.
\writeup A nonempty proper set in $C_n$ consists of $r\ge1$ arcs and has $2r$ boundary edges. For $|S|\le n/2$, its boundary ratio is therefore at least $2/\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. An arc of exactly that many vertices attains the bound. The adjacency eigenvalues give $\mu_2=2-2\cos(2\pi/n)$. The elementary limit $(1-\cos z)/z^2\to1/2$ shows
\[
h(C_n)\sim\frac4n,\qquad \mu_2(C_n)\sim\frac{4\pi^2}{n^2}.
\]
Their ratio tends to infinity, excluding any universal linear upper bound in $\mu_2$. In fact $h(C_n)/\sqrt{2d\mu_2}\to1/\pi$, so Cheeger's square-root scale has the correct order here.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:diameter}
\idea The constant spectral contribution eventually exceeds the absolute error from every other direction.
\writeup Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis with $\psi_1=\mathbf1/\sqrt n$. The spectral expansion reads
\[
(A^t)_{uv}=\frac{d^t}{n}+\sum_{i\ge2}\lambda_i^t\psi_i(u)\psi_i(v).
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz bounds $\sum_{i\ge2}|\psi_i(u)\psi_i(v)|$ by $1$, since the complete rows of the eigenvector matrix have squared norm $1$. This proves the desired lower bound for every $t\ge0$. Here $0<\lambda<d$: connectedness on at least two vertices gives $d>0$, and trace zero prevents all remaining eigenvalues from vanishing. Choose
$t=\lfloor\ln n/\ln(d/\lambda)\rfloor+1$.
Then $(d/\lambda)^t>n$, making every entry of $A^t$ positive. A walk of length $t$ joins each pair, so their distance is at most $t$ and the diameter obeys the same bound.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:walkmixing}
\idea Remove the stationary component before applying the operator norm.
\writeup The probability of arriving at $v$ is $p_{t+1}(v)=d^{-1}\sum_{w\sim v}p_t(w)$. This is the asserted matrix recursion. Since $Au=du$ and probability differences have coordinate sum zero,
\[
p_{t+1}-u=(A/d)(p_t-u),\qquad
\|p_{t+1}-u\|\le(\lambda/d)\|p_t-u\|.
\]
Iteration gives the stronger bound with initial factor $\|p_0-u\|$. For any probability vector,
$\|p_0-u\|^2=\sum_vp_0(v)^2-1/n\le1-1/n\le1$,
which proves the stated estimate. In a bipartite graph $\lambda=d$, so this bound promises no decay; a point-started usual walk really does alternate between the two parts.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:petersenalpha}
\idea With no internal edges, an independent set's entire expected edge count must fit inside the mixing error.
\writeup The spectrum $3,1,-2$ gives $\lambda=2$. For an independent set of size $s>0$, mixing gives $3s^2/10\le2s(1-s/10)$, or $s\le4$. Four pair vertices containing $1$ in the disjoint-pair model of Petersen have no edges between them, so the upper bound is attained.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:fiedlerpath}
\idea The cosine recurrence handles interior vertices; reflection at the endpoints supplies the missing neighbours.
\writeup Set $\theta=j\pi/n$ and $x_v=\cos((v-1/2)\theta)$. At an interior vertex,
$2x_v-x_{v-1}-x_{v+1}=(2-2\cos\theta)x_v$.
At the first endpoint use $x_0=\cos(-\theta/2)=x_1$. At the other use $x_{n+1}=\cos(j\pi+\theta/2)=(-1)^j\cos(\theta/2)=x_n$. Thus the same recurrence agrees with the degree-one endpoint equations. For $j=0,\ldots,n-1$, $x_1=\cos(j\pi/(2n))>0$, so each vector is non-zero. The eigenvalues are distinct, providing all $n$ eigenvalues and an eigenbasis.
At $j=1$, the coordinates decrease strictly along the path. Sweep sets are its initial or terminal segments. Each nonempty proper segment has one boundary edge, so the best ratio for a side of size at most $n/2$ comes from a segment of size $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. The cut is at the middle, with either of the two equivalent choices when $n$ is odd.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{spectral:ex:moorebound}
\idea Branching away from an edge permits at most $d-1$ choices per step.
\writeup (a) Let $V_i$ be its distance-$i$ layer. We have $|V_0|=2$ and $|V_{i+1}|\le(d-1)|V_i|$, as in Nilli's proof. Thus $|V_i|\le2(d-1)^i$, and summing gives the bound. If the girth is at least $2r+2$, every nonbacktracking path of length at most $r$ starting at one endpoint and first moving away from the fixed edge has a distinct endpoint, also distinct from those grown from the other endpoint. A repetition within one side would create a cycle of length at most $2r$, and a repetition between sides one of length at most $2r+1$. Such cycles are excluded. These paths therefore give exactly $2(d-1)^i$ new vertices at each depth $i\le r$. Edges between boundary vertices may still occur, but do not alter the vertex count.
(b) The size assumption supplies a vertex $x$ at distance at least $2k+3$ from the fixed edge. Choose a neighbour $x'$; it exists because $d\ge2$. Its distance is at least $2k+2$, so the edges have the required endpoint separation. The theorem gives its stated bound.
For fixed $d\ge3$, taking $k$ a sufficiently small positive multiple of $\log_{d-1}n$ makes the size condition hold for large $n$. This yields error $O(1/\log n)$. When $d=2$, the sum grows linearly in $k$, so one may take $k$ proportional to $n$, yielding the stronger error $O(1/n)$. In both cases the error tends to zero.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c06a}
\idea In a complete graph the boundary ratio depends only on the set's size.
\writeup A set of size $s$ has ratio $n-s$, minimised for $s=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Hence $h(K_n)=\lceil n/2\rceil$. The Laplacian $nI-J$ vanishes on constants and equals $nI$ on $\mathbf1^\perp$, so $\mu_2=n$. Its lower Cheeger bound is $n/2$. This equals $h(K_n)$ exactly when $n$ is even; for odd $n$ their difference is $1/2$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c06b}
\idea Laziness replaces each adjacency eigenvalue by a number between zero and one.
\writeup In cyclic order the initial mass decomposes exactly as
\[
(1,0,0,0)=\tfrac14(1,1,1,1)+\tfrac12(1,0,-1,0)
+\tfrac14(1,-1,1,-1).
\]
The three vectors have adjacency eigenvalues $2,0,-2$, respectively. Their lazy eigenvalues are $1,1/2,0$. Thus for every $t\ge1$,
\[
p_t=\tfrac14(1,1,1,1)+2^{-t-1}(1,0,-1,0),\qquad
\|p_t-u\|=\frac{2^{-t}}{\sqrt2}.
\]
For the usual transition $A/2$, the last component instead has eigenvalue $-1$ and changes sign forever. Its nonzero initial coefficient prevents convergence to uniform.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c06c}
\idea The integral is a finite sum of interval lengths times constant counts.
\writeup On $[0,1)$ the sweep set is $\{1,2,3\}$, on $[1,4)$ it is $\{1,2\}$, and on $[4,9)$ it is $\{1\}$. Beyond $9$ it is empty. Thus
\[
\int_0^\infty|S_t|\dd t=3\cdot1+2\cdot3+1\cdot5=14
=9+4+1+0.
\]
Each nonempty listed set has one crossing path edge, giving integral $1+3+5=9$. The edge-difference sum gives the same value: $|9-4|+|4-1|+|1-0|=5+3+1=9$. These prove both identities directly for this vector. This example illustrates the level-set identities on a nonregular graph; it does not apply the regular-graph theorem to a path.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c06d}
\idea The lazy transition moves the whole adjacency spectrum into $[0,1]$.
\writeup The row-sum bound gives $-d\le\lambda_i\le d$. Therefore the eigenvalues of $P$ are $\eta_i=(1+\lambda_i/d)/2\in[0,1]$. The constant eigenvalue is $1$, and the largest eigenvalue on $\mathbf1^\perp$ is $\eta_2=1-(d-\lambda_2)/(2d)<1$. Since $P$ fixes $u$ and $p_0-u\perp\mathbf1$, its spectral expansion gives
$\|P^t(p_0-u)\|\le\eta_2^t\|p_0-u\|$,
which is the requested bound. Connectedness is equivalent to $\lambda_2<d$ here. If the graph is bipartite, the eigenvalue $-d$ becomes zero rather than $-1$, so it causes no alternation and the estimate still gives convergence.
\end{solution}
