\chapter{Tauberian Theorems}\label{ch:tauberian}

\begin{center}\itshape
Averages can converge while the terms keep oscillating. Tauberian theorems tell us when we can recover the terms from those averages. Positivity or a smallness condition supplies the information that smoothing lost.
\end{center}

Abel's 1826 paper in the first volume of Crelle's journal made a careful distinction. If a series converges, its power series approaches that sum at the boundary. It does not follow that every boundary limit is an ordinary sum. For example, $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^n$ tends to $1/2$ as $x\uparrow1$, although $\sum(-1)^n$ diverges.

Abel was troubled by the careless handling of infinite series. In the same paper he gave a counterexample to Cauchy's assertion about continuity of series sums, and his 1826 letters complain about the lack of convincing proofs in analysis. The familiar quotation calling divergent series the devil's invention is harder to document; his actual mathematical objection is clear enough without it.

Tauber found a converse in 1897: if $na_n\to0$, a finite Abel limit forces $\sum a_n$ to converge. Hardy and Littlewood recognised the wider pattern and named it after him. A transform suppresses some detail; an extra condition on the original data lets us recover its asymptotic behaviour.

The four arguments here recover different kinds of information. Tauber compares a power series with its partial sums. Karamata uses polynomial approximation and positivity to recover a measure's growth from its Laplace transform. Wiener proves that translates of one $L^1$ function span $L^1$ exactly when its Fourier transform misses no frequency. Finally, Wiener--Ikehara turns a Dirichlet-series pole and boundary continuity into an asymptotic for non-negative coefficients. With the nonvanishing theorem from Volume~6, it proves the prime number theorem again. Karamata also gives a weighted count of sums of two squares, including its exact constant.

\section{Abel and Ces\`aro: regular ways to sum}

Write $s_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_k$ and $\sigma_n=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^ns_k$. These are the partial sums and their Ces\`aro averages. Both Abel and Ces\`aro summation respect an existing limit because their weights eventually leave every fixed finite initial segment.

\begin{lemma}[regular weights]\label{lem:regular}
For $0<x<1$, let $w_n(x)\ge0$, $\sum_{n\ge0}w_n(x)=1$, and assume $w_n(x)\to0$ as $x\uparrow1$ for each fixed $n$. Whenever $s_n\to s$, one has $\sum_nw_n(x)s_n\to s$ as $x\uparrow1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Convergence bounds $|s_n|$ by some $M$, so every weighted series is absolutely convergent. Choose $N$ with $|s_n-s|<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Then
\[\left|\sum_nw_n(x)s_n-s\right|
 \le(M+|s|)\sum_{n<N}w_n(x)+\varepsilon.
\]
The finite sum tends to zero. Taking the limit superior and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Abel, 1826]\label{thm:abel}
If $\sum_{n\ge0}a_n=s$ in the ordinary sense, then $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ converges for $|x|<1$ and tends to $s$ as $x\uparrow1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The coefficients are bounded, giving absolute convergence inside the unit disc. For $0<x<1$, boundedness of the partial sums permits
\[f(x)=(1-x)\sum_{n\ge0}s_nx^n.
\]
This follows by subtracting the shifted series; the coefficient at $x^n$ becomes $s_n-s_{n-1}=a_n$. The weights $(1-x)x^n$ sum to one and tend individually to zero. Lemma~\ref{lem:regular} now gives the boundary limit.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cesaroabel}
If the Ces\`aro means $\sigma_n$ tend to $\sigma$, then the Abel limit of $\sum a_n$ exists and equals $\sigma$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The identity $s_n=(n+1)\sigma_n-n\sigma_{n-1}$ gives $s_n=O(n)$, hence absolute convergence of $S(x)=\sum_ns_nx^n$ for $|x|<1$. Summing the coefficients once more gives
\[\sum_n(n+1)\sigma_nx^n=\frac{S(x)}{1-x}.
\]
Thus $f(x)=(1-x)^2\sum_n(n+1)\sigma_nx^n$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:regular} to the weights $(1-x)^2(n+1)x^n$, whose sum is one by differentiating the geometric series.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:gregoryseries}
For $a_n=(-1)^n$, the partial sums alternate between one and zero. Their Ces\`aro averages approach $1/2$, as does the Abel expression $1/(1+x)$. Figure~\ref{fig:abelmeans} compares them. The converse implication between the methods fails: Exercise~\ref{ex:cesaroabel} gives an Abel sum with no Ces\`aro sum.
\end{example}

\section{Tauber's theorem}

Tauber's 1897 paper in the \emph{Monatshefte} gives a surprisingly short converse. Much of his working life was spent as an actuary and chief mathematician at Vienna's Ph\"onix insurance company, alongside university teaching. In June 1942 he was deported to Theresienstadt, where he died aged seventy-five.

\portrait{tauber}{Alfred Tauber (1866--1942). Artist unknown, about 1915. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

\begin{theorem}[Tauber, 1897]\label{thm:tauber}
Suppose $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ for $|x|<1$, with $f(x)\to s$ as $x\uparrow1$ and $na_n\to0$. Then the ordinary series $\sum_na_n$ converges to $s$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $\varepsilon_n=n|a_n|$, $\eta_N=\sup_{n>N}\varepsilon_n$ and $\mu_N=N^{-1}\sum_{n\le N}\varepsilon_n$. Both $\eta_N$ and $\mu_N$ tend to zero. The second assertion follows by separating a fixed initial segment from a uniformly small tail.

For $N\ge2$ choose $x=1-1/N$. The difference from the partial sum is
\[s_N-f(x)=\sum_{n\le N}a_n(1-x^n)-\sum_{n>N}a_nx^n.
\]
Since $1-x^n\le n(1-x)=n/N$, the first sum has modulus at most $\mu_N$. For $n>N$, $|a_n|\le\eta_N/N$, so the second is at most $\eta_N/(N(1-x))=\eta_N$. Hence $s_N-f(1-1/N)\to0$, and the assumed Abel limit finishes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Littlewood proved in 1911 that the weaker condition $a_n=O(1/n)$ suffices. His account describes how difficult that proof was; Wielandt gave a short proof in 1952 using Karamata's method. We quote Littlewood's result for context and will not use it in a proof.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
Some extra assumption is necessary, as the alternating ones show, but Tauber's condition is not necessary for convergence and is not the weakest sufficient condition. Arithmetic applications usually need a different kind of information: positivity of large coefficients rather than small individual terms.
\end{remark}

\section{Karamata's theorem}

Hardy and Littlewood proved in 1914 that non-negative coefficients satisfy a useful converse to an Abel asymptotic: $\sum a_nx^n\sim C/(1-x)$ forces $\sum_{n\le N}a_n\sim CN$. Karamata's 1930 proof replaced a long argument with polynomial approximation. We use its measure form.

Let $\mu$ be a positive Borel measure on $[0,\infty[$, with
\[U(x)=\mu([0,x]),\qquad\omega(t)=\int_{[0,\infty[}e^{-tu}\dd\mu(u)<\infty\quad(t>0).
\]
We want to infer $U$ at infinity from $\omega$ near zero. Powers alone are too restrictive; logarithmic corrections also occur.

\begin{definition}
A positive measurable function $\ell$ on $]0,\infty[$ is \emph{slowly varying at infinity} if $\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\to1$ as $x\to\infty$ for every fixed $\lambda>0$.
\end{definition}

Examples include positive constants and, for sufficiently large $x$, $\log x$, $(\log x)^\alpha$ and $\log\log x$. Extend these positively on the remaining bounded interval to fit the definition. By contrast, $x^\alpha$ is not slowly varying unless $\alpha=0$. A function $x^\rho\ell(x)$ is called \emph{regularly varying of index $\rho$}; fixed dilations act on it asymptotically by the factor $\lambda^\rho$.

\begin{theorem}[Hardy, Littlewood, Karamata]\label{thm:karamata}
For $\mu,U,\omega$ above, let $\rho\ge0$ and let $\ell$ be slowly varying. If
\[\omega(t)\sim t^{-\rho}\ell(1/t)\qquad(t\downarrow0),\]
then
\[U(x)\sim\frac{x^\rho\ell(x)}{\Gamma(\rho+1)}\qquad(x\to\infty).\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For bounded Borel $g$ on $[0,1]$, define the positive normalised form
\[I_t(g)=\frac1{\omega(t)}\int e^{-tu}g(e^{-tu})\dd\mu(u).
\]
The assumed asymptotic makes $\mu$ nonzero, so $\omega(t)>0$. Define a candidate limit by
\[I(g)=\frac1{\Gamma(\rho)}\int_0^\infty e^{-v}g(e^{-v})v^{\rho-1}\dd v\quad(\rho>0),\qquad I(g)=g(1)\quad(\rho=0).
\]
These forms are positive and satisfy $I_t(1)=I(1)=1$, so each has bound $|I_t(g)|,|I(g)|\le\|g\|_\infty$.

For an integer $k\ge0$, the hypothesis and slow variation give
\[I_t(y^k)=\frac{\omega((k+1)t)}{\omega(t)}\longrightarrow(k+1)^{-\rho}=I(y^k).
\]
Linearity proves convergence for polynomials. For continuous $g$, Weierstrass approximation (Volume~7, Theorem~4.19) supplies $P$ with $\|g-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Then
\[|I_t(g)-I(g)|\le2\varepsilon+|I_t(P)-I(P)|,
\]
proving convergence for every continuous $g$.

The function that recovers the desired interval mass is $g_0(y)=y^{-1}\mathbf1_{[e^{-1},1]}(y)$, assigned zero at $y=0$. It satisfies
\[I_t(g_0)=\frac{U(1/t)}{\omega(t)},\qquad I(g_0)=\frac1{\Gamma(\rho+1)}.
\]
For $\rho>0$, the latter value follows by integrating $v^{\rho-1}$ from zero to one; for $\rho=0$, it is $g_0(1)=1$.

To justify convergence despite its jump, choose continuous ramps $\psi_\delta,\varphi_\delta$ with $\psi_\delta\le\mathbf1_{[e^{-1},1]}\le\varphi_\delta$. Let the lower ramp rise from zero to one on $[e^{-1},e^{-1}+\delta]$, and the upper on $[e^{-1}-\delta,e^{-1}]$. Both vanish near zero. Then $h^-_\delta=\psi_\delta/y$ and $h^+_\delta=\varphi_\delta/y$, extended by zero at zero, are continuous and enclose $g_0$. Their difference is bounded by $2e\mathbf1_{J_\delta}$ for small $\delta$, where $J_\delta=[e^{-1}-\delta,e^{-1}+\delta]$.

Positivity and the continuous-function limit give
\[I(h^-_\delta)\le\liminf_{t\downarrow0}I_t(g_0)
 \le\limsup_{t\downarrow0}I_t(g_0)\le I(h^+_\delta).
\]
For $\rho>0$, the measure defining $I$ assigns vanishing mass to these shrinking intervals, since the corresponding $v$ interval shrinks to $\{1\}$ and has an integrable density there. For $\rho=0$, $J_\delta$ misses $1$ for small $\delta$. Thus the outer bounds approach $I(g_0)$, proving
\[U(1/t)\sim\omega(t)/\Gamma(\rho+1).
\]
Set $x=1/t$ and insert the hypothesis.
\end{proof}

Positivity serves twice: it bounds the forms by the uniform norm, and it allows continuous functions to squeeze the jump. The proof uses slow variation only at fixed dilation factors $1/(k+1)$; no uniform convergence theorem for slowly varying functions is needed.

\begin{corollary}[power series]\label{cor:karamataseries}
Let $a_n\ge0$, with $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ finite for $0<x<1$. For $\rho\ge0$ and slowly varying $\ell$, the asymptotic $f(e^{-t})\sim t^{-\rho}\ell(1/t)$ as $t\downarrow0$ implies
\[\sum_{n\le x}a_n\sim x^\rho\ell(x)/\Gamma(\rho+1).\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Use the measure $\mu=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\delta_n$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:karamata}. Its Laplace transform is $f(e^{-t})$ and its distribution function is the asserted partial sum.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[a converse to Abel]\label{cor:abelconverse}
If $a_n\ge0$ and $\sum_na_nx^n$ tends to a finite $s$ as $x\uparrow1$, then $\sum_na_n$ converges ordinarily to $s$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The Abel expressions are non-negative, so $s\ge0$. If $s=0$, each fixed term satisfies $0\le a_nx^n\le f(x)$, giving $a_n=0$ on taking $x\uparrow1$. If $s>0$, apply Corollary~\ref{cor:karamataseries} with $\rho=0$ and the positive constant $\ell=s$.
\end{proof}

For non-negative coefficients, the boundary limit now determines the ordinary sum. No cancellation can conceal a divergent positive tail.

\section{Wiener's Tauberian theorem}

For bounded $\phi$ and $f\in L^1(\R)$, the convolution
\[(\phi*f)(x)=\int_\R\phi(x-y)f(y)\dd y\]
is a bounded continuous weighted average (Volume~7, Proposition~10.9(b)). Suppose it has a limit as $x\to\infty$. When does that force a corresponding limit for every other $L^1$ weight?

Wiener's 1932 theorem answers: it suffices that $\hat f$ never vanish. A missing frequency would be invisible to averaging by $f$. Chapter~\ref{ch:operators} introduced the Banach-algebra viewpoint, but $L^1(\R)$ has no convolution identity, so a naive global inverse argument is insufficient. We will construct inverses near one frequency, join them over a compact interval, and finish with an approximate identity.

Keep $\hat g(\xi)=\int g(x)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}\dd x$ and $\tau_yg(x)=g(x-y)$. The kernels we need are
\[F(x)=\left(\frac{\sin\pi x}{\pi x}\right)^2,\qquad\Lambda(\xi)=(1-|\xi|)_+.
\]
Convolving two indicators of $[-1/2,1/2]$ gives $\Lambda$. Proposition~\ref{prop:ftl1}(c) and inversion (Theorem~\ref{thm:ftinv}), using evenness, give $\hat F=\Lambda$. Thus $F\ge0$, $\int F=1$, and $F_a(x)=aF(ax)$ is an approximate identity as $a\to\infty$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.10), with transform $\Lambda(\xi/a)$.

Set $V=2F_2-F_1$. Its transform is the trapezoid
\[\hat V(\xi)=\begin{cases}1,&|\xi|\le1,\\2-|\xi|,&1\le|\xi|\le2,\\0,&|\xi|\ge2.\end{cases}\]
For $\delta>0$, put $V^{(\delta)}(x)=\delta V(\delta x)$. Its transform is one on $[-\delta,\delta]$ and zero off $[-2\delta,2\delta]$.

\begin{lemma}[local inverse]\label{lem:localinv}
For $f\in L^1(\R)$ and $\hat f(\xi_0)\ne0$, there exist $h\in L^1(\R)$ and $\delta>0$ such that $\hat h(\xi)\hat f(\xi)=1$ whenever $|\xi-\xi_0|\le\delta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First take $\xi_0=0$ and $\hat f(0)=1$. Let $w=f-V$, so $\int w=0$, and put $u_\delta=V^{(\delta)}*w$. Cancellation of the integral gives
\[u_\delta(x)=\int\bigl(V^{(\delta)}(x-y)-V^{(\delta)}(x)\bigr)w(y)\dd y.
\]
Consequently
\[\|u_\delta\|_1\le\int|w(y)|\,\|\tau_{\delta y}V-V\|_1\dd y\longrightarrow0.
\]
For each $y$, translation continuity in $L^1$ gives the limit (Volume~7, Proposition~10.8), and $2\|V\|_1|w(y)|$ provides an integrable majorant.

Choose $0<\delta<1/2$ with $\|u\|_1<1/2$, where $u=u_\delta$. Since $\hat V=1$ on the support of $\widehat{V^{(\delta)}}$, Fourier uniqueness gives $V^{(\delta)}*V=V^{(\delta)}$. Thus $V^{(\delta)}*f=V^{(\delta)}+u$ and, on $|\xi|\le\delta$, $\hat f(\xi)=1+\hat u(\xi)$. The norm-convergent geometric series
\[h=V^{(\delta)}+\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^nV^{(\delta)}*u^{*n}\]
has transform $\hat V(\xi/\delta)/(1+\hat u(\xi))$. Indeed, each term has norm at most $\|V\|_1\|u\|_1^n$. On the smaller interval this transform is $1/\hat f$.

For general $\xi_0$, apply the normalised case to $f_1(x)=f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi_0x}/\hat f(\xi_0)$. Its transform is $\hat f(\xi+\xi_0)/\hat f(\xi_0)$. If $h_1$ is its local inverse, set $h(x)=h_1(x)e^{2\pi i\xi_0x}/\hat f(\xi_0)$. Then
\[\hat h(\xi)\hat f(\xi)=\hat h_1(\xi-\xi_0)\hat f_1(\xi-\xi_0),\]
which equals one on the required interval.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Wiener, 1932]\label{thm:wienerl1}
If $f\in L^1(\R)$ has a nowhere-zero Fourier transform, the closed linear span of all its translates $\tau_yf$, $y\in\R$, equals $L^1(\R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Denote that closed span by $J$. First, $g*f\in J$ for every $g\in L^1$. For an interval indicator this follows by approximating $\int_a^b\tau_yf\dd y$ with Riemann sums in $L^1$, using translation continuity. It follows for step functions by linearity, then for all $g$ by density and $\|(g-s)*f\|_1\le\|g-s\|_1\|f\|_1$. The required density follows from compactly supported continuous approximation (Volume~7, Theorem~10.7), followed by uniform step approximation on a fixed interval.

Next suppose $K=\operatorname{supp}\hat g$ is compact. For each point of $K$, the local inverse lemma gives an open interval $I_i$ and $h_i\in L^1$ with $\hat h_i\hat f=1$ there; take a finite cover. There is $\rho>0$ such that each point of $K$ has its $\rho$-neighbourhood inside one of the $I_i$. To see this, otherwise a convergent sequence of bad points in $K$ would approach a point in some $I_i$, whose positive neighbourhood radius contradicts badness.

Choose $\eta>0$ with $2\eta<\rho$. The triangular hats $\Lambda_k(\xi)=\Lambda(\xi/\eta-k)$ sum to one on $\R$: between successive mesh points the two nonzero hats add to one. Each is the transform of $p_k(x)=\eta F(\eta x)e^{2\pi ik\eta x}\in L^1$. Only finitely many of their supports meet $K$; call those indices $S$. Each such support lies in some $I_{i(k)}$, because its diameter is $2\eta$ and it meets $K$. Therefore
\[\hat g=\sum_{k\in S}\hat g\Lambda_k
 =\sum_{k\in S}\hat g\Lambda_k\hat h_{i(k)}\hat f.
\]
Fourier uniqueness yields $g=h*f$ with $h=\sum_{k\in S}g*p_k*h_{i(k)}\in L^1$, so $g\in J$.

Finally, for arbitrary $g\in L^1$, the functions $F_a*g$ have compactly supported transforms and tend to $g$ in $L^1$ by the approximate-identity theorem. They belong to $J$ by the preceding argument. Closedness gives $g\in J$, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Wiener's Tauberian theorem]\label{thm:wienertaub}
Let $f\in L^1(\R)$ have nowhere-zero $\hat f$, let $\phi\in L^\infty(\R)$, and let $A\in\C$. If
\[(\phi*f)(x)\longrightarrow A\int f\qquad(x\to+\infty),\]
then $(\phi*g)(x)\to A\int g$ as $x\to+\infty$ for every $g\in L^1(\R)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $J$ consist of the $g$ satisfying the desired limit. It is linear, contains $f$, and is invariant under translation because $\phi*\tau_yg=\tau_y(\phi*g)$ and $\int\tau_yg=\int g$.

It is also closed. If $g_j\in J$ and $\|g_j-g\|_1\to0$, then
\[\limsup_{x\to\infty}|(\phi*g)(x)-A\int g|
 \le(\|\phi\|_\infty+|A|)\|g-g_j\|_1.
\]
Let $j\to\infty$. Thus $J$ contains the closed span of the translates of $f$, which is all $L^1$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:wienerl1}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
A zero of $\hat f$ really loses information. If $\hat f(\xi_0)=0$, take $\phi(x)=e^{2\pi i\xi_0x}$ and $A=0$. Then $\phi*f=0$, but $\phi*g=e^{2\pi i\xi_0x}\hat g(\xi_0)$ does not tend to zero when $\hat g(\xi_0)\ne0$. This includes $\xi_0=0$, where the surviving function is constant. Exercise~\ref{ex:wienerZ} gives the discrete analogue using Chapter~\ref{ch:operators}'s Wiener lemma.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
A reciprocal $1/\hat f$ need not be an $L^1$ transform: it need not even tend to zero at infinity. The proof avoids needing one everywhere. It constructs an inverse near a single frequency, joins finitely many inverses with triangular hats, then enlarges the frequency interval using an approximate identity. Each of these operations stays inside $L^1$.
\end{beauty}

\section{Wiener--Ikehara and the prime number theorem}

Ikehara, Wiener's student at MIT, published his theorem in 1931. Positivity of Dirichlet coefficients and a continuous boundary remainder suffice to turn a simple pole into linear growth of partial sums. His proof used Wiener's theorem. Bochner observed that a non-negative kernel with compactly supported transform gives a direct proof; that is the route below.

Use $k_\lambda=F_\lambda$, with
\[k_\lambda(v)=\lambda\left(\frac{\sin\pi\lambda v}{\pi\lambda v}\right)^2,\qquad
\hat k_\lambda(\xi)=(1-|\xi|/\lambda)_+.
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:fejerkernel} shows the two properties we need: positivity in the original variable and finite support in frequency.

\begin{theorem}[Wiener--Ikehara]\label{thm:ikehara}
Suppose $a_n\ge0$ and $F(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_nn^{-s}$ converges for $\Re s>1$. If, for some $C\ge0$, the difference $F(s)-C/(s-1)$ extends continuously to the closed half-plane $\Re s\ge1$, then
\[A(x)=\sum_{n\le x}a_n=Cx+o(x)\qquad(x\to\infty).
\]
For $C>0$ this is $A(x)\sim Cx$; for $C=0$ it means $A(x)=o(x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Summing the integrals $\int_n^\infty x^{-s-1}\dd x$ gives
\[\frac{F(s)}s=\int_1^\infty A(x)x^{-s-1}\dd x
 =\int_0^\infty S(v)e^{-sv}\dd v,\qquad S(v)=A(e^v).
\]
The function $S$ is non-decreasing and right-continuous. Put $\psi(v)=e^{-v}S(v)$ for $v\ge0$ and extend it by zero for $v<0$. We must show $\psi(v)\to C$. Define
\[H(s)=\frac{F(s)}s-\frac C{s-1}
 =\frac1s\left(F(s)-\frac C{s-1}\right)-\frac Cs.
\]
The second expression proves continuity on $\Re s\ge1$. For $\varepsilon>0$, the function $(\psi(v)-C)e^{-\varepsilon v}$ on $v\ge0$ is integrable, and its Fourier transform is $H(1+\varepsilon+2\pi i\xi)$. Indeed, its positive part is controlled by $F(1+\varepsilon)/(1+\varepsilon)$ and the constant part by $C/\varepsilon$.

Multiply the transform by the tent $\hat k_\lambda$ and invert. Absolute integrability and the bounded frequency range justify Fubini:
\[\begin{aligned}
&\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}H(1+\varepsilon+2\pi i\xi)(1-|\xi|/\lambda)e^{2\pi i\xi v}\dd\xi\\
&\hspace{1cm}=\int_0^\infty(\psi(w)-C)e^{-\varepsilon w}k_\lambda(v-w)\dd w.
\end{aligned}\tag{$*$}\label{eq:ikesmooth}
\]
Before removing $\varepsilon$, we need a bound on $\psi$. Fix $\lambda=1$. Continuity bounds $|H|$ by $M$ on $0\le\varepsilon\le1$, $|\xi|\le1$. Equation~\eqref{eq:ikesmooth} and positivity give
\[\int_0^\infty\psi(w)e^{-\varepsilon w}k_1(v-w)\dd w\le2M+C.
\]
For $v\ge0$ and $v\le w\le v+1/2$, monotonicity of $S$ gives $\psi(w)\ge e^{-1/2}\psi(v)$; also $k_1(v-w)\ge4/\pi^2$. Restrict the integral to this interval and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. We obtain $(2/\pi^2)e^{-1/2}\psi(v)\le2M+C$. Thus $0\le\psi\le B$ globally for some finite $B$.

Now fix $\lambda,v$ and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ in \eqref{eq:ikesmooth}. On the left, continuity of $H$ gives uniform convergence on the compact frequency interval. On the right, $(B+C)k_\lambda(v-w)$ is integrable, so dominated convergence applies. The resulting left side tends to zero as $v\to\infty$ by Riemann--Lebesgue (Proposition~\ref{prop:ftl1}(b)). The missing range $w<0$ has kernel mass tending to zero as $v\to\infty$. After $w=v+r$ we therefore have, for every $\lambda>0$,
\[\lim_{v\to\infty}\int_\R(\psi(v+r)-C)k_\lambda(r)\dd r=0.\tag{$**$}\label{eq:ikelimit}
\]

To remove the smoothing, set $\psi^*=\limsup_{v\to\infty}\psi(v)$ and $\psi_*=\liminf_{v\to\infty}\psi(v)$. Fix $\delta>0$ and let $m=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}k_\lambda$, which tends to one as $\lambda\to\infty$.

Choose $v_j\to\infty$ with $\psi(v_j)\to\psi^*$. At $v_j+\delta$, for $|r|\le\delta$, monotonicity implies $\psi(v_j+\delta+r)\ge e^{-2\delta}\psi(v_j)$. Outside that range $\psi-C\ge-C$. Equation~\eqref{eq:ikelimit} yields
\[0\ge(e^{-2\delta}\psi^*-C)m-C(1-m).
\]
Let $\lambda\to\infty$ and then $\delta\downarrow0$, giving $\psi^*\le C$.

Choose instead $\psi(v_j)\to\psi_*$. At $v_j-\delta$ and $|r|\le\delta$, for large $j$ the same monotonicity gives $\psi(v_j-\delta+r)\le e^{2\delta}\psi(v_j)$. The complementary integrand is bounded above by $B+C$, so
\[0\le(e^{2\delta}\psi_*-C)m+(B+C)(1-m).
\]
The same limits give $\psi_*\ge C$. Hence $\psi\to C$, proving $A(x)/x\to C$, including the zero case.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[the prime number theorem, second proof]\label{cor:pntikehara}
The sums $\psi(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n)$ satisfy $\psi(x)\sim x$, and consequently $\pi(x)\sim x/\log x$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $a_n=\Lambda(n)$. Their Dirichlet series is $F=-\zeta'/\zeta$ for $\Re s>1$, by logarithmic differentiation of Volume~5, Theorem~10.3. Zeta has a simple pole at one and is otherwise holomorphic on $\Re s>0$ (Volume~5, Proposition~10.6). The Euler product excludes zeros for $\Re s>1$, and Volume~6, Theorem~5.2, excludes them on the boundary line. This nonvanishing theorem is the arithmetic input imported here.

Near one, the Laurent expansion of zeta shows that $-\zeta'/\zeta$ has residue one. Everywhere else on the closed half-plane it is holomorphic in a neighbourhood of each point. Thus $F(s)-(s-1)^{-1}$ has the continuous extension required by Theorem~\ref{thm:ikehara}. It gives $\psi(x)\sim x$. Volume~6, Theorem~5.5, converts this to the prime-counting asymptotic.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[progressions]\label{cor:pntapikehara}
For $q\ge1$ and $\gcd(a,q)=1$,
\[\psi(x;q,a)=\sum_{\substack{n\le x\\n\equiv a\ (q)}}\Lambda(n)\sim\frac{x}{\varphi(q)}.\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Character orthogonality gives the non-negative coefficients $\Lambda(n)\mathbf1_{n\equiv a\ (q)}$ the Dirichlet series
\[F(s)=\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline\chi(a)\left(-\frac{L'}L(s,\chi)\right).
\]
For a nonprincipal character, $L(s,\chi)$ is holomorphic on $\Re s>0$ and nonzero on $\Re s\ge1$, including one, by Volume~6, Theorem~5.2. Its logarithmic derivative is therefore continuous on the closed half-plane. For the principal character,
\[-\frac{L'}L(s,\chi_0)=-\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(s)-\sum_{p\mid q}\frac{\log p}{p^s-1}.
\]
The finite extra sum is holomorphic for $\Re s>0$. Subtracting $1/(\varphi(q)(s-1))$ from $F$ therefore leaves a continuous boundary remainder. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:ikehara} with $C=1/\varphi(q)$.
\end{proof}

Volume~6 obtained both conclusions using Newman's contour argument. The Tauberian proof isolates a reusable theorem about non-negative Dirichlet coefficients. The same positivity will now give a count with a square-root singularity, using Karamata instead of Wiener--Ikehara.

\section{Counting sums of two squares}

Let $b(n)$ be one if $n=x^2+y^2$ for integers $x,y$, and zero otherwise. Volume~2, Theorem~7.3, says precisely which integers qualify: every prime congruent to three modulo four must occur to an even exponent.

Landau proved in 1908 that $B(x)=\sum_{n\le x}b(n)\sim Kx/\sqrt{\log x}$. Ramanujan announced the same leading term in his 1913 letter to Hardy, with an unjustified error claim. Their constant is $K=0.76422365\ldots$. The unweighted result requires more than the Tauberian theorem proved here. Its logarithmically weighted version is within reach, and contains the same exact constant.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twosqeuler}
For $\Re s>1$, the absolutely convergent series $F(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}b(n)n^{-s}$ satisfies
\[F(s)=\frac1{1-2^{-s}}\prod_{p\equiv1(4)}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}\prod_{q\equiv3(4)}(1-q^{-2s})^{-1},\]
and
\[F(s)^2=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_4)\frac1{1-2^{-s}}\prod_{q\equiv3(4)}(1-q^{-2s})^{-1}.\]
Along the real axis as $\sigma\downarrow1$,
\[F(\sigma)\sim\frac{K\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt{\sigma-1}},\qquad
K=\frac1{\sqrt2}\prod_{q\equiv3(4)}(1-q^{-2})^{-1/2}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $0\le b(n)\le1$, absolute convergence holds for $\Re s>1$. The prime-exponent criterion makes $b$ multiplicative. The Euler-product argument of Volume~5, Theorem~10.3, therefore gives one geometric factor allowing every exponent at $2$ and at primes $p\equiv1$, and only even exponents at $q\equiv3$. This is the first formula.

The character $\chi_4$ takes values $1$ and $-1$ at the two odd prime classes, so
\[\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_4)=\frac1{1-2^{-s}}\prod_{p\equiv1(4)}(1-p^{-s})^{-2}\prod_{q\equiv3(4)}(1-q^{-2s})^{-1}.
\]
Comparison with $F^2$ proves the second identity. The remaining product $P(s)=\prod_{q\equiv3(4)}(1-q^{-2s})^{-1}$ converges absolutely and locally uniformly for $\Re s>1/2$, since its logarithmic errors are bounded by a convergent prime subseries of $\sum n^{-2\sigma}$. Thus it is continuous at one. Use $(\sigma-1)\zeta(\sigma)\to1$ and $L(1,\chi_4)=\pi/4$ (Volume~6, Chapter~3, Example~3.11) to get
\[(\sigma-1)F(\sigma)^2\longrightarrow(\pi/4)\,2P(1)=\pi K^2.
\]
Positivity of $F(\sigma)$ fixes the square-root sign and proves the asymptotic.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:twosqlog}
The reciprocal sum over sums of two squares satisfies
\[\sum_{\substack{n\le x\\n=\square+\square}}\frac1n\sim2K\sqrt{\log x}\qquad(x\to\infty),\qquad K=0.7642236535\ldots.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Place mass $b(n)/n$ at $\log n$, obtaining $\mu=\sum_{n\ge1}(b(n)/n)\delta_{\log n}$. Its distribution function is $U(y)=\sum_{n\le e^y}b(n)/n$ and its Laplace transform is $F(1+t)$, finite for every $t>0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:twosqeuler} gives $F(1+t)\sim K\sqrt\pi\,t^{-1/2}$. Karamata with $\rho=1/2$ and constant $\ell=K\sqrt\pi$ yields
\[U(y)\sim\frac{K\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(3/2)}\sqrt y=2K\sqrt y.
\]
Replacing $y$ by $\log x$ proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The unweighted formula $B(x)\sim Kx/\sqrt{\log x}$ does imply our weighted formula by elementary partial summation:
\[\sum_{n\le x}\frac{b(n)}n=\frac{B(x)}x+\int_1^x\frac{B(t)}{t^2}\dd t.
\]
Insert the asymptotic, bound its relative error uniformly beyond a fixed threshold, and use $\int_2^x\dd t/(t\sqrt{\log t})=2\sqrt{\log x}+O(1)$. The reverse inference is the difficult one: logarithmic averaging can hide oscillations in an unweighted count. We quote Landau's unweighted result, not a proof of it. Delange's 1954 extension of Ikehara treats the branch-point singularity and gives Landau's form with further terms.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
Only primes congruent to three modulo four appear in the product for $K$. At those primes an odd exponent is forbidden; at the others it is allowed. The resulting Dirichlet series has a square-root singularity because its square has a simple pole. Karamata turns that exponent $1/2$ into the factor $\sqrt{\log x}$ in the weighted sum. More general multiplicative counting theorems relate other singularity exponents to logarithmic powers, but they require hypotheses beyond this example.
\end{beauty}

\section{What the extra hypotheses recover}

Tauber's smallness assumption compares a series directly with its Abel average. Positivity lets Karamata recover interval masses, while a nowhere-zero Fourier transform lets Wiener replace one averaging kernel by any other. Wiener--Ikehara combines positivity with continuity along a whole boundary line. Each hypothesis repairs a specific loss of information under smoothing.

For the prime number theorem, boundary nonvanishing comes from Volume~6; the Tauberian step converts it into coefficient growth. For sums of two squares, a real-axis square-root asymptotic already gives the weighted count. Littlewood's stronger converse, Landau's unweighted theorem and quantitative prime-counting errors remain outside the proofs here. The final chapter asks harder questions about zeta's values on vertical lines.

\section{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:cesaroabel}
\emph{A stronger summation method.} For $a_n=(-1)^n(n+1)$, (a) compute $f(x)$ and its Abel limit $1/4$; (b) find $s_n$ and $\sigma_n$, showing that the latter has two distinct subsequential limits; (c) identify the failed assumption in Tauber's theorem.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:divisorkaramata}
\emph{The divisor average.} Exercise~\ref{ex:lambert} gives $\sum_{n\ge1}d(n)e^{-nt}\sim t^{-1}\log(1/t)$. Apply Karamata to obtain $\sum_{n\le N}d(n)\sim N\log N$. Compare the information used with Dirichlet's sharper elementary formula $N\log N+(2\gamma-1)N+O(\sqrt N)$, quoted here.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:partitionsup}
\emph{Bounding partition coefficients.} Let $p(n)$ count partitions and $P(z)=\prod_{n\ge1}(1-z^n)^{-1}=\sum_np(n)z^n$ for $|z|<1$. (a) Prove
\[\log P(e^{-t})=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m(e^{mt}-1)}\le\frac{\pi^2}{6t}.\]
(b) Show $t\log P(e^{-t})\to\zeta(2)$ as $t\downarrow0$. (c) Deduce $\log p(n)\le\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$ for every $n\ge1$. Hardy--Ramanujan's quoted asymptotic $p(n)\sim e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}/(4n\sqrt3)$ has the same exponential factor; proving its lower bound and prefactor needs further ideas.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:quicksort}
\emph{Quicksort's mean cost.} With a uniform pivot on distinct items, the mean comparison count satisfies $C_0=0$ and $C_n=n-1+(2/n)\sum_{k<n}C_k$. (a) Derive
\[C(z)=\sum_nC_nz^n=\frac{2}{(1-z)^2}\left(\log\frac1{1-z}-z\right).\]
(b) Prove that $C_n$ is non-decreasing. (c) Use Karamata to find $\sum_{k\le n}C_k\sim n^2\log n$, then squeeze differences of these sums using (b) to obtain $C_n\sim2n\log n$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:wienerZ}
\emph{Discrete averaging.} Let $a\in\ell^1(\Z)$ with $\hat a(\theta)=\sum_ka_ke^{ik\theta}\ne0$ everywhere and $x\in\ell^\infty(\Z)$. Prove that $(a*x)_n\to s$ implies $x_n\to s/\hat a(0)$ as $n\to\infty$. Use Theorem~\ref{thm:wiener}. Conclude that $(2x_n+x_{n-1})/3\to c$ forces $x_n\to c$, whereas $(x_n+x_{n-1})/2\to0$ allows nonconvergence.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:ikeharaline}
\emph{Extra boundary poles.} Set $a_n=1+\cos(\log n)\ge0$. (a) Prove that its Dirichlet series is $\zeta(s)+(\zeta(s-i)+\zeta(s+i))/2$ for $\Re s>1$. (b) Establish
\[\sum_{n\le x}a_n=x+\frac{x}{\sqrt2}\cos(\log x-\pi/4)+O(\log x),\]
so the normalised sums have limit inferior $1-1/\sqrt2$ and limit superior $1+1/\sqrt2$. (c) Explain the obstruction to Wiener--Ikehara.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c15a}
\emph{Zero residue.} Take $a_n=1/n$. Check Wiener--Ikehara's assumptions with $C=0$, using $F(s)=\zeta(s+1)$. Verify its conclusion directly from the harmonic sums. Explain why writing $A(x)\sim0x$ would be meaningless.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c15b}
\emph{Recovering a uniformly continuous function.} Let $\phi:\R\to\C$ be bounded and uniformly continuous, and suppose $(\phi*f)(x)\to A\int f$ for some $f\in L^1$ whose transform never vanishes. Prove $\phi(x)\to A$ as $x\to\infty$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c15c}
\emph{The model for Karamata's constant.} For $\rho>0$, give $[0,\infty[$ the measure $u^{\rho-1}\dd u/\Gamma(\rho)$. Compute $U(x)$ and $\omega(t)$ exactly and check the Gamma factor in Theorem~\ref{thm:karamata}. Which measure gives the model when $\rho=0$ and $\omega(t)=1$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c15d}
\emph{A pole at a different abscissa.} Let $\alpha>0$, $a_n\ge0$, and suppose $F(s)=\sum a_nn^{-s}$ converges for $\Re s>\alpha$, with $F(s)-C/(s-\alpha)$ extending continuously to $\Re s\ge\alpha$, where $C>0$. Apply Wiener--Ikehara to $a_nn^{1-\alpha}$, then use partial summation to prove $\sum_{n\le x}a_n\sim(C/\alpha)x^\alpha$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}
\begin{solution}{ex:cesaroabel}
\idea The alternating linear coefficients retain oscillation under Ces\`aro averaging but have a simple power-series formula.
\writeup (a) Differentiating the geometric series gives $f(x)=(1+x)^{-2}$, hence Abel limit $1/4$. (b) Pairing terms yields $s_{2m}=m+1$ and $s_{2m+1}=-(m+1)$. The cumulative sums of $s_n$ are $1,0,2,0,3,0,\ldots$, so $\sigma_{2m}=(m+1)/(2m+1)\to1/2$ and $\sigma_{2m+1}=0$. (c) The quantities $na_n=(-1)^nn(n+1)$ do not tend to zero. Positivity also fails, so neither Tauber nor the positive-coefficient converse forces convergence.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:divisorkaramata}
\idea A logarithm fits into the slowly varying factor without changing the power index.
\writeup Extend $\ell(y)=\log y$ positively for small $y$. For each fixed $\lambda>0$, $\log(\lambda y)/\log y\to1$, so it is slowly varying. The hypothesis is $f(e^{-t})\sim t^{-1}\ell(1/t)$ with non-negative coefficients. Corollary~\ref{cor:karamataseries} gives $\sum_{n\le N}d(n)\sim N\log N/\Gamma(2)=N\log N$. This uses only the leading transform asymptotic, and therefore does not recover Dirichlet's next term or its error bound.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:partitionsup}
\idea Positivity bounds a single coefficient by the entire generating function; choosing the scale optimally gives the exponential estimate.
\writeup (a) The logarithm series and non-negative interchange give
\[\log P(e^{-t})=\sum_{n,m\ge1}\frac{e^{-nmt}}m
 =\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m(e^{mt}-1)}\le\frac1t\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m^2}.
\]
(b) Put $g(u)=u/(e^u-1)$. It lies between zero and one and tends to one at zero, so dominated convergence in $t\log P(e^{-t})=\sum_mg(mt)/m^2$ gives $\zeta(2)$.

(c) Since $p(n)e^{-nt}\le P(e^{-t})$, we have $\log p(n)\le nt+\pi^2/(6t)$. Choosing $t=\pi/\sqrt{6n}$ gives $\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$. Karamata does not apply directly to $P(e^{-t})$: its growth is exponential in $1/t$, rather than a fixed power times a slowly varying factor. The coefficient inequality alone supplies this upper bound and gives no matching lower estimate.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:quicksort}
\idea A telescoping recurrence gives an exact formula; monotonicity then removes the averaging in Karamata's conclusion.
\writeup (a) Subtracting consecutive multiplied recurrences gives
\[nC_n=(n+1)C_{n-1}+2(n-1)\quad(n\ge2).
\]
Thus $C_n/(n+1)-C_{n-1}/n=2(n-1)/(n(n+1))=-2/n+4/(n+1)$. Summation, with $C_1=0$, gives $C_n=2(n+1)H_n-4n$. The identity $\sum_nH_nz^n=(1-z)^{-1}\log(1/(1-z))$ and differentiation give the stated generating function.

(b) The exact formula yields $C_{n+1}-C_n=2H_n-2+2/(n+1)\ge0$ for $n\ge1$; also $C_1=C_0=0$.

(c) At $z=e^{-t}$ the generating function is asymptotic to $2t^{-2}\log(1/t)$. Karamata gives $S_n=\sum_{k\le n}C_k\sim n^2\log n$. For fixed $0<\theta<1$, monotonicity bounds $S_n-S_{\lfloor\theta n\rfloor}$ above by $(n-\lfloor\theta n\rfloor)C_n$, yielding
\[\liminf\frac{C_n}{n\log n}\ge1+\theta.
\]
Apply the lower monotonicity bound to a block running from $n$ to $\lfloor n/\theta\rfloor$ to obtain
\[\limsup\frac{C_n}{n\log n}\le1+1/\theta.
\]
Integer endpoints affect these limits by vanishing relative errors. Let $\theta\uparrow1$ to conclude $C_n\sim2n\log n$. Compared with the information bound $n\log_2n$, the leading-factor ratio is $2\ln2$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:wienerZ}
\idea The discrete Wiener inverse turns convergence of one convolution into convergence of the original sequence.
\writeup Theorem~\ref{thm:wiener}, under the identification in Section~\ref{sec:wienerlemma}, provides $b\in\ell^1(\Z)$ with $a*b=\delta_0$ and $\hat b=1/\hat a$. Set $y=a*x$, so $x=b*y$; absolute convergence justifies associativity with the bounded sequence. Each $y_{n-k}\to s$ and $|b_ky_{n-k}|\le|b_k|\|y\|_\infty$. Dominated convergence gives $x_n\to s\sum_kb_k=s/\hat a(0)$.

For weights $2/3,1/3$ at lags zero and one, $\hat a=(2+e^{i\theta})/3$ never vanishes and $\hat a(0)=1$. For equal weights, the transform vanishes at $\pi$; indeed $x_n=(-1)^n$ has consecutive averages zero without converging.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:ikeharaline}
\idea Shifting zeta's pole along the boundary creates a main-term-sized oscillation in the coefficients' sum.
\writeup (a) Substituting $\cos\log n=(n^i+n^{-i})/2$ gives the series identity by absolute convergence. (b) If $u(t)=\cos\log t$, then $|u'(t)|\le1/t$. Comparing each unit interval with its left endpoint gives a total error $O(\log x)$. Therefore
\[\sum_{n\le x}a_n=x+\Re\int_1^xt^i\dd t+O(\log x)
 =x+\Re\frac{x^{1+i}}{1+i}+O(\log x).
\]
The real part is $x(\cos\log x+\sin\log x)/2$, proving the formula and the two limits. (c) After subtracting $(s-1)^{-1}$, poles remain at $1\pm i$. The required continuous extension fails there. In \eqref{eq:ikesmooth}, boundary values blow up near frequencies $\pm1/(2\pi)$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, preventing the limiting step that would remove those oscillations.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c15a}
\idea Zero residue means sublinear growth, not a ratio to zero.
\writeup The shifted zeta series converges for $\Re s>0$ and is holomorphic near $\Re s\ge1$. Thus Wiener--Ikehara applies with $C=0$ and gives $A(x)=o(x)$. Directly, $A(x)=\sum_{n\le x}1/n=\log x+O(1)$ by integral comparison, so $A(x)/x\to0$. The notation $A(x)\sim Cx$ would require dividing by $Cx$, which is undefined when $C=0$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c15b}
\idea Uniform continuity prevents the narrow spikes that could survive while all fixed local averages converge.
\writeup Wiener gives $(\phi*g_\delta)(x)\to A$ for $g_\delta=(2\delta)^{-1}\mathbf1_{[-\delta,\delta]}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta$ so $|\phi(x-y)-\phi(x)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|y|\le\delta$, uniformly in $x$. Then $|\phi*g_\delta(x)-\phi(x)|\le\varepsilon$. Taking the limit superior of $|\phi(x)-A|$ gives at most $\varepsilon$; let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c15c}
\idea The Gamma normalisation makes the Laplace transform exactly a power.
\writeup Direct integration gives $U(x)=x^\rho/(\rho\Gamma(\rho))=x^\rho/\Gamma(\rho+1)$. Substitution $v=tu$ gives $\omega(t)=t^{-\rho}\Gamma(\rho)^{-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-v}v^{\rho-1}\dd v=t^{-\rho}$. Thus Karamata's asymptotic is an equality for this model. At index zero the unit point mass $\delta_0$ has transform one and distribution function one for every $x\ge0$, matching $1/\Gamma(1)=1$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c15d}
\idea Shift the Dirichlet variable to put the pole at one, then undo the weight by partial summation.
\writeup The coefficients $b_n=a_nn^{1-\alpha}$ have Dirichlet series $F(s+\alpha-1)$. It satisfies Theorem~\ref{thm:ikehara} with residue $C$, giving $B(x)=\sum_{n\le x}b_n=Cx+o(x)$. Partial summation yields
\[A(x)=x^{\alpha-1}B(x)-(\alpha-1)\int_1^xt^{\alpha-2}B(t)\dd t.
\]
The main terms combine as $Cx^\alpha-C(\alpha-1)(x^\alpha-1)/\alpha=(C/\alpha)x^\alpha+O(1)$. If $B(t)=Ct+R(t)$ with $R(t)=o(t)$, then $x^{\alpha-1}R(x)=o(x^\alpha)$. Split the remaining integral at a fixed large threshold: beyond it $|R(t)|\le\varepsilon t$, giving an error at most $\varepsilon\int t^{\alpha-1}\dd t=O(\varepsilon x^\alpha)$, while the fixed initial segment is $O(1)$. This proves the asserted asymptotic for every $\alpha>0$.
\end{solution}

W.~Rudin, \emph{Functional Analysis}, 2nd ed. (McGraw-Hill, 1991), Chapter~9, and H.~Reiter, \emph{Classical Harmonic Analysis and Locally Compact Groups}
(Oxford, 1968). S.~Ikehara, ``An extension of Landau's theorem in the analytic theory of numbers'', \emph{J.\ Math.\ Phys.\ (MIT)} 10 (1931), 1--12;
S.~Bochner's kernel proof is in his \emph{Vorlesungen \"uber Fouriersche Integrale} (Akademische Verlagsgesellschaft, 1932); the version we give follows
D.~J.~Newman's and J.~Korevaar's expositions.

E.~Landau, ``\"Uber die Einteilung der positiven ganzen Zahlen in vier Klassen nach der Mindestzahl der zu ihrer additiven Zusammensetzung erforderlichen
Quadrate'', \emph{Arch.\ Math.\ Phys.} (3) 13 (1908), 305--312, is the original proof of $B(x)\sim Kx/\sqrt{\log x}$; Ramanujan's announcement is in his
first letter to Hardy, 16 January 1913, reprinted in \emph{Collected Papers} (Cambridge, 1927), xxiii--xxx. H.~Delange, ``G\'en\'eralisation du
th\'eor\`eme de Ikehara'', \emph{Ann.\ Sci.\ \'Ec.\ Norm.\ Sup.} (3) 71 (1954), 213--242, handles singularities of the form $(s-1)^{-\alpha}$ and gives
Landau's form with an expansion. For the constant to many digits and its history, see S.~R.~Finch, \emph{Mathematical Constants} (Cambridge, 2003),
Section~2.3.

The two indispensable books are G.~H.~Hardy, \emph{Divergent Series} (Oxford, 1949), written by one of the two people who made the subject, and
J.~Korevaar, \emph{Tauberian Theory: A Century of Developments} (Springer, 2004), which is the modern survey. For the analysis of algorithms by these
methods, P.~Flajolet and R.~Sedgewick, \emph{Analytic Combinatorics} (Cambridge, 2009), Chapters~IV--VIII, and the quicksort recurrence in
D.~E.~Knuth, \emph{The Art of Computer Programming}, vol.~3, 2nd ed. (Addison-Wesley, 1998), Section~5.2.2.
