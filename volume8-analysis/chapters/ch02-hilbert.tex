\chapter{Hilbert Spaces}\label{ch:hilbert}

\begin{center}\itshape
Functions have lengths and right angles. Fourier coefficients act as coordinates, and the nearest point in a subspace is found by dropping a perpendicular. Pythagoras reaches much further than the plane.
\end{center}

Integral equations drew Hilbert into infinite-dimensional geometry. Fredholm showed in 1900 how an equation such as $f(x)-\int_0^1K(x,y)f(y)\,dy=g(x)$ could be approached through linear systems with ever more unknowns. He was a Swedish mathematician who also worked in insurance and later as an actuary. Hilbert heard of his work from Holmgren in the winter of 1900--1901. Six papers, published between 1904 and 1910, developed spectral theory for ``quadratic forms in infinitely many variables''. The variables were coefficients $x_n$ with $\sum x_n^2<\infty$.

\portrait{fredholm}{Erik Ivar Fredholm (1866--1927). Autochrome by Auguste L\'eon, 1 October 1921, Archives de la Plan\`ete, Mus\'ee Albert-Kahn. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

\portrait{hilbert}{David Hilbert (1862--1943). Photograph, artist unknown, before 1912. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

Schmidt made the geometry explicit in 1908: square-summable sequences have lengths, perpendiculars and projections onto closed subspaces. Riesz and Fischer had independently proved the completeness of $L^2([a,b])$ in 1907. Consequently every square-summable list of Fourier coefficients comes from a function. Sequences and functions could be viewed as two coordinate descriptions of one space. Von Neumann gave an axiomatic treatment for quantum mechanics in 1929 and used the name \emph{Hilbert space}. A later story has Hilbert asking a lecturer what a Hilbert space actually was; without a first-hand source, we leave it as a possible legend.

The useful geometry begins with a nearest point in a closed convex set. It leads to the representation of linear forms, to Fourier bases, and to the identification of infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces with $\ell^2$. The same projection argument controls averages along orbits. Riesz representation also gives another proof of Radon--Nikodym and constructs spaces where a kernel represents point evaluation.

\section{Inner products}

Our inner products are linear in their first variable. Precisely, $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:H\times H\to\mathbb K$ is linear in the first entry, satisfies $\langle y,x\rangle=\overline{\langle x,y\rangle}$, and has $\langle x,x\rangle>0$ for nonzero $x$. The second entry is therefore conjugate-linear. Set $\|x\|=\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2}$. For $y\ne0$, substitute $t=\langle x,y\rangle/\|y\|^2$ into
\[0\le\|x-ty\|^2=\|x\|^2-\frac{|\langle x,y\rangle|^2}{\|y\|^2}.\]
This proves Cauchy--Schwarz, $|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\|y\|$, as in Volume~1, Chapter~9. It gives $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,y\rangle+\|y\|^2\le(\|x\|+\|y\|)^2$, so $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm. An inner product space complete for that norm is a \emph{Hilbert space}.

\portrait{schwarz}{Hermann Amandus Schwarz (1843--1921). Photograph by Louis Zipfel, about 1890, ETH Library. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

Besides $\mathbb K^n$ and $\ell^2$, with $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_nx_n\overline{y_n}$, we have $L^2(\mu)$, with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f\overline g\,d\mu$. Riesz--Fischer supplies its completeness (Volume~7, Theorem~10.4). Write $x\perp y$ when $\langle x,y\rangle=0$. Expanding the norm then gives Pythagoras: $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2$. Adding the expansions for the sum and difference gives the \emph{parallelogram law}:
\[\|x+y\|^2+\|x-y\|^2=2\|x\|^2+2\|y\|^2.\]
The diagonals and the four sides have the same total squared length. Exercise~\ref{ex:jvn} proves that this identity characterises inner-product norms. It will also make minimising sequences converge.

\section{The projection theorem}

\begin{theorem}[projection on a closed convex set]\label{thm:projection}
For a nonempty closed convex set $C$ in Hilbert space $H$, each $x\in H$ has exactly one nearest point $p=P_C(x)\in C$, with $\|x-p\|=d(x,C)$. Equivalently,
\[p\in C,\qquad\operatorname{Re}\langle x-p,c-p\rangle\le0\quad(c\in C).\]
This projection is nonexpansive: $\|P_C(x)-P_C(y)\|\le\|x-y\|$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Finding the minimiser.} Write $d=d(x,C)$ and choose $c_n\in C$ with $\|x-c_n\|^2\le d^2+1/n$. The midpoint stays in $C$, so the parallelogram identity yields
\[\|c_n-c_m\|^2=2\|x-c_n\|^2+2\|x-c_m\|^2-4\left\|x-\frac{c_n+c_m}2\right\|^2\le\frac2n+\frac2m.\]
Thus $(c_n)$ is Cauchy. Completeness gives a limit $p$, closedness puts it in $C$, and continuity of distance gives $\|x-p\|=d$. For two minimisers, the same identity has upper bound zero, so they coincide.

\emph{Recognising the minimiser.} For $c\in C$ and $0<t\le1$, convexity puts $p+t(c-p)$ in $C$. Minimality implies
\[\|x-p\|^2\le\|x-p-t(c-p)\|^2=\|x-p\|^2-2t\operatorname{Re}\langle x-p,c-p\rangle+t^2\|c-p\|^2.\]
Cancel, divide by $t$, and let $t$ decrease to zero. Conversely, the claimed inner-product inequality gives $\|x-c\|^2=\|x-p\|^2+\|p-c\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle x-p,c-p\rangle\ge\|x-p\|^2$ for every $c\in C$.

\emph{Comparing two projections.} Let $p=P_C(x)$ and $q=P_C(y)$. Apply the characterisation with $c=q$ and $c=p$, respectively. Adding its two inequalities gives
\[\|p-q\|^2\le\operatorname{Re}\langle x-y,p-q\rangle\le\|x-y\|\|p-q\|.\]
If $p=q$ the bound is immediate; otherwise divide by $\|p-q\|$.
\end{proof}

For a closed subspace $M$, the vectors $c-p$ range through $M$. Applying the inequality to $m,-m$ and, over $\C$, $im,-im$ gives $\langle x-p,m\rangle=0$. Thus $p$ is characterised by $p\in M$ and $x-p\perp M$. The \emph{orthogonal complement} $M^\perp=\{y:y\perp M\}$ is closed: it is an intersection of kernels of continuous forms.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:orthdecomp}
For closed $M$, $H=M\oplus M^\perp$. Its projection is linear and satisfies $P_M^2=P_M$, $\|P_M\|\le1$, and $\langle P_Mx,y\rangle=\langle x,P_My\rangle$. Every subspace $N$ satisfies $(N^\perp)^\perp=\overline N$; consequently $N$ is dense exactly when $N^\perp=\{0\}$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The projection gives $x=P_Mx+(x-P_Mx)$, with terms in $M$ and $M^\perp$. Their intersection is zero, since a vector orthogonal to itself has zero norm. Adding and scaling such decompositions proves linearity. Applying the projection twice fixes the first term, while Pythagoras gives its norm bound. Orthogonality gives $\langle P_Mx,y\rangle=\langle P_Mx,P_My\rangle=\langle x,P_My\rangle$. For arbitrary $N$, continuity gives $N^\perp=(\overline N)^\perp$. Certainly $\overline N\subseteq((\overline N)^\perp)^\perp$. If $x$ is in the latter space, then $x-P_{\overline N}x$ lies in $(\overline N)^\perp$ and is orthogonal to itself. It is zero, so $x\in\overline N$.
\end{proof}

\section{The Riesz representation theorem}

Cauchy--Schwarz makes $x\mapsto\langle x,y\rangle$ a bounded form of norm $\|y\|$, with equality detected by $x=y$ when $y\ne0$. Fr\'echet and Riesz proved in 1907, in the same volume of the \emph{Comptes rendus}, that this describes the entire dual.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz--Fr\'echet]\label{thm:rieszrep}
Each $\varphi\in H^*$ has a unique representing vector $y\in H$ such that $\varphi(x)=\langle x,y\rangle$ for every $x$. Its length is $\|y\|=\|\varphi\|$. The correspondence $\varphi\mapsto y$ is a conjugate-linear isometric bijection from $H^*$ to $H$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The zero form is represented by zero. Otherwise its closed kernel $M$ is proper, and Corollary~\ref{cor:orthdecomp} gives a unit vector $z\in M^\perp$. For every $x$, the vector $\varphi(x)z-\varphi(z)x$ belongs to $M$. Taking its inner product with $z$ yields $\varphi(x)=\varphi(z)\langle x,z\rangle$. Thus $y=\overline{\varphi(z)}z$ represents $\varphi$. If $y$ and $y'$ both represent it, test their difference against $x=y-y'$ to obtain $y=y'$. Cauchy--Schwarz and the test $x=y$ give the norm identity. Uniqueness and conjugate-linearity in the second entry give the stated conjugate-linear correspondence.
\end{proof}

Hilbert spaces are reflexive under the canonical embedding. To see this, write $\varphi_y(x)=\langle x,y\rangle$. For $\Phi\in H^{**}$, the map $y\mapsto\overline{\Phi(\varphi_y)}$ is a bounded linear form. Riesz gives $x$ with $\overline{\Phi(\varphi_y)}=\langle y,x\rangle$, hence $\Phi(\varphi_y)=\langle x,y\rangle=\varphi_y(x)$ for every $y$. Since these are all the forms, $\Phi=J(x)$. Bounded sequences also have weakly convergent subsequences, even when the whole space is not separable.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:weakcompact}
Every bounded sequence in a Hilbert space admits a weakly convergent subsequence. Furthermore, weak convergence $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ together with $\|x_n\|\to\|x\|$ implies norm convergence $x_n\to x$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $\|x_n\|\le M$ and let $H_0$ be their closed linear span. It is separable. Diagonal extraction gives a subsequence $(y_k)$ for which $\langle y_k,x_n\rangle$ converges at each $n$. Linearity extends convergence to the span. Approximate any $z\in H_0$ by a vector $z'$ in that span and use $|\langle y_k,z-z'\rangle|\le M\|z-z'\|$ to obtain convergence for all $z\in H_0$. The rule $z\mapsto\lim_k\langle z,y_k\rangle$ is a bounded linear form of norm at most $M$. Riesz representation produces $x\in H_0$ with $\langle y_k,z\rangle\to\langle x,z\rangle$ there. For $z\in H$, project onto $H_0$ to get
\[\langle y_k,z\rangle=\langle y_k,P_{H_0}z\rangle\longrightarrow\langle x,P_{H_0}z\rangle=\langle x,z\rangle.\]
This is weak convergence in $H$. For the second assertion, expand
\[\|x_n-x\|^2=\|x_n\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle x_n,x\rangle+\|x\|^2\longrightarrow0.\]
\end{proof}

\section{Orthonormal bases}

A family $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ is \emph{orthonormal} when $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle$ equals $1$ for $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise. For a finite orthonormal family, the projection onto its span is $\sum_k\langle x,e_k\rangle e_k$: subtracting it leaves a vector orthogonal to each $e_k$. Pythagoras gives
\[\|x\|^2=\sum_{k\le n}|\langle x,e_k\rangle|^2+\left\|x-\sum_{k\le n}\langle x,e_k\rangle e_k\right\|^2.\]
Therefore every finite sum of squared coefficients is bounded by $\|x\|^2$. Taking their supremum gives \emph{Bessel's inequality}, $\sum_i|\langle x,e_i\rangle|^2\le\|x\|^2$, even for an arbitrary index set. Only countably many coefficients can be nonzero: for each positive integer $m$, only finitely many can have absolute value at least $1/m$. We use sequences in the theorem; arbitrary orthonormal families obey the same argument with sums over their index sets.

\begin{theorem}[orthonormal bases]\label{thm:onb}
For an orthonormal sequence $(e_n)_{n\ge1}$ in Hilbert space $H$, the series $\sum_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$ converges to the projection of $x$ on their closed span. These four assertions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Their linear span is dense in $H$.
\item[(b)] A vector orthogonal to every $e_n$ is zero.
\item[(c)] Every $x\in H$ equals $\sum_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$.
\item[(d)] Every $x\in H$ satisfies Parseval's identity $\|x\|^2=\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$.
\end{enumerate}
Such a sequence is called a \emph{Hilbert basis}. Every square-summable sequence $(c_n)$ gives a convergent series $\sum_nc_ne_n$. For a Hilbert basis, the coefficient map $x\mapsto(\langle x,e_n\rangle)_n$ is therefore an isometric bijection $H\to\ell^2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $(c_n)\in\ell^2$, orthogonality gives $\|\sum_{M<n\le N}c_ne_n\|^2=\sum_{M<n\le N}|c_n|^2\to0$. Completeness sums the series. Bessel permits $c_n=\langle x,e_n\rangle$; its sum $s$ lies in the closed span $M$ and satisfies $x-s\perp M$, so $s=P_Mx$. For the equivalences, (a) makes a vector orthogonal to every $e_n$ orthogonal to itself, proving (b). Apply (b) to $x-s$ to get (c). Under (c), the finite Pythagorean sums converge to $\|x\|^2$, proving (d). Finally (d) gives $\|x-\sum_{n\le N}\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n\|^2\to0$, proving density in (a). The same norm identity proves isometry of the coefficient map; the series construction proves that it is onto.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:separable}
An infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space has a Hilbert basis and is isometrically isomorphic to $\ell^2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Start with a dense sequence $(x_k)$, skipping every vector already in the span of the previously retained vectors. Gram--Schmidt gives
\[f_1=x_{k_1},\qquad f_n=x_{k_n}-\sum_{j<n}\langle x_{k_n},e_j\rangle e_j,\qquad e_n=f_n/\|f_n\|.\]
The retained vectors are independent, so the denominators are nonzero. Their span is dense and equals the span of the orthonormal vectors. Theorem~\ref{thm:onb} identifies the space with $\ell^2$.
\end{proof}

In a nonseparable Hilbert space, Zorn's lemma supplies a maximal orthonormal family. A nonzero vector orthogonal to that family could be normalised and added, so maximality gives condition (b). Thus every Hilbert space is isometric to $\ell^2(I)$ for some index set $I$, finite or infinite. Spaces with equal Hilbert dimension have the same geometry. For a particular $L^2$ space, the useful extra information is which basis makes its functions easy to describe.

\section{Fourier series and orthogonal polynomials}

Use the normalised inner product $\langle f,g\rangle=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\overline g$ on $L^2(\mathbb T)$. The exponentials $e_n(t)=e^{int}$, indexed by $\Z$, are orthonormal, and their coefficients $\langle f,e_n\rangle=\hat f(n)$ are exactly the Fourier coefficients from Volume~5, Chapter~6.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fourierl2}
The exponentials $(e^{int})_{n\in\Z}$ form a Hilbert basis of $L^2(\mathbb T)$. Consequently every $f\in L^2(\mathbb T)$ has
\[\sum_{|n|\le N}\hat f(n)e^{int}\longrightarrow f\ \text{in }L^2,\qquad\frac1{2\pi}\int|f|^2=\sum_n|\hat f(n)|^2.\]
Conversely, each $(c_n)\in\ell^2(\Z)$ is the Fourier coefficient sequence of exactly one $f\in L^2(\mathbb T)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:onb}, density of trigonometric polynomials is enough. Approximate $f$ within $\varepsilon$ in $L^2$ by a continuous function $g$ compactly supported in $]-\pi,\pi[$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.7). Extend $g$ periodically. Trigonometric Weierstrass (Volume~7, Corollary~4.20(b)) gives $P$ with $\|g-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. The normalised $L^2$ norm is at most the supremum norm, so $\|f-P\|_2<2\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

Volume~5's Parseval identity now applies to every square-integrable function, and completeness gives its converse. For example, the coefficients $c_0=0$ and $c_n=|n|^{-0.51}$ for $n\ne0$ are square summable but not absolutely summable. They therefore belong to an $L^2$ function whose Fourier series converges absolutely nowhere, since the absolute value of each exponential term is $|c_n|$.

Polynomials provide another orthogonal basis. Applying Gram--Schmidt to $1,x,x^2,\dots$ on $[-1,1]$ gives the Legendre polynomials, with the normalisation
\[P_n(x)=\frac1{2^nn!}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^2-1)^n,\qquad P_0=1,\ P_1=x,\ P_2=\tfrac12(3x^2-1),\ P_3=\tfrac12(5x^3-3x).\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:legendre}
The polynomials $P_n$ are mutually orthogonal in $L^2([-1,1])$, with $\int_{-1}^1P_n^2=2/(2n+1)$. The normalised family $(\sqrt{n+\frac12}\,P_n)_{n\ge0}$ is a Hilbert basis.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $u=(x^2-1)^n$. It vanishes to order $n$ at both endpoints, so $u^{(k)}(\pm1)=0$ for $k<n$. Integrating by parts $n$ times gives $\int_{-1}^1u^{(n)}q=(-1)^n\int_{-1}^1u\,q^{(n)}$ with no boundary terms. This is zero for $\deg q<n$, proving orthogonality. For $q=u^{(n)}$, its $n$th derivative is $(2n)!$, and
\[\int_{-1}^1(u^{(n)})^2=(2n)!\int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)^n\,dx=(2n)!\frac{2^{2n+1}(n!)^2}{(2n+1)!}.\]
The last equality is the Beta integral after $x=2t-1$ (Volume~5, Chapter~5). Divide by $(2^nn!)^2$ to obtain the stated norm. The nonzero leading coefficient of $P_n$ makes $P_0,\dots,P_n$ span all polynomials of degree at most $n$. Weierstrass (Volume~7, Corollary~4.20(a)) and density of continuous functions in $L^2$ now give completeness.
\end{proof}

Changing the weight changes the orthogonal polynomials: Hermite uses $e^{-x^2}$ on $\R$, Laguerre uses $e^{-x}$ on $[0,\infty[$, and Chebyshev uses $(1-x^2)^{-1/2}$. Completeness for the Hermite family will use the Fourier transform in Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}. Once a family is normalised, its first $n+1$ coefficients give the least-squares polynomial approximation of degree at most $n$. This is the projection theorem applied to the polynomial subspace.

\section{Averages along orbits}

Weyl's theorem in Volume~5, Corollary~6.14, distributes the multiples of one irrational number evenly modulo $1$. For several coordinates, put $\mathbb T^d=\R^d/\Z^d$, with Lebesgue measure on $[0,1[^d$, and $e_k(x)=e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}$ for $k\in\Z^d$. These characters separate points of the torus. Stone--Weierstrass (Volume~7, Theorem~4.19(b)) makes their finite linear combinations dense in $C(\mathbb T^d)$. As in Theorem~\ref{thm:fourierl2}, they form a Hilbert basis in $L^2(\mathbb T^d)$.

\begin{theorem}[Kronecker--Weyl]\label{thm:kroneckerweyl}
Suppose $1,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_d$ are linearly independent over $\Q$, for $\alpha\in\R^d$. For every continuous $f$ on $\mathbb T^d$,
\[\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(x+n\alpha)\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb T^d}f\]
uniformly in $x$. Every orbit is dense and equidistributed: for each box $B$, its proportion of visits to $B$ tends to the volume of $B$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For nonzero $k\in\Z^d$, independence gives $k\cdot\alpha\notin\Z$. Summing a geometric progression gives
\[\left|\frac1N\sum_{n<N}e_k(x+n\alpha)\right|=\frac1N\left|\frac{e^{2\pi iNk\cdot\alpha}-1}{e^{2\pi ik\cdot\alpha}-1}\right|\le\frac2{N|e^{2\pi ik\cdot\alpha}-1|}\longrightarrow0=\int e_k.\]
The bound is independent of $x$. For $k=0$, the average and integral both equal $1$. Linearity proves the claim for trigonometric polynomials. Given continuous $f$, choose such a polynomial $P$ with $\|f-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Replacing $f$ by $P$ changes its average by at most $\varepsilon$ and its integral by at most $\varepsilon$, so the limiting uniform error is at most $2\varepsilon$. For a box, choose continuous $g\le\mathbf 1_B\le h$ with $\int(h-g)<\varepsilon$. Their averages bound the visit proportions and squeeze the limit to the box's volume. Each nonempty open set contains a positive-volume box, so each orbit is dense.
\end{proof}

Von Neumann found an averaging theorem in 1931 that needs no torus or characters. For a measure-preserving transformation, composition acts on $L^2$ by an operator of norm at most $1$. The convergence of averages follows from Hilbert-space geometry alone.

\begin{theorem}[von Neumann's mean ergodic theorem]\label{thm:meanergodic}
Let $T$ be a bounded operator on a Hilbert space with $\|T\|\le1$. Write $P$ for projection onto the fixed subspace $\ker(I-T)$. Then, for every $x$,
\[A_Nx=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^nx\longrightarrow Px\qquad\text{in norm}.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First define the \emph{adjoint}. For fixed $y$, Riesz represents the bounded linear form $x\mapsto\langle Tx,y\rangle$ by a unique vector $T^*y$, so $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^*y\rangle$. Uniqueness shows that $T^*$ is linear. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\|T^*y\|\le\|T\|\|y\|$; conversely, taking the supremum over unit $y$ in $|\langle Tx,y\rangle|$ gives $\|T\|\le\|T^*\|$. Thus the two norms agree. Conjugating the defining identity also gives $(T^*)^*=T$.

If $Tx=x$, expand
\[\begin{aligned}
\|T^*x-x\|^2&=\|T^*x\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle T^*x,x\rangle+\|x\|^2\\
&=\|T^*x\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,Tx\rangle+\|x\|^2\eqbr
\le\|x\|^2-2\|x\|^2+\|x\|^2=0.
\end{aligned}\]
Hence $T^*x=x$. Applying the same reasoning to $T^*$ proves equality of the fixed subspaces. If $R$ is the range of $I-T$, then $y\perp R$ exactly when $(I-T^*)y=0$. Corollary~\ref{cor:orthdecomp} therefore gives $H=\ker(I-T)\oplus\overline R$.

On the first summand, $A_Nx=x=Px$. For $y=(I-T)z\in R$, telescoping gives $A_Ny=(z-T^Nz)/N$, whose norm is at most $2\|z\|/N$. For $y\in\overline R$, approximate by $y'\in R$ within $\varepsilon$ and use $\|A_N\|\le1$. Then $\limsup_N\|A_Ny\|\le\varepsilon$, so the limit is zero. Combining the two summands proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

If $\tau$ preserves a probability measure, $Tf=f\circ\tau$ is an isometry on $L^2$. The theorem says that $N^{-1}\sum_{n<N}f\circ\tau^n$ converges in $L^2$ to the projection onto the invariant functions. When those functions are only the constants, we call $\tau$ \emph{ergodic}; the limit is then $\int f\,d\mu$. For rotation by $\alpha$, $Te_k=e^{2\pi ik\cdot\alpha}e_k$. The invariant functions are precisely the constants when $1,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_d$ are rationally independent. Birkhoff's pointwise theorem, proved in Volume~11, gives almost-sure convergence; we do not use that theorem here. Von Neumann found his result first and discussed it with Birkhoff, whose paper appeared in December 1931, a month before von Neumann's. The sources discuss their priority dispute.

\section{A second proof of the Radon--Nikodym theorem}

The Hahn-decomposition proof of Radon--Nikodym is already available from Volume~7, Theorem~11.4. Von Neumann's proof of 1940 instead represents a linear form in a suitable $L^2$ space. Both proofs produce the same density.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rnvn}
Suppose $\mu$ and $\nu$ are finite measures on $(E,\mathcal A)$ and $\nu\ll\mu$. A nonnegative density $h$ exists with $\nu(A)=\int_Ah\,d\mu$ for every measurable $A$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Set $\rho=\mu+\nu$. The form $f\mapsto\int f\,d\nu$ on real $L^2(\rho)$ is bounded because $|\int f\,d\nu|\le\int|f|\,d\rho\le\rho(E)^{1/2}\|f\|_{L^2(\rho)}$. Riesz supplies $g\in L^2(\rho)$ satisfying
\[\int f\,d\nu=\int fg\,d\rho\qquad(f\in L^2(\rho)).\]
Indicator tests give $0\le\int_Ag\,d\rho=\nu(A)\le\rho(A)$ for all $A$, forcing $0\le g\le1$ almost everywhere. Set $g=0$ on the measurable exceptional $\rho$-null set, so these bounds hold everywhere without changing any integral. Rearrangement gives $\int f(1-g)\,d\nu=\int fg\,d\mu$. On $B=\{g=1\}$, its indicator test gives $\mu(B)=0$, hence also $\nu(B)=0$. Now substitute the bounded function $(1+g+\cdots+g^n)\mathbf 1_A$ to obtain
\[\int_A(1-g^{n+1})\,d\nu=\int_A(g+g^2+\cdots+g^{n+1})\,d\mu.\]
Dominated convergence sends the left side to $\nu(A\setminus B)=\nu(A)$. Monotone convergence sends the right side to integration of $g/(1-g)$ off $B$, with infinite limit on $B$. Since $B$ is $\mu$-null, we may set $h=0$ there. The measurable function $h=g/(1-g)$ on $E\setminus B$, zero on $B$, has the required identity.
\end{proof}

For two $\sigma$-finite measures, choose a common countable partition into pieces of finite measure and apply this argument on each piece. The Hilbert-space method is to express a desired quantity as a bounded linear form, then let Riesz supply its representing vector. Here that vector encodes the density.

\section{Spaces with a reproducing kernel}

Point values are not defined for an $L^2$ equivalence class. Other Hilbert spaces consist of actual functions whose point values are bounded linear forms. Such a space on $X$ is called a \emph{reproducing kernel Hilbert space}, or RKHS. Riesz gives a vector $k_x$ for each evaluation: $f(x)=\langle f,k_x\rangle$. Its \emph{kernel} is $K(x,y)=k_y(x)=\langle k_y,k_x\rangle$. The kernel is Hermitian and positive semi-definite, meaning
\[\sum_{i,j}a_i\overline{a_j}K(x_j,x_i)=\left\|\sum_ia_ik_{x_i}\right\|^2\ge0\]
for every finite choice of points and scalars.

\begin{theorem}[Moore--Aronszajn]\label{thm:aronszajn}
A positive semi-definite kernel $K$ on any set $X$, with $K(x,y)=\overline{K(y,x)}$, determines exactly one RKHS of functions on $X$ having kernel $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $K_y=K(\cdot,y)$ and take their linear span $H_0$. Define
\[\left\langle\sum_ia_iK_{x_i},\sum_jb_jK_{y_j}\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j}a_i\overline{b_j}K(y_j,x_i).\]
For $f=\sum_ia_iK_{x_i}$ and $g=\sum_jb_jK_{y_j}$, the right side is both $\sum_j\overline{b_j}f(y_j)$ and $\sum_ia_i\overline{g(x_i)}$. Thus changing a representation of either function does not change the result. It is sesquilinear and nonnegative on the diagonal, and $\langle f,K_x\rangle=f(x)$. Cauchy--Schwarz follows, even before positive definiteness, by expanding $\langle f+tg,f+tg\rangle\ge0$ and minimising over $t$. Hence
\[|f(x)|\le\langle f,f\rangle^{1/2}K(x,x)^{1/2}.\]
Zero squared norm forces $f$ to vanish at every point, so this is an inner product on $H_0$.

Take its completion (Volume~7, Theorem~2.9), extending the inner product by limits along Cauchy sequences; Cauchy--Schwarz ensures these limits are well defined. For each vector $v$ in the completion define the function $f_v(x)=\langle v,K_x\rangle$. This identification is injective: if all values vanish, $v$ is orthogonal to the dense span of the $K_x$, hence to itself. Transporting the inner product to these functions gives a complete space $H$ with the reproducing property and kernel $K$. In any other RKHS with that kernel, the $K_x$ span a dense subspace, since orthogonality to all of them forces a function to vanish. The inner product on that span is already fixed by $K$, so its completion, including its point values, is unique.
\end{proof}

The Gaussian kernel $K(x,y)=e^{-|x-y|^2/2}$ on $\R^d$ has this positivity. Let $\gamma$ be standard Gaussian measure. The Gaussian transform from Volume~5, Theorem~8.3, applied coordinate by coordinate and rescaled, gives $e^{-|u|^2/2}=\int e^{i\xi\cdot u}\,d\gamma(\xi)$. Therefore
\[\sum_{i,j}a_i\overline{a_j}K(x_j,x_i)=\int\left|\sum_ia_ie^{-i\xi\cdot x_i}\right|^2\,d\gamma(\xi)\ge0.\]
This kernel is widely used in machine learning. Its RKHS consists of smooth functions, while the representer theorem in Exercise~\ref{ex:representer} reduces a regularised fitting problem to an $n\times n$ kernel matrix. Chapter~\ref{ch:spectral} will analyse such kernels through Mercer's theorem.

\begin{beauty}
The map $x\mapsto K_x$ turns a kernel value into an inner product. Data that resist separation by a line in their original coordinates can sometimes be separated after this change of coordinates; which data can be separated depends on the kernel. The construction makes that geometric question precise. A kernel on just two points already shows how its off-diagonal entry fixes an angle between two evaluation vectors.
\end{beauty}

\section{Coordinates for an operator}

Projection gives both the nearest point in a closed convex set and the decomposition $H=M\oplus M^\perp$. Riesz identifies bounded linear forms with inner products. An orthonormal basis then supplies coordinates and Parseval's identity. The exponentials and Legendre polynomials are concrete bases; von Neumann's theorem averages an orbit by projecting onto its fixed vectors. The same representation theorem constructs Radon--Nikodym densities and reproducing-kernel spaces.

An operator might have a particularly useful basis: one in which it only stretches each coordinate axis. Chapter~\ref{linear:ch:spectral} proves this for symmetric matrices. Part~II will follow these preferred axes from matrices to graphs and then to operators on a Hilbert space.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:jvn}
\emph{Jordan--von Neumann.} Suppose a real normed space satisfies the parallelogram law. Prove that $\langle x,y\rangle=(\|x+y\|^2-\|x-y\|^2)/4$ is an inner product inducing the original norm. Deduce that the usual $L^p$ norm can be an inner-product norm only for $p=2$, provided the measure space has two disjoint sets of positive finite measure.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:legendrefit}
Among polynomials of degree at most $2$, find the one closest to $|x|$ in $L^2([-1,1])$. Compute the minimum squared error $\int_{-1}^1(|x|-p(x))^2\,dx$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:noclosest}
\emph{A closed subspace with no nearest point.} In supremum-normed $C([0,1])$, put $\varphi(f)=\int_0^{1/2}f-\int_{1/2}^1f$ and $M=\ker\varphi$. (a) Prove $\|\varphi\|=1$ but $|\varphi(f)|<\|f\|$ for every nonzero $f$. (b) For any normed $E$ and nonzero $\psi\in E^*$, prove $d(x,\ker\psi)=|\psi(x)|/\|\psi\|$. (c) Show that no $g\notin M$ has a nearest point in $M$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:vandercorput}
\emph{Van der Corput's inequality.} (a) Extend complex numbers $u_1,\dots,u_N$ by zero to all other integer indices. For $1\le H\le N$, prove
\[\left|\sum_nu_n\right|^2\le\frac{N+H-1}{H^2}\sum_{|r|<H}(H-|r|)\gamma(r),\qquad\gamma(r)=\sum_mu_{m+r}\overline{u_m}.\]
(b) Show that $(x_n)$ is equidistributed modulo $1$ if every difference sequence $(x_{n+r}-x_n)_n$, for integer $r\ge1$, is equidistributed. (c) Prove equidistribution of $n^2\alpha$ for irrational $\alpha$, then of $P(n)$ for every real polynomial of positive degree with irrational leading coefficient (Weyl, 1916).
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:hardy2}
\emph{Hardy's space.} Let $H^2$ consist of the series $f(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nz^n$ with $\|f\|^2=\sum_n|a_n|^2<\infty$. (a) Prove holomorphy on the unit disc and $|f(w)|\le\|f\|(1-|w|^2)^{-1/2}$. (b) Identify its reproducing kernel as $K(z,w)=1/(1-z\overline w)$, Szeg\H o's kernel. (c) Prove the boundary norm formula $\|f\|^2=\lim_{r\to1}(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{it})|^2\,dt$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:representer}
\emph{The representer theorem.} In a real RKHS with kernel $K$, take data $(x_i,y_i)\in X\times\R$, $i\le n$, and $\lambda>0$. Prove that $J(f)=\sum_i(f(x_i)-y_i)^2+\lambda\|f\|^2$ has exactly one minimiser, lying in the span of the $K_{x_i}$. Show that $f=\sum_ia_iK_{x_i}$ with $a=(G+\lambda I)^{-1}y$ and $G=(K(x_i,x_j))_{i,j}$. This is kernel ridge regression.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c02a}
For $u=(1,2)$ and $x=(3,1)$ in Euclidean $\R^2$, find the projection of $x$ onto $\R u$ and its orthogonal remainder. Write the projection matrix, verify its square equals itself, and check Pythagoras numerically using exact integers.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c02b}
Let $x\in\ell^2$ have coordinates $x_n=2^{-n/2}$ for $n\ge1$, and let $s_N$ keep its first $N$ coordinates and replace the rest by zero. Compute $\|x\|_2$ and $\|x-s_N\|_2$. Find the smallest $N$ for which this error is strictly less than $1/16$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c02c}
On $\R^2$, compare $T(a,b)=(-b,a)$ and $U(a,b)=(a,0)$. Compute the averages $N^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}T^nx$ by separating $N$ into complete blocks of four and a remainder. Prove their norms are at most $2\|x\|/N$. Compute the corresponding averages for $U$ and identify the two limiting projections.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c02d}
On $X=\{a,b\}$, let $K(a,a)=K(b,b)=1$ and $K(a,b)=K(b,a)=1/2$. Prove positivity of this kernel. For a real function with values $f(a)=u$ and $f(b)=v$, compute its RKHS norm. Find the function of smallest norm subject to $f(a)=1$ and explain the role of $K_a$.
\end{exercise}

\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:hardy2}

\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:jvn}
\idea Polarisation gives the only possible inner product. The parallelogram law supplies additivity; continuity then extends rational homogeneity to real homogeneity.
\writeup Symmetry and $\langle x,x\rangle=\|x\|^2$ follow directly. Applying the parallelogram law to $x+y,x'$ and $x-y,x'$ and subtracting gives
\[\langle x+x',y\rangle+\langle x-x',y\rangle=2\langle x,y\rangle.\]
Set $x'=x$ to obtain $\langle2x,y\rangle=2\langle x,y\rangle$. Next put $x=(a+b)/2$ and $x'=(a-b)/2$. The displayed identity becomes $\langle a,y\rangle+\langle b,y\rangle=\langle a+b,y\rangle$. Additivity gives homogeneity for rational scalars. The defining norm formula is continuous, so taking rational limits gives real homogeneity. Positive definiteness follows from the original norm. For disjoint $A,B$ of positive finite measures, normalise their indicators to have norm $1$. The two sums and differences have squared norm $2^{2/p}$, whereas the parallelogram law would require $2\cdot2^{2/p}=4$. Thus $p=2$. For $p=\infty$, both sum and difference have norm $1$, so the law fails as well.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:legendrefit}
\idea Projection onto orthogonal polynomials computes the coefficients independently. The even target has zero coefficient on the odd polynomial.
\writeup The projection theorem gives $p=\sum_{n=0}^2c_nP_n$ with $c_n=(2n+1)\int_{-1}^1|x|P_n/2$. Parity gives $c_1=0$, while $c_0=1/2$ and
\[c_2=\frac52\cdot2\int_0^1x\frac{3x^2-1}2\,dx=\frac52\left(\frac34-\frac12\right)=\frac58.\]
Therefore $p(x)=1/2+(5/8)P_2(x)=3/16+(15/16)x^2$. Pythagoras gives the minimum squared error
\[\frac23-\left(\frac12\right)^2 2-\left(\frac58\right)^2\frac25=\frac23-\frac12-\frac5{32}=\frac1{96}.\]
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:noclosest}
\idea The form nearly reaches its norm on increasingly sharp transitions. Attaining that norm would require a continuous function to take two incompatible values at the midpoint.
\writeup (a) Integration gives $|\varphi(f)|\le\int_0^1|f|\le\|f\|_\infty$. For $n\ge2$, take $f_n$ equal to $1$ up to $1/2-1/n$, equal to $-1$ from $1/2+1/n$, and linear between. Then $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ and $\varphi(f_n)=1-1/n$, proving the norm is $1$. For real $f$ with norm $1$, equality $\varphi(f)=1$ would give
\[\int_0^{1/2}(1-f)+\int_{1/2}^1(1+f)=0.\]
The continuous nonnegative integrands must vanish. This forces $f(1/2)=1$ and $f(1/2)=-1$. Changing sign handles equality at $-1$. For complex $f$, first multiply by a unit scalar to make $\varphi(f)$ nonnegative real, then apply the real argument to $\operatorname{Re}f$, whose norm is no larger. Scaling proves strict inequality for every nonzero $f$.

(b) For $m\in\ker\psi$, $|\psi(x)|\le\|\psi\|\|x-m\|$, giving the lower bound. Given $0<\varepsilon<\|\psi\|$, choose unit $u$ with $|\psi(u)|>\|\psi\|-\varepsilon$. The vector $m=x-\psi(x)u/\psi(u)$ belongs to the kernel, and $\|x-m\|=|\psi(x)|/|\psi(u)|$. Let $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero. (c) A nearest $m\in M$ to $g\notin M$ would satisfy $\|g-m\|=|\varphi(g)|=|\varphi(g-m)|$, contradicting (a).
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:vandercorput}
\idea Squaring an average replaces its terms by correlations. Correlations of exponential polynomial sequences involve differences, whose degrees are smaller.
\writeup (a) Every $u_m$ occurs $H$ times in $\sum_n\sum_{0\le h<H}u_{n+h}$. The inner sum can be nonzero only for $1-H<n\le N$, at most $N+H-1$ indices. Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[H^2\left|\sum_nu_n\right|^2\le(N+H-1)\sum_n\left|\sum_{h<H}u_{n+h}\right|^2=(N+H-1)\sum_{h,h'<H}\sum_nu_{n+h}\overline{u_{n+h'}}.\]
The inner correlation is $\gamma(h-h')$. Each integer $r$ with $|r|<H$ occurs $H-|r|$ times, proving the formula. (b) Fix nonzero integer $k$ and set $u_n=e^{2\pi ikx_n}$ for $1\le n\le N$. Then $\gamma(0)=N$, $\gamma(-r)=\overline{\gamma(r)}$, and for $r\ge1$,
\[\gamma(r)=\sum_{m=1}^{N-r}e^{2\pi ik(x_{m+r}-x_m)}.\]
Discarding weights by $H-r\le H$ in (a) gives
\[\left|\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}u_n\right|^2\le\frac{N+H-1}{NH}+\frac{2(N+H-1)}{NH}\sum_{r=1}^{H-1}\frac{|\gamma(r)|}N.\]
For fixed $H$, the hypothesis and Weyl's criterion (Volume~5, Theorem~6.13) send every $\gamma(r)/N$ to zero. The limiting upper bound is $1/H$. Letting $H$ grow proves that each nontrivial exponential average tends to zero, which is equidistribution by the same criterion. (c) A degree-one polynomial with irrational slope is covered by Volume~5, Corollary~6.14, including its constant term as a fixed phase in the exponential average. If $P$ has degree $d\ge2$ and irrational leading coefficient $\alpha_d$, then $P(n+r)-P(n)$ has degree $d-1$ and irrational leading coefficient $rd\alpha_d$ for every $r\ge1$. Induction and (b) conclude. For $P(n)=\alpha n^2$, these differences are $2r\alpha n+r^2\alpha$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:hardy2}
\idea Coefficients identify $H^2$ with $\ell^2$. A geometric sequence represents evaluation inside the disc.
\writeup (a) For $|w|<1$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_n|a_n||w|^n\le\|f\|(\sum_n|w|^{2n})^{1/2}=\|f\|(1-|w|^2)^{-1/2}$. The bound is uniform on every smaller closed disc. Thus the series converges normally there and is holomorphic by Volume~5, Theorem~9.4. (b) The coefficient map is an isometry onto $\ell^2$, so $H^2$ is complete. The vector $k_w(z)=\sum_n\overline w^nz^n$ belongs to $H^2$ and satisfies $f(w)=\langle f,k_w\rangle$. Its kernel is the geometric sum $K(z,w)=k_w(z)=1/(1-z\overline w)$. (c) At radius $r<1$, uniform convergence identifies the Fourier coefficients of $f(re^{it})$ as $a_nr^n$. Parseval gives $(2\pi)^{-1}\int|f(re^{it})|^2\,dt=\sum_n|a_n|^2r^{2n}$. Monotone convergence as $r\uparrow1$ gives the required limit.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:representer}
\idea The data see only the component in the span of their evaluation vectors. An orthogonal component adds a norm penalty without changing a fitted value.
\writeup The finite-dimensional subspace $V=\operatorname{span}(K_{x_i})$ is closed. Write $f=v+w$ with $v\in V$ and $w\perp V$. Evaluation gives $f(x_i)=v(x_i)$, and Pythagoras gives $J(f)=J(v)+\lambda\|w\|^2$. Thus every minimiser lies in $V$. There, continuity and $J(v)\ge\lambda\|v\|^2$ reduce minimisation to a large closed ball, which is compact. Strict convexity of the squared Hilbert norm, together with $\lambda>0$, gives uniqueness. For $f=\sum_ia_iK_{x_i}$, the objective becomes $|Ga-y|^2+\lambda a^TGa$. Its gradient is $2G((G+\lambda I)a-y)$. Positivity of $G$ makes $G+\lambda I$ invertible, and $a=(G+\lambda I)^{-1}y$ makes the gradient vanish. Convexity proves that this is the minimising function, even if its kernel-section representation is not unique.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c02a}
\idea Divide the inner product with $u$ by its squared length. The residual then has zero inner product with the line.
\writeup Since $\langle x,u\rangle=5$ and $\|u\|^2=5$, the projection is $u=(1,2)$ and the residual is $(2,-1)$. Their dot product is $2-2=0$. The projection matrix is $P=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying the numerator matrix by itself gives five times that matrix, so $P^2=P$. Finally $\|x\|^2=10$, $\|Px\|^2=5$ and $\|x-Px\|^2=5$, giving Pythagoras exactly.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c02b}
\idea Orthogonality turns the error into the square root of a geometric tail.
\writeup The squared norm is $\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}=1$. The omitted squared norm is $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}=2^{-N}$, so $\|x-s_N\|_2=2^{-N/2}$. Strict inequality $2^{-N/2}<2^{-4}$ is equivalent to $N>8$, making $N=9$ the smallest choice.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c02c}
\idea Full periods cancel for the rotation; after the first term, the projection contributes the same vector every time.
\writeup Write $N=4q+r$ with $0\le r<4$. The rotation's complete blocks sum to zero. Its remainder sums, for $r=0,1,2,3$, are respectively $0$, $x$, $x+Tx$, and $-T^3x$. Their norms are $0$, $\|x\|$, $\sqrt2\|x\|$, and $\|x\|$, since $x\perp Tx$. Dividing by $N$ proves the stated bound and convergence to zero. The only fixed vector of this rotation is zero. For $U$, $U^0=I$ and $U^n=U$ for $n\ge1$, so the average of $x=(a,b)$ is $(a,b/N)$. Its limit $(a,0)$ is the orthogonal projection onto the fixed line of $U$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c02d}
\idea The Gram matrix converts function values into coefficients of kernel sections. Minimisation then becomes completing a square.
\writeup For real coefficients $\alpha,\beta$, the kernel quadratic form is $\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta/2)^2+3\beta^2/4$, positive except at zero. The Hermitian complex version is $|\alpha+\beta/2|^2+3|\beta|^2/4$, proving kernel positivity over either field. Its invertible Gram matrix gives a unique expansion $f=\alpha K_a+\beta K_b$ for any values $(u,v)$, with
\[\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}=\frac43\begin{pmatrix}1&-1/2\\-1/2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}.\]
The reproducing inner product gives $\|f\|^2=\alpha u+\beta v=\frac43(u^2-uv+v^2)$. For $u=1$, this is $1+\frac43(v-1/2)^2$. Its minimum is $1$, attained at $v=1/2$, exactly the function $K_a$.
\end{solution}
