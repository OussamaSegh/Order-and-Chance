\chapter{The Values of Zeta}\label{ch:harder}

\begin{center}\itshape
The creation of the theory of almost periodic functions of a real variable was a performance of extraordinary power.\\
\upshape --- E.~C.~Titchmarsh, obituary of Harald Bohr, \emph{Journal of the London Mathematical Society} (1953)
\end{center}
Harald Bohr played on Denmark's silver-medal football team at the 1908 London Olympics. He was also studying mathematics in Copenhagen; accounts of his thesis defence describe football supporters competing with mathematicians for seats. His mathematical interest was Dirichlet series. In 1911 he asked which values $\zeta(\sigma+it)$ can take when $\sigma>1$ is fixed and $t$ varies.

Absolute convergence keeps the values bounded, but does not describe their shape. The Euler product does:
\[\zeta(\sigma+it)=\prod_p(1-p^{-\sigma}p^{-it})^{-1}.\]
Unique factorisation makes the numbers $\log p$ linearly independent over $\Q$. Kronecker's theorem therefore lets finitely many phases $p^{-it}$ approach independently chosen points of the unit circle. After controlling the tail, the closure of the value set becomes the image of a product with one free phase per prime. Bohr developed this into power series in infinitely many variables in 1913 and into almost periodic functions in the 1920s.

A sharper question asks when the real part stays positive along the entire line. For $\sigma\ge2$, the disc $|\zeta(\sigma+it)-1|\le\zeta(\sigma)-1<1$ already answers yes. Van de Lune located the exact threshold in 1983:
\[\sigma_0=1.19234\ldots,\qquad\sum_p\arcsin(p^{-\sigma_0})=\pi/2.
\]
For $\sigma>1$, positivity holds for every $t$ exactly when $\sigma\ge\sigma_0$. Problem~\ref{pb:vandelune} proves both sides and the boundary case. Volume~15, Chapter~11, studies the limiting distribution for fixed $\sigma>1/2$; that later result is not a premise here.

These problems combine Hilbert geometry from Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert}, Plancherel from Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}, and Volume~7's measure theory. Nearly orthogonal vectors lead to large-value bounds; kernels solve evaluation problems; arithmetic functions become vectors in $L^2$. Bohr's value sets then connect phase geometry with zeta, and the final problems use projections and Hermite expansions for random variables.

\begin{think}
Choose $N$ lines through the origin in the plane and represent them by unit vectors. For three lines the largest absolute inner product can be $1/2$. For four, can it be less than $1/\sqrt2$? Sorting the line angles modulo $\pi$ should help. Then ask how many equally angled lines fit in $\R^3$.
\end{think}

Our complex inner product is linear in its first argument. Write $e(t)=e^{2\pi it}$, let $\|t\|$ denote distance to the nearest integer, and set $B(x)=\{x\}-1/2$. For an arithmetic function, write $M(f)=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{n\le N}f(n)$ when the limit exists. We repeatedly use the geometric-sum bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dirichletkernel}
\left|\sum_{n=1}^Ne(n\theta)\right|
 =\left|\frac{\sin\pi N\theta}{\sin\pi\theta}\right|
 \le\min\left(N,\frac1{2\|\theta\|}\right)\qquad(\theta\notin\Z).
\end{equation}
Concavity on $[0,\pi/2]$ gives $|\sin\pi\theta|\ge2\|\theta\|$, proving the second bound; the first is the triangle inequality.

\section{Almost orthogonal vectors}

Bessel's inequality bounds the squared coefficients of a vector against an orthonormal family by its squared norm. Exponentials at noninteger frequencies, Dirichlet polynomials and sampled characters usually lose exact orthogonality. Their Gram matrices measure how much the bound must change.

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:selberg}
\emph{A Gram-matrix bound.} Take nonzero $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_R\in H$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove Selberg's inequality
\[\sum_{r=1}^R|\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle|^2\le\|f\|^2\max_r\sum_{s=1}^R|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|\qquad(f\in H).\]
\item Suppose real $\alpha_r$ satisfy $\|\alpha_r-\alpha_s\|\ge\delta>0$ for $r\ne s$. For $S(\alpha)=\sum_{n=1}^Na_ne(n\alpha)$, deduce
\[\sum_r|S(\alpha_r)|^2\le\left(N+\frac{1+\log R}{\delta}\right)\sum_n|a_n|^2.\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:welch}
\emph{How close to orthogonal?} Let $u_1,\ldots,u_R$ be unit vectors in $\C^n$, with $R>n$, and put $\varepsilon=\max_{r\ne s}|\langle u_r,u_s\rangle|$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Establish $\varepsilon^2\ge(R-n)/(n(R-1))$.
\item Rearrange this as $R\le n(1-\varepsilon^2)/(1-n\varepsilon^2)$ when $n\varepsilon^2<1$.
\item Verify equality in both bounds for planar unit vectors at angles $0,2\pi/3,4\pi/3$ and for six unit directions through opposite vertices of a regular icosahedron.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:gerzon}
\emph{Counting equiangular lines.} Unit vectors $u_i\in\R^n$ represent equiangular lines when $|\langle u_i,u_j\rangle|=\alpha$ for $i\ne j$, with $0\le\alpha<1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute the Gram matrix of $P_i=u_iu_i^{\mathsf T}$ in the real symmetric matrices with inner product $\operatorname{tr}(AB)$.
\item Infer $R\le n(n+1)/2$.
\item Give equality examples in dimensions two and three.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:halasz}
\emph{Counting large character sums.} Take $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_R\in H$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove Halász's inequality
\[\left(\sum_r|\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle|\right)^2\le\|f\|^2\sum_{r,s}|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|.\]
\item Let $q$ be prime, $1\le N<q$, $A=\sum_{n\le N}|a_n|^2$, and let $X$ contain $R$ Dirichlet characters modulo $q$. Suppose $|\sum_{n\le N}a_n\chi(n)|\ge V>0$ for every $\chi\in X$. Show
\[R\le\frac{2AN}{V^2}\quad\text{if}\quad V^2\ge2A\sqrt q(1+\log q).\]
\item Sum over all characters to establish $\sum_\chi|\sum_{n\le N}a_n\chi(n)|^2=(q-1)A$. Compare its large-value bound with (b) for $N\ll q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:ingham}
\emph{Separated frequencies.} Suppose $\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_N$ have gaps at least $\gamma>0$, and $f(t)=\sum_na_ne^{i\lambda_nt}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $K(\lambda)=\int k(t)e^{i\lambda t}\dd t$ for $k(t)=\cos(\pi t/(2T))$ on $[-T,T]$, zero elsewhere.
\item For $T>\pi/\gamma$, prove
\[\int_{-T}^T|f|^2\dd t\ge\frac{4T}{\pi}\left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{\gamma^2T^2}\right)\sum_n|a_n|^2.\]
\item For $T\ge\pi/(2\gamma)$, prove
\[\int_{-T}^T|f|^2\dd t\le\frac{8\sqrt2T}{\pi}\left(1+\frac{\pi^2}{4\gamma^2T^2}\right)\sum_n|a_n|^2.\]
\item If $\lambda_n=n+\delta_n$ for $n\in\Z$, with $|\delta_n|\le1/4$, show that for every $T>2\pi$ there are constants $0<c\le C$, depending only on $T$, such that
\[c\sum_n|a_n|^2\le\int_{T_0-T}^{T_0+T}|f|^2\dd t\le C\sum_n|a_n|^2\]
for every real $T_0$ and finite combination of these exponentials.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Journey]\label{pb:mvt}
\emph{A mean-value bound without a logarithm.} Let real $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N$ be separated by $\Delta>0$ and put $F(t)=\sum_na_ne(\lambda_nt)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For $k(t)=\Delta(\sin(\pi\Delta t)/(\pi\Delta t))^2$, verify $\int k=1$ and $\hat k(\xi)=(1-|\xi|/\Delta)_+$.
\item Given $T_0\in\R$ and $T,U>0$, put $w=\mathbf1_{[T_0-U,T_0+T+U]}*k$. Prove $\int w|F|^2=(T+2U)\sum|a_n|^2$ and $w\ge1-2/(\pi^2\Delta U)$ on $[T_0,T_0+T]$. For $\pi^2\Delta U>2$, conclude
\[\int_{T_0}^{T_0+T}|F|^2\dd t\le\frac{T+2U}{1-2/(\pi^2\Delta U)}\sum_n|a_n|^2.\]
\item For $N\ge2$ and $T\ge16N$, deduce
\[\int_{T_0}^{T_0+T}\left|\sum_{n\le N}a_nn^{-it}\right|^2\dd t\le(T+5\sqrt{TN})\sum_{n\le N}|a_n|^2.\]
\item Explain why no smaller leading coefficient than one can multiply $T\sum|a_n|^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\section{Spaces with a kernel}

Continuous point evaluation in a Hilbert space has a representing vector: $f(x)=\langle f,K_x\rangle$. Consequently $|f(x)|\le\|f\|\sqrt{K(x,x)}$. When $K_x\ne0$, multiples of $K_x$ attain the bound and are its only nonzero extremisers. Computing the kernel therefore solves an evaluation problem at the same time.

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:christoffel}
\emph{Extremal polynomials.} On real polynomials $\Pi_n$ of degree at most $n$, use $\langle p,q\rangle=\int_{-1}^1pq$. Let $P_k$ be Legendre's polynomials with $P_k(1)=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find the kernel $K_n(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^n(k+1/2)P_k(x)P_k(y)$.
\item Prove $p(1)^2\le((n+1)^2/2)\int_{-1}^1p^2$, with equality exactly for multiples of $K_n(\cdot,1)$.
\item For $n=2$, give this polynomial explicitly and check its ratio. Find also the optimal constant in $p(0)^2\le c\int_{-1}^1p^2$ on $\Pi_2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:bergman}
\emph{Square-integrable holomorphic functions.} Let $A^2$ consist of holomorphic functions on the open unit disc $\mathbb D$ with norm squared $\int_{\mathbb D}|f|^2\dd A$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove $|f(z)|^2\le\|f\|^2/(\pi(1-|z|)^2)$ and use it to show that $A^2$ is closed in $L^2(\mathbb D)$, with continuous point evaluations.
\item For $f(z)=\sum_na_nz^n$, establish $\|f\|^2=\pi\sum_n|a_n|^2/(n+1)$ and deduce $K(z,w)=1/(\pi(1-z\bar w)^2)$.
\item Obtain the sharp inequalities $|f(z)|\le\|f\|/(\sqrt\pi(1-|z|^2))$ and $|f'(0)|\le\sqrt{2/\pi}\|f\|$.
\item If $f$ is holomorphic and injective on $\mathbb D$, with $f(0)=0,f'(0)=1$, prove $\operatorname{area}f(\mathbb D)\ge\pi$, with equality only for $f(z)=z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:fock}
\emph{The Gaussian-weighted entire space.} Let $\mathcal F$ be the entire functions with $\|f\|^2=\pi^{-1}\int_\C|f(z)|^2e^{-|z|^2}\dd A(z)<\infty$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For an entire series $f(z)=\sum_na_nz^n$, prove $\|f\|^2=\sum_nn!|a_n|^2$, allowing infinity on both sides.
\item Compute the kernel $e^{z\bar w}$ and prove $|f(z)|\le\|f\|e^{|z|^2/2}$, attaining equality at any prescribed $z$.
\item Show $e^{az^2}\in\mathcal F$ exactly when $|a|<1/2$, and then $\|e^{az^2}\|^2=(1-4|a|^2)^{-1/2}$.
\item For polynomials, verify $\langle f',g\rangle=\langle f,zg\rangle$ and $(zf)'-zf'=f$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:sobolev}
\emph{Best evaluation constants on an interval.} Let $L>0$ and use the real space $H^1(]0,L[)$ with norm squared $\int_0^L(u^2+(u')^2)$. Chapter~\ref{ch:distributions} supplies its continuous representatives on $[0,L]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For $0\le y\le L$, prove that
\[K_y(x)=\frac{\cosh(\min(x,y))\cosh(L-\max(x,y))}{\sinh L}\]
belongs to $H$ and satisfies $\langle u,K_y\rangle=u(y)$ for every $u\in H$.
\item Infer the sharp inequality $\sup_{[0,L]}|u|^2\le\coth L\,\|u\|^2$ and classify equality.
\item Prove the sharp midpoint bound $u(L/2)^2\le\tfrac12\coth(L/2)\|u\|^2$. Take $L\to\infty$ and compare with Exercise~\ref{ex:gagliardo}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:dirichlet}
\emph{Square-summable Dirichlet coefficients.} Let $\mathcal H$ consist of series $F(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_nn^{-s}$ with norm squared $\sum_n|a_n|^2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show absolute convergence for $\Re s>1/2$, prove $|F(s)|^2\le\zeta(2\sigma)\|F\|^2$, and identify this Hilbert space's kernel as $K(s,w)=\zeta(s+\bar w)$.
\item Prove sharpness at every evaluation point and uniqueness of the coefficients: vanishing on the whole half-plane forces $F=0$.
\item For $a,b>1/2$, establish membership of $\zeta(s+a)\zeta(s+b)$ in $\mathcal H$ and the norm formula
\[\|\zeta(s+a)\zeta(s+b)\|^2=\frac{\zeta(2a)\zeta(2b)\zeta(a+b)^2}{\zeta(2a+2b)}.\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\section{Arithmetic in \texorpdfstring{$L^2$}{L2}}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:franel}
\emph{Dilates of the sawtooth.} Use $B(x)=\{x\}-1/2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove Franel's formula $\int_0^1B(mx)B(nx)\dd x=\gcd(m,n)^2/(12mn)$ for all positive integers $m,n$.
\item Show that $(\gcd(m,n)^2/(mn))_{m,n\le N}$ is positive definite.
\item Establish $\int_0^1(\sum_{n\le N}B(nx))^2\dd x=N/4+O(\log^2N)$. Compare with the diagonal contribution $N/12$. You may use $\sum_{k\ge1}H_k/k^2=2\zeta(3)$ after supplying a proof, where $H_k=\sum_{j\le k}1/j$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:ramanujan}
\emph{An orthogonal arithmetic expansion.} Define $c_q(n)=\sum_{1\le a\le q,\,(a,q)=1}e(an/q)$. Möbius inversion gives $c_q(n)=\sum_{d\mid(q,n)}\mu(q/d)d$; finite character sums give $N^{-1}\sum_{n\le N}c_q(n)c_r(n)=\varphi(q)\mathbf1_{q=r}$ whenever $q,r\mid N$. Justify these identities in your argument; they are also the subject of Problem Book~I, Problem~2.3.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For every $n\ge1$, prove the absolutely convergent identity $\sigma(n)/n=\zeta(2)\sum_{q\ge1}c_q(n)/q^2$.
\item Establish $M(c_qc_r)=\varphi(q)\mathbf1_{q=r}$ and the uniform bound
\[\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}|c_q(n)c_r(n)|\le\sigma_{1/2}(q)\sigma_{1/2}(r),\qquad \sigma_{1/2}(q)=\sum_{d\mid q}\sqrt d.\]
\item Prove Parseval for the expansion and conclude $N^{-1}\sum_{n\le N}(\sigma(n)/n)^2\to\tfrac52\zeta(3)$. Its value is about $3.00514$; numerical averages are illustrations, not a substitute for the limit proof.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:zetamellin}
\emph{An exact weighted zeta mean.} For measurable $g$ on $]0,\infty[$, write $\mathcal Mg(s)=\int_0^\infty g(u)u^{s-1}\dd u$ when integrable.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If both $\int|g(u)|u^{\sigma-1}\dd u$ and $\int|g(u)|^2u^{2\sigma-1}\dd u$ are finite, prove Mellin Plancherel:
\[\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R|\mathcal Mg(\sigma+it)|^2\dd t=\int_0^\infty|g(u)|^2u^{2\sigma-1}\dd u.\]
\item Establish $\zeta(s)/s=-\int_0^\infty\{1/u\}u^{s-1}\dd u$ for $0<\Re s<1$.
\item Deduce $(2\pi)^{-1}\int_\R|\zeta(1/2+it)|^2/(1/4+t^2)\dd t=\log(2\pi)-\gamma$, about $1.2606$.
\item Conclude $\int_{-T}^T|\zeta(1/2+it)|^2\dd t\le2\pi(\log(2\pi)-\gamma)(T^2+1/4)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:nyman}
\emph{One direction of Nyman--Beurling.} For $0<\theta\le1$, let $\rho_\theta(x)=\{\theta/x\}-\theta\{1/x\}$ on $]0,1[$, and let $\mathcal N$ be their closed linear span in $L^2(]0,1[)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Extend the same formula to $x>1$, show it vanishes there, and prove $\int_0^1\rho_\theta(x)x^{s-1}\dd x=-(\zeta(s)/s)(\theta^s-\theta)$ for $0<\Re s<1$.
\item If $\zeta(s_0)=0$ and $1/2<\sigma_0=\Re s_0<1$, show $\|\mathbf1-f\|_2\ge\sqrt{2\sigma_0-1}/|s_0|$ for every $f\in\mathcal N$.
\item Infer that $\mathbf1\in\mathcal N$ would exclude all zeta zeros with real part above $1/2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\section{Kronecker and Bohr}

Fix $\sigma>1$. For one unit complex number $z_p$ per prime, set $Z_\sigma(z)=\prod_p(1-p^{-\sigma}z_p)^{-1}$ on $\mathbb T^{\mathcal P}$. The zeta values correspond to the choices $z_p=p^{-it}$.

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:bohrvalues}
\emph{The closed value set.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove convergence of $Z_\sigma$ for every phase sequence, its continuity on the compact product $\mathbb T^{\mathcal P}$, and compactness of its image $E_\sigma$.
\item Given distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_k$, phases $w_j\in\mathbb T$, $\varepsilon>0$ and $t_0\in\R$, find $t>t_0$ with $|p_j^{-it}-w_j|<\varepsilon$ for every $j$.
\item Show that $E_\sigma$ is exactly the closure of $\{\zeta(\sigma+it):t\in\R\}$.
\item Obtain $\sup_t|\zeta(\sigma+it)|=\zeta(\sigma)$ and $\inf_t|\zeta(\sigma+it)|=\zeta(2\sigma)/\zeta(\sigma)$. Evaluate these at $\sigma=2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:almostperiodic}
\emph{Returns with bounded gaps.} A subset of $\R$ is \emph{relatively dense} if some $L>0$ makes every interval of length $L$ meet it.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For arbitrary $\alpha\in\R^k$ and $\eta>0$, prove relative density of $\{\tau:\|\tau\alpha_j\|<\eta\text{ for every }j\}$. No rational-independence assumption is available.
\item Given $\sigma_1>1$ and $\varepsilon>0$, show that the $\tau$ satisfying $|\zeta(s+i\tau)-\zeta(s)|<\varepsilon$ simultaneously for every $\Re s\ge\sigma_1$ form a relatively dense set.
\item Infer that $|\zeta(\sigma+it)|$, for fixed $\sigma>1$, returns within $\varepsilon$ of $\zeta(\sigma)$ with bounded gaps, but has no limit at positive infinity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[Journey]\label{pb:vandelune}
\emph{The real-part threshold.} Put $A(\sigma)=\sum_p\arcsin(p^{-\sigma})$ for $\sigma>1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For $0<r<1$, show that $(1-rz)^{-1}$ traces a circle in the right half-plane as $z\in\mathbb T$ varies, with arguments filling $[-\arcsin r,\arcsin r]$.
\item Prove that $A$ is continuous and strictly decreasing, diverges as $\sigma\downarrow1$, and tends to zero as $\sigma\to\infty$. Denote its unique solution of $A(\sigma_0)=\pi/2$ by $\sigma_0$.
\item Show $\Re\zeta(\sigma+it)>0$ for every $t$ when $\sigma>\sigma_0$. Settle the boundary $\sigma=\sigma_0$ too, using the prime number theorem to exclude simultaneous extreme arguments at every prime.
\item For $1<\sigma<\sigma_0$, prove that some $t$ has $\Re\zeta(\sigma+it)<0$.
\item The numerical value is $\sigma_0=1.19234\ldots$. Explain why direct evaluation of this prime sum is much slower than evaluation of $\zeta(\sigma_0)$, and describe a convergent acceleration.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\section{Hilbert spaces of chance}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:khinchinrec}
\emph{Recurrence above the independent benchmark.} Let $T$ preserve a probability measure $\mu$, and let $A$ be measurable with $\mu(A)=a>0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $N^{-1}\sum_{n<N}\mu(A\cap T^{-n}A)$ converges to a value at least $a^2$.
\item For each $\varepsilon>0$, prove that $\{n:\mu(A\cap T^{-n}A)>a^2-\varepsilon\}$ has lower density at least $\varepsilon/(a-a^2+\varepsilon)$.
\item For irrational circle rotation by $\alpha$ and $A=[0,a[$, $a\le1/2$, compute the correlations and their average limit. Give another system with a limit strictly larger than $a^2$ when $0<a<1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:norate}
\emph{Arbitrarily slow ergodic averages.} For any positive non-increasing sequence $a_N\to0$, construct a Hilbert space, a unitary $U$ and a vector $x$ orthogonal to the fixed vectors such that
\[\left\|\frac1N\sum_{n<N}U^nx\right\|\ge a_N\qquad(N\ge1).\]
Thus the fixed-space projection $P$ satisfies $Px=0$, but convergence can be as slow as the prescribed sequence.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:wiener}
\emph{Detecting atoms by Fourier energy.} For a finite Borel measure $\mu$ on $\R/\Z$, write $\hat\mu(n)=\int e(-nx)\dd\mu(x)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove $(2N+1)^{-1}\sum_{|n|\le N}|\hat\mu(n)|^2\to\sum_x\mu(\{x\})^2$.
\item Apply this to the Cantor law of $\sum_{k\ge1}2\varepsilon_k3^{-k}$, with independent fair digits. Its averaged energy tends to zero, although $|\hat\mu(3^m)|$ is the same nonzero number, about $0.37143$, for every $m$.
\item For independent identically distributed real $X,X'$ with characteristic function $\phi$, show $(2T)^{-1}\int_{-T}^T|\phi(t)|^2\dd t\to P(X=X')$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:gebelein}
\emph{Gaussian maximal correlation.} Let $\gamma$ be standard Gaussian measure and define $\mathrm{He}_n$ by $e^{sx-s^2/2}=\sum_{n\ge0}\mathrm{He}_n(x)s^n/n!$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that $\mathrm{He}_n/\sqrt{n!}$ form a Hilbert basis of $L^2(\gamma)$.
\item If $(X,Y)$ is a centred Gaussian pair with unit variances and correlation $\rho$, show $\mathbb E[\mathrm{He}_m(X)\mathrm{He}_n(Y)]=n!\rho^n\mathbf1_{m=n}$.
\item For centred $f,g\in L^2(\gamma)$, obtain $|\mathbb E[f(X)g(Y)]|\le|\rho|\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$. After normalising variances, this says functions of the pair cannot exceed $|\rho|$ in absolute correlation.
\item Diagonalise $Qf=\mathbb E[f(Y)\mid X=\cdot]$, finding eigenvalues $\rho^n$, and compute the correlation of $X^2,Y^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c16a}

\begin{solution}{pb:selberg}
\idea Control the synthesis vector by the absolute row sums of its Gram matrix, then compare it with $f$.
\writeup (a) Set $c_r=\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle$, $C=\sum_r|c_r|^2$, and $g=\sum_rc_r\varphi_r$. Since $\langle g,f\rangle=C$, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $C\le\|g\|\|f\|$. If $M=\max_r\sum_s|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|$, symmetry and $2|c_rc_s|\le|c_r|^2+|c_s|^2$ yield
\[\|g\|^2\le\sum_{r,s}|c_rc_s|\,|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|
 \le\sum_r|c_r|^2\sum_s|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|\le CM.
\]
For $C>0$, divide $C\le\|f\|\sqrt{CM}$ by $\sqrt C$ and square. If $C=0$, the result is immediate.

(b) Use $f=(a_n)$ and $\varphi_r=(e(-n\alpha_r))_{n\le N}$ in $\C^N$. Diagonal Gram entries equal $N$; off-diagonal absolute entries are at most $1/(2\|\alpha_r-\alpha_s\|)$ by \eqref{eq:dirichletkernel}. On each side of a fixed circle point, the $k$th other point has distance at least $k\delta$. Each row sum is therefore at most
\[N+2\sum_{k=1}^{R-1}\frac1{2k\delta}\le N+\frac{1+\log R}{\delta}.
\]
Part (a) proves the desired bound.
\tools{Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert} supplies Cauchy--Schwarz; equation~\eqref{eq:dirichletkernel} controls exponential inner products.}
\leads Bombieri's 1974 monograph helped spread Selberg's inequality. Extremal functions of Beurling and Selberg improve the exponential-family constant to $N-1+1/\delta$, the sharp large-sieve bound developed in Volume~14, Chapter~4. That improvement is quoted here; the Gram-matrix argument applies much more widely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:welch}
\idea Fixed trace and small rank force a large squared eigenvalue sum, which is also the sum of squared Gram entries.
\writeup (a) The Gram matrix $G=(\langle u_s,u_r\rangle)_{r,s}$ is positive semidefinite, has rank at most $n$ and trace $R$. List its nonzero eigenvalues with zeros appended to $n$ entries as $\mu_i$. Then
\[R^2=(\sum_i\mu_i)^2\le n\sum_i\mu_i^2
 =n\sum_{r,s}|\langle u_r,u_s\rangle|^2
 \le n(R+R(R-1)\varepsilon^2).
\]
Rearranging gives (a). (b) The same inequality reads $R(1/n-\varepsilon^2)\le1-\varepsilon^2$; divide by its positive coefficient under the stated condition.

(c) Three planar directions have mutual inner products $-1/2$, attaining the bound $1/4$ for the squared coherence. For the icosahedron choose one vector per opposite pair from cyclic permutations of $(0,\pm1,\pm\varphi)$, where $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$. Their squared lengths are $\varphi+2$ and their pairwise inner products have absolute value $\varphi$. The identity $(\varphi+2)^2=5\varphi^2$ gives normalised squared coherence $1/5=(6-3)/(3\cdot5)$, again equality.
\tools{The Hermitian spectral theorem in Chapter~\ref{linear:ch:spectral} and the Gram construction from Volume~1, Chapter~8, justify the eigenvalue calculation.}
\leads Welch's 1974 bound is used for communication sequences with small cross-correlations. Equality forces the $n$ nonzero Gram eigenvalues to equal $R/n$ and all off-diagonal magnitudes to agree. In particular $\sum_r|\langle x,u_r\rangle|^2=(R/n)\|x\|^2$: these redundant vectors form a tight frame.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:gerzon}
\idea Rank-one matrices turn squared angles into a positive definite Gram matrix in a space of known dimension.
\writeup (a) Cyclicity of trace gives $\operatorname{tr}(P_iP_j)=(u_i^{\mathsf T}u_j)^2$. Hence the Gram matrix is $(1-\alpha^2)I+\alpha^2J$.

(b) Its eigenvalues are $1-\alpha^2$ on the zero-sum subspace and $1+(R-1)\alpha^2$ on constants. They are positive. Therefore the $P_i$ are linearly independent: a relation would make its coefficient vector have zero Gram quadratic form. Real symmetric matrices have dimension $n(n+1)/2$, yielding the bound.

(c) Three planar lines separated by $\pi/3$ attain the bound in dimension two. The six icosahedral lines of Problem~\ref{pb:welch} attain it in dimension three.
\tools{Use Gram-matrix positivity and the spectral calculation of Chapter~\ref{linear:ch:spectral}.}
\leads Lemmens and Seidel published Gerzon's bound in 1973. Equality examples are known in dimensions $2,3,7,23$; beyond dimension three, equality requires $n+2$ to be an odd square. These classification facts are quoted. Jiang, Tidor, Yao, Zhang and Zhao's 2021 theorem determines the eventual maximum for a fixed angle in large dimension, where the growth is linear.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:halasz}
\idea Align the coefficient phases before applying Cauchy--Schwarz. Character cancellation then limits how many coefficients can be large.
\writeup (a) Choose unit scalars $\xi_r$ with $\overline\xi_r\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle=|\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle|$, choosing any phase for a zero coefficient. For $g=\sum_r\xi_r\varphi_r$,
\[\sum_r|\langle f,\varphi_r\rangle|=\langle f,g\rangle\le\|f\|\|g\|,
\qquad\|g\|^2\le\sum_{r,s}|\langle\varphi_r,\varphi_s\rangle|.
\]
Squaring proves (a).

(b) Take $\varphi_\chi=(\overline{\chi(n)})_{n\le N}$. The diagonal inner products are $N$, since $N<q$. For distinct characters their quotient is nonprincipal and primitive modulo the prime $q$. Pólya--Vinogradov (Volume~6, Theorem~11.11) bounds the other inner products by $G=\sqrt q(1+\log q)$. Consequently
\[R^2V^2\le A(RN+R(R-1)G)\le ARN+AR^2G.
\]
If $R=0$ there is nothing to prove; otherwise $V^2\ge2AG$ lets us absorb the final term and divide to get $R\le2AN/V^2$.

(c) Expand the squared sums and use character orthogonality. The inner character sum is $q-1$ when $m\equiv n\pmod q$, zero otherwise; because $m,n<q$, only $m=n$ survives. Thus the total is $(q-1)A$ and $R\le(q-1)A/V^2$. Part (b) replaces $q-1$ with $2N$ when its threshold applies, a substantial gain for $N\ll q$.
\tools{The earlier ingredients are Cauchy--Schwarz, character orthogonality in Volume~6, Chapter~3, and its Theorem~11.11.}
\leads Halász and Montgomery developed this large-values method for Dirichlet polynomials. Bounds for inner products then come from zeta estimates instead of character sums, leading to the zero-density arguments of Volume~14, Chapter~1. Guth and Maynard's 2024 work improved such large-values and density estimates; those results are not used here.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ingham}
\idea A cosine weight has a transform decaying quadratically. Frequency separation makes its off-diagonal row sums smaller than the diagonal term.
\writeup (a) Product-to-sum integration, with $a=\pi/(2T)$, gives
\[K(\lambda)=\frac{\sin((a+\lambda)T)}{a+\lambda}+\frac{\sin((a-\lambda)T)}{a-\lambda}
 =\frac{4\pi T\cos(\lambda T)}{\pi^2-4\lambda^2T^2}.
\]
At $\lambda=\pm a$ use the continuous limiting value. In particular $K(0)=4T/\pi$, and for $2|\lambda|T>\pi$,
\[|K(\lambda)|\le\frac{4\pi T}{4\lambda^2T^2-\pi^2}.
\]
(b) Because $0\le k\le\mathbf1_{[-T,T]}$, expanding the weighted integral gives the diagonal $(4T/\pi)\sum|a_n|^2$ minus at most $\sum_m|a_m|^2\sum_{n\ne m}|K(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)|$. Here we used $2|a_ma_n|\le|a_m|^2+|a_n|^2$. Set $x=\gamma T/\pi>1$. Since $|\lambda_m-\lambda_n|\ge|m-n|\gamma$,
\[\sum_{n\ne m}|K(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)|
 \le\frac{8T}{\pi}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1{4j^2x^2-1}
 \le\frac{8T}{\pi x^2}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac1{4j^2-1}=\frac{4T}{\pi x^2}.
\]
The last series telescopes after writing its terms as $\tfrac12((2j-1)^{-1}-(2j+1)^{-1})$. Subtract this bound to obtain (b).

(c) Use $k_2(t)=\cos(\pi t/(4T))$ on $[-2T,2T]$, zero elsewhere. It is at least $1/\sqrt2$ on $[-T,T]$. The preceding row-sum computation, with $y=2\gamma T/\pi\ge1$, bounds its weighted integral above by $(8T/\pi)(1+y^{-2})\sum|a_n|^2$. Multiplying by $\sqrt2$ proves (c).

(d) Consecutive perturbed frequencies have gap at least $1/2$. The lower bound applies for $T>2\pi$ and the upper for $T\ge\pi$. Translation by $T_0$ multiplies each coefficient by a unit scalar, so the constants
\[c=\frac{4T}{\pi}(1-4\pi^2/T^2),\qquad C=\frac{8\sqrt2T}{\pi}(1+\pi^2/T^2)
\]
work on every translated interval.
\tools{Only integration, coefficient quadratic forms and the separation hypothesis enter this proof.}
\leads Ingham's 1936 theorem gives the stated lower threshold, which cannot in general be reduced. Kadec's 1964 theorem is sharper for perturbed integers: under the uniform condition $\sup_n|\delta_n|<1/4$, the exponentials form a Riesz basis of $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$. The strict uniform bound matters; pointwise strict inequalities alone do not assert it. Such estimates also enter controllability of vibrating systems.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:mvt}
\idea A compactly supported Fourier transform cancels all cross terms exactly. Smoothing a longer interval makes a weight close enough to its indicator.
\writeup (a) Inverting the triangular function gives
\[2\int_0^\Delta(1-\xi/\Delta)\cos(2\pi\xi t)\dd\xi
 =\frac{1-\cos(2\pi\Delta t)}{2\pi^2\Delta t^2}=k(t),
\]
with the removable value at zero. Both functions are integrable and continuous, so Fourier inversion (Theorem~\ref{thm:ftinv}) gives $\hat k=\Lambda$, and $\int k=\Lambda(0)=1$.

(b) For $I=[T_0-U,T_0+T+U]$, the transform of $w=\mathbf1_I*k$ is $\widehat{\mathbf1_I}\Lambda$, zero for $|\xi|\ge\Delta$ and equal to $T+2U$ at zero. Expanding $|F|^2$ leaves only diagonal terms, proving the integral identity. On the target interval, the integration range defining $w(t)$ contains $[-U,U]$. Hence
\[w(t)\ge1-2\int_U^\infty k(v)\dd v\ge1-\frac2{\pi^2\Delta U}.
\]
Positivity of $w$ outside the interval and this lower bound inside prove the inequality.

(c) Put $\lambda_n=-\log n/(2\pi)$. Distinct integers at most $N$ have logarithms separated by at least $\log(N/(N-1))\ge1/N$, so take $\Delta=1/(2\pi N)$ and $U=\sqrt{TN}$. Let $h=\sqrt{N/T}\le1/4$. The ratio in (b) becomes $T(1+2h)/(1-4h/\pi)$. Its excess over $T$ is
\[\sqrt{TN}\frac{2+4/\pi}{1-4h/\pi}
 \le\sqrt{TN}\frac{2\pi+4}{\pi-1}<5\sqrt{TN},
\]
since $\pi>3$. This proves the stated constant by exact inequalities.

(d) A single nonzero coefficient gives exactly $T|a_n|^2$, excluding a leading factor below one. For a block $M<n\le2M$, the older bound in Volume~14, Lemma~1.3, has error $8M(1+\log M)$; this argument gives $5\sqrt{2TM}$. Their relative usefulness depends on $T$, and neither is optimal.
\tools{Use Fourier inversion, convolution and the elementary reciprocal-square tail estimate.}
\leads Montgomery--Vaughan's 1974 theorem gives $\int_0^T|\sum a_nn^{-it}|^2\dd t=\sum_n(T+O(n))|a_n|^2$. Beurling--Selberg majorants also give the sharp separated-frequency upper constant $T+1/\Delta$. These stronger results require tools beyond the Fej\'er smoothing used here.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{pb:christoffel}
\idea Sum the evaluation vectors from an orthonormal polynomial basis, then read the optimal bound from the kernel diagonal.
\writeup (a) Proposition~\ref{prop:legendre} gives $\int_{-1}^1P_k^2=2/(2k+1)$, so $e_k=\sqrt{k+1/2}P_k$ is orthonormal. Expanding $p$ in that basis verifies $\langle p,\sum_ke_k(y)e_k\rangle=p(y)$, yielding the kernel.

(b) Cauchy--Schwarz gives $|p(1)|^2\le\|p\|^2K_n(1,1)$. Since $P_k(1)=1$, the diagonal is $\sum_{k=0}^n(k+1/2)=(n+1)^2/2$. Equality holds precisely for multiples of the representing vector.

(c) Insert $P_0=1,P_1=x,P_2=(3x^2-1)/2$ to find $K_2(x,1)=(15x^2+6x-3)/4$. Its value and squared norm are both $9/2$; direct integration gives $(90-36+18)/16=9/2$. Thus its evaluation ratio is $9/2$. At zero the diagonal is $1/2+(5/2)(1/4)=9/8$, attained by $K_2(x,0)=(9-15x^2)/8$.
\tools{Legendre orthogonality and the reproducing property from Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert} supply the calculation.}
\leads The reciprocal $1/K_n(x,x)$ is the Christoffel function. The endpoint value grows quadratically in $n$, whereas for fixed $-1<x<1$ the quoted asymptotic is $K_n(x,x)\sim n/(\pi\sqrt{1-x^2})$. General weights lead to a substantial theory of orthogonal polynomials; Nevai's survey in the sources develops it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bergman}
\idea Disc averages control evaluations. Orthogonal monomials then determine the kernel and express image area as a coefficient sum.
\writeup (a) For $r=1-|z|$, Cauchy's formula on smaller circles, integrated in their radii, gives $f(z)=(\pi r^2)^{-1}\int_{D(z,r)}f\dd A$. One can first use radius $r'<r$ and let $r'\uparrow r$; Cauchy--Schwarz and integrability justify this limit. Therefore $|f(z)|^2\le\|f\|^2/(\pi r^2)$. If $f_j\to g$ in $L^2$, this bound makes $f_j$ uniformly Cauchy on compact subdiscs. The locally uniform limit is holomorphic (Volume~5, Theorem~9.15) and agrees almost everywhere with $g$, using an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence. This proves closedness and continuity of evaluations.

(b) Parseval on a circle and Tonelli give
\[\int_{|z|<r}|f|^2\dd A=\pi\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{|a_n|^2r^{2n+2}}{n+1}.
\]
Monotone convergence as $r\uparrow1$ proves the norm formula. Taylor tails tend to zero in this norm, so $e_n(z)=\sqrt{(n+1)/\pi}z^n$ is a Hilbert basis. Summing $e_n(z)\overline{e_n(w)}$ gives $K(z,w)=1/(\pi(1-z\bar w)^2)$.

(c) The diagonal kernel gives the first sharp bound, with equality for $f=K_z$. Also $f'(0)=a_1$ and $\|f\|^2\ge\pi|a_1|^2/2$, with equality for multiples of $z$.

(d) We first justify the change of variables. If $f-f(a)$ had a zero of order $m>1$, choose a small closed disc about $a$ on which $f'$ has no other zeros and $f-f(a)$ has none on the boundary. Rouch\'e's theorem (Volume~5, Theorem~9.38) gives $m$ zeros of $f-w$ inside for $w$ near but unequal to $f(a)$. They are simple and distinct, contradicting injectivity. Thus $f'(a)\ne0$. The same local argument shows the image is open; the inverse is continuous and its difference quotient tends to $1/f'(a)$. It is therefore holomorphic, making $f$ a real $C^1$ diffeomorphism with Jacobian $|f'|^2$.

Volume~7, Theorem~9.8, now gives $\operatorname{area}f(\mathbb D)=\int_{\mathbb D}|f'|^2\dd A$. If infinite, the inequality is immediate. Otherwise (b), applied to $f'$, gives
\[\operatorname{area}f(\mathbb D)=\pi\sum_{n\ge1}n|a_n|^2\ge\pi|a_1|^2=\pi.
\]
Equality forces $a_n=0$ for $n\ge2$; the normalisation then makes $f(z)=z$.
\tools{Cauchy's formula, locally uniform holomorphic limits, Rouch\'e, and change of variables are available in Volumes~5 and~7.}
\leads Bergman introduced these spaces in his 1921 thesis, published in 1922. For a proper simply connected domain, the kernel can recover its normalised Riemann map via $F'(z)=\sqrt{\pi/K_\Omega(w,w)}K_\Omega(z,w)$. This quoted identity belongs to the broader theory developed by Hedenmalm, Korenblum and Zhu; no mapping theorem was needed for the disc calculation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:fock}
\idea A radial Gaussian weight keeps monomials orthogonal and assigns their squared norms the factorials.
\writeup (a) Circle Parseval followed by Tonelli gives
\[\frac1\pi\int_\C|f|^2e^{-|z|^2}\dd A
 =2\sum_n|a_n|^2\int_0^\infty r^{2n+1}e^{-r^2}\dd r
 =\sum_nn!|a_n|^2.
\]
The substitution $t=r^2$ evaluates each radial integral as $n!/2$, and positivity permits infinite values.

(b) Coefficients identify $\mathcal F$ isometrically with $\ell^2$ via $f\mapsto(\sqrt{n!}a_n)$. Conversely, any $\ell^2$ sequence gives an entire series after division by $\sqrt{n!}$, since its root coefficients tend to zero. Thus $z^n/\sqrt{n!}$ is a Hilbert basis. Its kernel sum is $e^{z\bar w}$; Cauchy--Schwarz gives the bound, attained at $z$ by the kernel vector $w\mapsto e^{w\bar z}$.

(c) The norm formula becomes $\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}k|a|^{2k}$. Bounds $4^k/(2k+1)\le\binom{2k}k\le4^k$ show convergence for $|a|<1/2$ and divergence for $|a|\ge1/2$, including the harmonic lower bound at equality. If $\phi(x)=\sum_k\binom{2k}kx^k$, its coefficient recurrence gives $(1-4x)\phi'=2\phi$ and $\phi(0)=1$. Hence $\phi(x)=(1-4x)^{-1/2}$ on $|x|<1/4$, proving the stated norm.

(d) For polynomial coefficients $a_n,b_n$, the two pairings both equal $\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)!a_{n+1}\overline{b_n}$. The commutator identity is the product rule.
\tools{Use Tonelli from Volume~7, circle Parseval, and the coefficient description of a Hilbert basis.}
\leads Fock's 1928 work and Bargmann's 1961 construction connect this space to the quantum harmonic oscillator. Differentiation and multiplication are annihilation and creation operators; both are unbounded, as $\|z^{n+1}\|/\|z^n\|=\sqrt{n+1}$. The Bargmann transform sends Hermite functions to these monomials. Such operator statements are context here, not extra premises in the norm proof.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:sobolev}
\idea An interior derivative jump represents evaluation inside the interval; at an endpoint a boundary derivative does the same job.
\writeup (a) For $0<y<L$, the displayed function is continuous and piecewise smooth, hence in $H^1$. On either side, $K_y''=K_y$, with $K_y'(0)=K_y'(L)=0$ and
\[K_y'(y^-)-K_y'(y^+)
 =\frac{\sinh y\cosh(L-y)+\cosh y\sinh(L-y)}{\sinh L}=1.
\]
The absolutely continuous representative of $u$ allows integration by parts on both pieces. The boundary terms at zero and $L$ vanish, and the jump gives $\int u'K_y'=u(y)-\int uK_y$. Thus $\langle u,K_y\rangle=u(y)$.

For $y=0$, use $K_0(x)=\cosh(L-x)/\sinh L$. It has $K_0'(0)=-1$, $K_0'(L)=0$ and $K_0''=K_0$, so the single integration by parts gives the boundary term $u(0)$. For $y=L$, $K_L'(0)=0,K_L'(L)=1$, giving $u(L)$. These endpoint derivatives replace, rather than satisfy, the interior Neumann conditions.

(b) The diagonal is
\[K_y(y)=\frac{\cosh L+\cosh(2y-L)}{2\sinh L}\le\coth L,
\]
with equality only at the endpoints. For nonzero $u$, equality in the global bound requires its maximum at an endpoint and equality in Cauchy--Schwarz there. Thus the extremisers are multiples of $K_0$ or $K_L$, including the zero function. Both endpoint kernels do attain the bound.

(c) At $y=L/2$, the diagonal equals $\cosh^2(L/2)/\sinh L=\tfrac12\coth(L/2)$, attained by $K_{L/2}$. Its limit is $1/2$, compared with endpoint limit one. On $\R$, Exercise~\ref{ex:gagliardo} gives $\|u\|_\infty^2\le\|u\|_2\|u'\|_2\le(\|u\|_2^2+\|u'\|_2^2)/2$, with equality for a multiple of $e^{-|x|}$. This is the translated midpoint-kernel limit after normalisation. Reflection at an endpoint doubles the norm integral without changing the endpoint value, explaining the factor two.
\tools{Use the absolutely continuous Sobolev representatives from Chapter~\ref{ch:distributions}, reproducing kernels and Exercise~\ref{ex:gagliardo}.}
\leads Higher-order Sobolev evaluation kernels exist when the smoothness exceeds $d/2$, under suitable domain hypotheses. On the whole space they are related to elliptic Green functions and to Mat\'ern covariance kernels. Sharp higher-dimensional inequalities require additional work, such as Aubin and Talenti's 1976 results.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:dirichlet}
\idea Square-summable coefficients make evaluation a bounded coefficient pairing. Multiplicativity turns a product's squared norm into Euler factors.
\writeup (a) Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_n|a_n|n^{-\sigma}\le\|F\|\sqrt{\zeta(2\sigma)}$ for $\sigma>1/2$. It also gives locally uniform convergence in that half-plane. Coefficients identify the space with $\ell^2$; their uniqueness is checked next. The vector with coefficients $n^{-\bar w}$ represents evaluation at $w$, so its function is $K_w(s)=\zeta(s+\bar w)$.

(b) Taking $F=K_s$ gives equality in the evaluation bound. If a series vanishes identically, let real $\sigma\to\infty$ to recover $a_1=0$. Inductively, after removing the first $n-1$ coefficients, multiply by $n^\sigma$ to obtain $a_n+\sum_{m>n}a_m(n/m)^\sigma\to a_n$. Domination follows by fixing any $\sigma_*>1/2$ and using absolute convergence of $\sum|a_m|m^{-\sigma_*}$. Thus all coefficients vanish, as also proved in Volume~5, Proposition~10.2.

(c) The coefficients $c_n=\sum_{de=n}d^{-a}e^{-b}$ are multiplicative. At a prime write $u=p^{-a},v=p^{-b}$; then $c_{p^k}=h_k=\sum_{j=0}^ku^jv^{k-j}$. Summing three geometric series for $u\ne v$ gives
\[\sum_{k\ge0}h_k^2=\frac{1+uv}{(1-u^2)(1-v^2)(1-uv)}.
\]
The same formula holds at $u=v$ by continuity; here $h_k=(k+1)u^k$, and local uniform convergence permits the limit. Its product over primes converges since $2a,2b,a+b>1$. Non-negative summation over integers with prime factors in finite sets then increases to $\sum_nc_n^2$, proving membership and the Euler product without assuming finiteness in advance. Finally $(1+uv)/(1-uv)=(1-u^2v^2)/(1-uv)^2$, so the product is
\[\frac{\zeta(2a)\zeta(2b)\zeta(a+b)^2}{\zeta(2a+2b)}.
\]
\tools{Coefficient Cauchy--Schwarz, Dirichlet-series uniqueness and Euler products in Volume~5, Chapter~10, suffice.}
\leads This norm formula is a special case of Ramanujan's divisor-sum identity
\[\sum_n\frac{\sigma_\alpha(n)\sigma_\beta(n)}{n^s}
 =\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(s-\alpha)\zeta(s-\beta)\zeta(s-\alpha-\beta)}{\zeta(2s-\alpha-\beta)},
\]
initially in its region of absolute convergence. Hedenmalm, Lindqvist and Seip used this Hilbert space in 1997 to study dilated functions. Bohr's prime coordinates identify its coefficients with monomials on an infinite torus; the next problems explain part of that connection.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{pb:franel}
\idea Fourier support identifies the common multiples of two dilation frequencies. Möbius inversion then organises the sum of all these inner products.
\writeup (a) Integration by parts gives $\hat B(j)=i/(2\pi j)$ for $j\ne0$ and $\hat B(0)=0$. Substitution and a finite geometric sum show that $B(mx)$ has coefficient zero unless $m\mid j$, in which case it is $im/(2\pi j)$. Parseval (Theorem~\ref{thm:fourierl2}), with $\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$, gives
\[\int_0^1B(mx)B(nx)\dd x
 =\sum_{\ell\mid j,\ j\ne0}\frac{mn}{4\pi^2j^2}
 =\frac{mn}{12\ell^2}=\frac{\gcd(m,n)^2}{12mn}.
\]
(b) The matrix is twelve times a Gram matrix. For strict positivity, suppose $\sum_{m\le N}c_mB(mx)=0$. Its positive Fourier coefficient at $j\le N$ gives $\sum_{m\mid j}mc_m=0$. Induction starts at $c_1=0$ and leaves only $jc_j=0$ at each step. Thus the functions are independent and their Gram matrix is positive definite.

(c) Let $S(N)=\sum_{m,n\le N}\gcd(m,n)^2/(mn)$ and $J_2=\mu*\mathrm{id}^2$. Divisor inversion yields
\[S(N)=\sum_{d\le N}\frac{J_2(d)}{d^2}H_{\lfloor N/d\rfloor}^2
 =\sum_{e\le N}\frac{\mu(e)}{e^2}T(N/e),\quad T(x)=\sum_{k\le x}H_{\lfloor x/k\rfloor}^2.
\]
Write $w_i=H_i^2-H_{i-1}^2=(2H_i-1/i)/i$. Exchanging the finite sums gives $T(x)=\sum_{i\le x}w_i\lfloor x/i\rfloor$. Replacing the floor by $x/i$ costs at most $\sum_{i\le x}w_i=H_{\lfloor x\rfloor}^2=O((1+\log x)^2)$. Extending the remaining series to infinity costs $x\sum_{i>x}w_i/i=O(1+\log x)$, since $w_i\le2(1+\log i)/i$. Euler's identity below gives $\sum_iw_i/i=2\sum_iH_i/i^2-\zeta(3)=3\zeta(3)$. Hence
\[T(x)=3\zeta(3)x+O((1+\log x)^2).
\]
Substitute this into $S(N)$. Volume~5, Corollary~10.4, gives $\sum_{e\le N}\mu(e)/e^3=1/\zeta(3)+O(N^{-2})$, while $\sum e^{-2}<\infty$ controls the errors. Thus $S(N)=3N+O(\log^2N)$. Divide by twelve to obtain $N/4+O(\log^2N)$.

To prove the Euler identity, put $E=\sum_{m,n\ge1}(mn(m+n))^{-1}$. Splitting $1/(mn)=(1/m+1/n)/(m+n)$ gives $E=2\sum_{k\ge2}H_{k-1}/k^2$. On the other hand, Tonelli and $1/(m+n)=\int_0^1x^{m+n-1}\dd x$ give
\[E=\int_0^1\frac{\log^2(1-x)}x\dd x
 =\int_0^1\frac{\log^2u}{1-u}\dd u
 =\sum_{k\ge0}\frac2{(k+1)^3}=2\zeta(3).
\]
The integral of $u^k\log^2u$ follows by two differentiations of $\int_0^1u^k\dd u$, or two integrations by parts. Therefore $\sum H_{k-1}/k^2=\zeta(3)$ and $\sum H_k/k^2=2\zeta(3)$, as needed.
\tools{Use Parseval, Tonelli, Möbius inversion and Volume~5, Corollary~10.4.}
\leads Franel's 1924 formula and Landau's companion asymptotic connected these sums with Farey fractions and the Riemann hypothesis. Allowing arbitrary distinct integers instead of the first $N$ changes the maximal size to order $N(\log\log N)^2$, by Gál's 1949 theorem. Later work, including Bondarenko--Seip, uses large gcd sums to construct large zeta values.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ramanujan}
\idea Orthogonality alone handles finite expansions; a uniform summable bound is what permits Parseval for this infinite one.
\writeup First justify the two finite identities in the question. Insert $\mathbf1_{(a,q)=1}=\sum_{d\mid(a,q)}\mu(d)$ in the defining character sum. The inner complete sum is zero unless $q/d\mid n$, when it equals $q/d$. Renaming that divisor gives $c_q(n)=\sum_{d\mid(q,n)}\mu(q/d)d$, which is real. For orthogonality use $c_q(n)\overline{c_r(n)}$ and average over a common period. A pair of reduced fractions $a/q,b/r$ contributes only if they agree modulo one; this requires $q=r$ and the same numerator, giving $\varphi(q)$. This proof includes $q=1$ and needs no result from the problem book.

(a) The divisor formula bounds $|c_q(n)|$ by $\sigma(n)$ for each fixed $n$, so the series is absolutely convergent. Reorder its absolutely summable divisor terms:
\[\sum_{q\ge1}\frac{c_q(n)}{q^2}
 =\sum_{d\mid n}\frac1d\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^2}
 =\frac1{\zeta(2)}\sum_{d\mid n}\frac1d
 =\frac{\sigma(n)}{n\zeta(2)}.
\]
(b) Periodicity makes the mean equal to the common-period average just computed. Also
\[\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}|c_q(n)c_r(n)|
 \le\sum_{d\mid q,e\mid r}de\frac{\lfloor N/\operatorname{lcm}(d,e)\rfloor}{N}
 \le\sum_{d\mid q,e\mid r}\gcd(d,e)
 \le\sigma_{1/2}(q)\sigma_{1/2}(r).
\]
(c) Square (a), average, and use this bound on the pair series. Its majorant is summable because $\sum_q\sigma_{1/2}(q)/q^2=\zeta(2)\zeta(3/2)<\infty$, by exchanging non-negative divisor sums. Dominated convergence therefore gives
\[M((\sigma(n)/n)^2)=\zeta(2)^2\sum_q\frac{\varphi(q)}{q^4}
 =\zeta(2)^2\frac{\zeta(3)}{\zeta(4)}=\frac52\zeta(3).
\]
Here the totient Dirichlet series is Volume~5, Corollary~10.4. This is Parseval with coefficients $\zeta(2)/q^2$ and squared basis norms $\varphi(q)$.
\tools{Finite character sums, the Möbius and totient Dirichlet series, and dominated convergence establish every step here.}
\leads Ramanujan's 1918 paper contains these expansions and others for totients and representation counts. Partial summation relates the mean above to his 1916 leading term $\sum_{n\le x}\sigma(n)^2\sim(5/6)\zeta(3)x^3$. His stronger error estimate requires additional information; it does not follow from the limit alone. Carmichael and Wintner developed broader conditions for Ramanujan expansions and their Parseval identities.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:zetamellin}
\idea Logarithmic coordinates turn Mellin Plancherel into ordinary Plancherel. An elementary fractional-part integral then fixes the exact zeta mean.
\writeup (a) Set $u=e^{-x}$ and $h(x)=g(e^{-x})e^{-\sigma x}$. The hypotheses say $h\in L^1\cap L^2$, and $\mathcal Mg(\sigma+2\pi i\xi)=\hat h(\xi)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:plancherel}, followed by $t=2\pi\xi$, proves the formula.

(b) For $\Re s>1$, termwise integration gives
\[\zeta(s)=s\int_1^\infty\lfloor x\rfloor x^{-s-1}\dd x
 =\frac{s}{s-1}-s\int_1^\infty\{x\}x^{-s-1}\dd x.
\]
The last integral is holomorphic for $\Re s>0$ by local domination. The identity theorem continues the formula there away from one. When $0<\Re s<1$, the integral from zero to one is $\int_0^1x^{-s}\dd x=1/(1-s)$. It absorbs the rational term, giving $\zeta(s)=-s\int_0^\infty\{x\}x^{-s-1}\dd x$. Substitute $x=1/u$.

(c) Take $g(u)=\{1/u\}$ and $\sigma=1/2$. The bound $g(u)\le\min(1,1/u)$ verifies both integrability hypotheses. It remains to evaluate $I=\int_0^\infty\{1/u\}^2\dd u=\int_0^\infty\{x\}^2x^{-2}\dd x$. The part from zero to one is one. On $[n,n+1]$ direct integration gives
\[\int_n^{n+1}\frac{(x-n)^2}{x^2}\dd x
 =1+\frac n{n+1}-2n\log(1+1/n).
\]
Summing through $n=N$ yields
\[I_N=2N+2-H_{N+1}-2N\log(N+1)+2\log(N!).\]
Stirling's formula, available in Volume~6, Theorem~6.1(c), and $H_N-\log N\to\gamma$ give $I_N\to\log(2\pi)-\gamma$. This supplies the integral directly; Problem Book~I, Problem~9.12, is an additional exercise on the same calculation. Parts (a) and (b) now prove (c).

(d) On $|t|\le T$, the factor $(T^2+1/4)/(t^2+1/4)$ is at least one. Multiply the integrand by it and bound the resulting weighted integral by the full one in (c).
\tools{Plancherel, holomorphic parameter integrals, analytic continuation and Stirling are earlier results. The fractional-part integral is computed here.}
\leads The unweighted estimate here is deliberately weak. Hardy--Littlewood's 1916 theorem gives $\int_0^T|\zeta(1/2+it)|^2\dd t\sim T\log T$. The exact weighted identity is useful for a different reason: it puts zeta into the Hilbert-space setting of the next problem.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bohrvalues}
\idea Approximate finitely many prime phases independently, then make the remaining product uniformly negligible.
\writeup (a) Since $p^{-\sigma}<1/2$, the principal logarithms obey $|\log(1-p^{-\sigma}z_p)^{-1}|\le2p^{-\sigma}$. Their series converges uniformly on the product torus, giving a continuous logarithm and a nonzero continuous product $Z_\sigma$. The countable product of circles is compact (Volume~7, Theorem~4.11), so its image is compact as well.

(b) Put $\beta_j=\log p_j/(2\pi)$. An integer relation among the $\beta_j$ would give $\prod_jp_j^{r_j}=1$, forcing every $r_j=0$ by unique factorisation. Choose $c>0$ such that $1,c\beta_1,\ldots,c\beta_k$ are rationally independent. This is possible because a forbidden relation excludes only one value $c=-r_0/(\sum_jr_j\beta_j)$, and there are countably many integer tuples. Write $w_j=e(-\theta_j)$. Kronecker--Weyl (Theorem~\ref{thm:kroneckerweyl}) gives arbitrarily large integers $n$ with all $\|nc\beta_j-\theta_j\|<\varepsilon/(2\pi)$. Then $t=nc>t_0$ works, since $|e(u)-e(v)|\le2\pi\|u-v\|$.

(c) All zeta values belong to the compact image, so their closure is contained in it. Conversely, truncate $Z_\sigma$ at a large prime cutoff so that its uniform error is below $\varepsilon$. Continuity of that finite product and (b) make its value within $\varepsilon$ of any prescribed phase product, at an arbitrarily large time. The two tail errors add at most $2\varepsilon$. Thus each point of $E_\sigma$ lies in the value closure, even using only sufficiently large positive times.

(d) Each factor's modulus lies between $(1+p^{-\sigma})^{-1}$ and $(1-p^{-\sigma})^{-1}$. The all-minus and all-plus phase choices attain those extremes in $E_\sigma$. Their products are $\zeta(2\sigma)/\zeta(\sigma)$ and $\zeta(\sigma)$, respectively. At $\sigma=2$ they are $\pi^2/15$ and $\pi^2/6$. The upper extreme occurs at $t=0$. Attainment of the lower would require $2^{-it}=3^{-it}=-1$, making $\log3/\log2$ a ratio of odd integers, contrary to unique factorisation.
\tools{Use compact products, Theorem~\ref{thm:kroneckerweyl}, and unique factorisation from Volume~2, Theorem~1.6.}
\leads Bohr's 1911--1913 value-set work led to Bohr--Jessen's limiting distributions in 1930--1932. For $\sigma>1$ the random model is the image of product Haar measure under $Z_\sigma$. They also treated $1/2<\sigma\le1$, where this absolute-product argument no longer applies. Bohr--Courant proved density in $\C$ for $1/2<\sigma<1$, and Voronin's 1975 universality theorem approximates nowhere-zero holomorphic functions on suitable compact sets after vertical shifts. Volume~15, Chapter~11, develops the distribution questions; none is used above.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:almostperiodic}
\idea Compact orbit closures give returns with bounded gaps even when the frequencies satisfy rational relations.
\writeup (a) Use the torus metric $d(x,y)=\max_j\|x_j-y_j\|$. The closure $O$ of $\{\tau\alpha:\tau\in\R\}$ modulo $\Z^k$ is a compact subgroup. Cover it by finitely many balls of radius $\eta/2$ centred at orbit points $\tau_i\alpha$. Let $M=\max_i|\tau_i|$. Given real $a$, approximate $-a\alpha$ by one of these centres. Then $\|(a+\tau_i)\alpha\|<\eta/2$, with $a+\tau_i\in[a-M,a+M]$. Taking a positive length larger than $2M$ proves relative density, including $M=0$.

(b) Choose $N\ge2$ with $\sum_{n>N}n^{-\sigma_1}<\varepsilon/4$. The two tails in the translated difference contribute less than $\varepsilon/2$, uniformly in the half-plane. For $n=\prod_pp^{k_p}\le N$, telescoping unit factors gives
\[|n^{-i\tau}-1|\le\sum_pk_p|p^{-i\tau}-1|
 \le2\pi\log_2N\max_{p\le N}\left\|\frac{\tau\log p}{2\pi}\right\|.
\]
Apply (a) to the finite prime frequencies with $\eta=\varepsilon/(4\pi N\log_2N)$. Their relatively dense returns make the sum of the first $N$ errors less than $\varepsilon/2$, proving (b).

(c) Apply (b) at real $s=\sigma$ to get returns near the maximum modulus. Any limit would therefore have to equal $\zeta(\sigma)$. \leads Bohr's almost periodic functions have relatively dense sets of uniform $\varepsilon$-returns for every $\varepsilon>0$. His 1925--1926 approximation theorem identifies them with uniform limits of finite sums $\sum c_ke^{i\lambda_kt}$ with real frequencies. \begin{solution}{pb:vandelune}
\idea Each Euler factor has a maximal turning angle. Their sum controls the sign, and Bohr's theorem nearly realises any chosen finite alignment.
\writeup (a) The point $v=1-rz$ lies on the circle centred at one with radius $r$. Tangents from zero make angles $\pm\arcsin r$, so its arguments fill that interval. Inversion changes their signs and preserves the right half-plane. It also maps this circle, which misses zero, to a circle, proving (a). The extreme angles occur when $\Re z=r$.

(b) The bounds $x\le\arcsin x\le\pi x/2$ on $[0,1]$ give local uniform convergence of the prime sum for $\sigma>1$. Each term decreases strictly, so the continuous sum does too. The upper bound $A(\sigma)\le(\pi/2)(\zeta(\sigma)-1)$ tends to zero as $\sigma\to\infty$. Monotone convergence and divergence of $\sum_p1/p$ (Volume~5, Corollary~10.5) give divergence at one. The intermediate value theorem supplies exactly one $\sigma_0$.

(c) The absolutely convergent logarithm $L(s)=\sum_p\log(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ satisfies $e^{L(s)}=\zeta(s)$ and $|\Im L(s)|\le A(\sigma)$. Above $\sigma_0$ this is less than $\pi/2$, proving positivity.

At $\sigma=\sigma_0$, equality $|\Im L|=\pi/2$ would require every term to attain an extreme angle with the same sign. At $t=0$ all arguments are zero. For $t\ne0$, choose arbitrarily large intervals $[X,cX]$, with a fixed $c>1$, on which $\Re(x^{-it})>1/2$. Such intervals exist by periodicity in $\log x$, choosing $c$ close enough to one. The prime number theorem, already proved in Chapter~\ref{ch:tauberian}, places a prime in each sufficiently large such interval. At that prime, $\Re(p^{-it})>1/2>p^{-\sigma_0}$, so (a)'s tangent condition fails. At least one term is strictly below its maximal absolute angle, forcing $|\Im L|<\pi/2$ and positivity also on the boundary.

(d) Below $\sigma_0$, choose finitely many primes with angle sum $S>\pi/2$. Choose $\theta\in(\pi/2,\min(\pi,S))$ and turn each factor by the fraction $\theta/S$ of its positive extreme angle; the intermediate value theorem permits this. Set all other phases to one. The resulting product has argument $\theta$ and negative real part. Bohr's value-closure theorem approximates it by a zeta value, preserving that strict sign.

(e) A direct prime tail behaves on the scale $\int_X^\infty t^{-\sigma_0}/\log t\dd t$, which decays slowly because $\sigma_0-1$ is small. Integer zeta sums can instead be accelerated by Euler--Maclaurin. For the prime sum expand
\[A(\sigma)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\binom{2k}k}{4^k(2k+1)}P((2k+1)\sigma),\qquad P(s)=\sum_pp^{-s}.
\]
Positive convergence permits interchange. Euler's logarithmic product and Möbius inversion give, for real $s>1$,
\[P(s)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\mu(m)}m\log\zeta(ms).
\]
Absolute convergence justifies inversion, and the summands decay exponentially in $m$. This provides an efficient numerical method. The decimal $1.192347337186193\ldots$ is a reported approximation, not the proof of the threshold or its endpoint.
\tools{The Euler product, prime-harmonic divergence, Bohr's theorem and the earlier prime number theorem provide the ingredients.}
\leads Van de Lune's 1983 report located the threshold. Arias de Reyna, Brent and van de Lune computed it to many digits in arXiv:1112.4910 and studied zero curves of the real part. Such curves can approach the threshold from its left; the strict boundary positivity above prevents them from crossing it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:norate}

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:wiener}
\idea Average the Fourier characters first. The resulting bounded kernel converges to the diagonal indicator in the product space.
\writeup (a) Fubini gives
\[\frac1{2N+1}\sum_{|n|\le N}|\hat\mu(n)|^2
 =\iint F_N(x-y)\dd\mu(x)\dd\mu(y),\quad
F_N(\theta)=\frac1{2N+1}\sum_{|n|\le N}e(-n\theta).
\]
Its modulus is at most one. It equals one at zero modulo integers and tends to zero elsewhere by the geometric-sum formula. Dominated convergence yields the product measure of the diagonal, namely $\int\mu(\{x\})\dd\mu(x)=\sum_x\mu(\{x\})^2$. A finite measure has countably many atoms, so this last sum is well defined.

(b) Every level-$k$ Cantor interval has mass $2^{-k}$, making each singleton have mass zero. Part (a) therefore gives vanishing average energy. Independence of the digits gives
\[\hat\mu(n)=(-1)^n\prod_{j\ge1}\cos(2\pi n/3^j)\qquad(n\in\Z).
\]
For $n=3^m$, the first $m$ cosine factors equal one and the remaining product is the same as for $n=1$. It is positive after combining the phase $-1$ with the first cosine $-1/2$; all later cosines are positive, with summable errors from one. Thus the common coefficient is nonzero, numerically about $0.37143$, as also discussed in Volume~7, Problem~13.16. A sparse subsequence need not follow the averaged limit.

(c) Independence gives $|\phi(t)|^2=\mathbb E[e^{it(X-X')}]$. Its time average inside the expectation is $\sin(Tu)/(Tu)$ at $u=X-X'$, assigned one at zero. It has modulus at most one and tends to $\mathbf1_{u=0}$. Fubini and dominated convergence give the probability $P(X=X')$.
\tools{Finite-measure Fubini, Tonelli and dominated convergence justify these exchanges.}
\leads Wiener's 1924 theorem detects atoms without forcing each Fourier coefficient to decay. Cantor measure illustrates that distinction. Menshov constructed singular measures whose coefficients do decay; such Rajchman measures play a role in uniqueness questions for trigonometric series. The real-line limit equals the sum of squared atomic probabilities and vanishes exactly for a nonatomic law.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:gebelein}
\idea Hermite coefficients diagonalise Gaussian correlation. Removing the constant coefficient forces at least one factor of $\rho$.
\writeup (a) For standard Gaussian $X$,
\[\mathbb E[e^{sX-s^2/2}e^{uX-u^2/2}]=e^{su}.
\]
Comparing coefficients yields $\mathbb E[\mathrm{He}_m(X)\mathrm{He}_n(X)]=n!\mathbf1_{m=n}$. To justify coefficient comparison, on bounded parameter sets the series of absolute terms is dominated by an integrable exponential of $|X|$ times a fixed constant. This follows by multiplying the absolutely convergent series for $e^{sX}$ and $e^{-s^2/2}$, and similarly for $u$.

The polynomials $\mathrm{He}_n$ have leading term $x^n$, hence span all polynomials. For completeness, the map $f\mapsto F$, $F(y)=f(2\sqrt\pi y)e^{-\pi y^2}$, satisfies $\|F\|_2^2=\|f\|_{L^2(\gamma)}^2/\sqrt2$ and is onto $L^2(\R)$ after this fixed scaling. It sends polynomials to polynomial multiples of $e^{-\pi y^2}$, whose span contains Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite}'s Hilbert basis. The polynomial span is therefore dense in $L^2(\gamma)$.

(b) A Gaussian vector is determined by its covariance (Volume~4, Theorem~6.14), so we may write $Y=\rho X+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z$ with independent standard Gaussian $Z$. This includes $\rho=\pm1$. Its generating expectation is $e^{\rho su}$; the same dominated coefficient comparison gives $n!\rho^n\mathbf1_{m=n}$.

(c) The bilinear form $\mathbb E[f(X)g(Y)]$ is continuous by Cauchy--Schwarz. Expand the centred functions as $f=\sum_{n\ge1}f_n\mathrm{He}_n/\sqrt{n!}$ and $g=\sum_{n\ge1}g_n\mathrm{He}_n/\sqrt{n!}$. Passing from finite sums by that continuity gives
\[\mathbb E[f(X)g(Y)]=\sum_{n\ge1}\rho^nf_ng_n.
\]
Its modulus is at most $|\rho|\sum|f_ng_n|\le|\rho|\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$, proving the bound.

(d) A version of conditional expectation is $Qf(x)=\mathbb E[f(\rho x+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}Z)]$. Its generating function gives $Q\mathrm{He}_n=\rho^n\mathrm{He}_n$. Conditional expectation is an $L^2$ contraction, so the basis expansion determines $Q$ completely; its eigenvalues are precisely these diagonal values, with their natural multiplicities, including the degenerate cases. Since $X^2=1+\mathrm{He}_2(X)$, the squared-variable covariance is $2\rho^2$ and each variance is two. Their correlation is $\rho^2$.
\tools{Gaussian covariance, Theorem~\ref{thm:hermite}, basis expansions and the earlier conditional-expectation contraction are enough.}
\leads Gebelein's 1941 inequality identifies Gaussian maximal correlation as $|\rho|$, later part of R\'enyi's dependence theory. For $0<\rho<1$, $Q$ is the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck operator at time $-\log\rho$; negative $\rho$ also involves reflection. Nelson's quoted hypercontractivity theorem gives an $L^2$ to $L^{1+\rho^{-2}}$ contraction for $0<|\rho|<1$. At zero the operator projects onto constants, and at the two endpoints it is identity or reflection.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c16a}
\idea One of four angular gaps is small, while a symmetric arrangement balances the projection energy exactly.
\writeup Sort the four line angles modulo $\pi$. Their circular gaps sum to $\pi$, so some gap is at most $\pi/4$. The corresponding absolute inner product is at least $\cos(\pi/4)=1/\sqrt2$. For the displayed four directions, direct matrix addition gives $\sum_{j=0}^3u_ju_j^{\mathsf T}=2I$, hence the exact identity. Each absolute Gram row contains $1,1/\sqrt2,0,1/\sqrt2$, with sum $1+\sqrt2$. Selberg gives that larger constant, while the actual optimal coefficient is two.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c16b}
\idea Project the evaluation vector perpendicular to the constant function before applying Cauchy--Schwarz.
\writeup The constraint $f(0)=0$ means $f\perp1$. On this subspace, $v(z)=e^{z\bar a}-1$ represents evaluation at $a$ and has squared norm $v(a)=e^{|a|^2}-1>0$. Thus $1=|f(a)|^2\le\|f\|^2(e^{|a|^2}-1)$. Equality forces $f$ to be a scalar multiple of $v$; the second constraint fixes it as $f(z)=(e^{z\bar a}-1)/(e^{|a|^2}-1)$. It attains the bound and is unique.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c16c}
\idea The averaged energy sees only atomic masses, while sparse coefficients can also record the continuous part.
\writeup The only atom of $\mu$ is at zero with mass $c$, so Problem~\ref{pb:wiener} gives averaged energy limit $c^2$. For Cantor $\nu$, the product computed there gives $\hat\nu(3^m)=\kappa=\tfrac12\prod_{j\ge2}\cos(2\pi/3^j)>0$. Positivity follows from positive factors and the convergent sum $\sum_{j\ge2}(1-\cos(2\pi/3^j))$; a logarithm estimate then keeps their product nonzero. Linearity gives $\hat\mu(3^m)=c+(1-c)\kappa$. These indices have density zero, so the persistent extra coefficient size does not alter the averaged energy limit.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c16d}
\idea A real diagonal tending to zero is compact, and its squared entries give the Hilbert--Schmidt norm.
\writeup In the orthonormal Hermite basis, $Q$ has diagonal $\rho^n$, $n\ge0$. The entries are real, proving self-adjointness. Keeping the first $N+1$ entries gives a finite-rank operator whose norm error is $|\rho|^{N+1}$, tending to zero, so $Q$ is compact. Its Hilbert--Schmidt squared norm is $\sum_{n\ge0}\rho^{2n}=(1-\rho^2)^{-1}$. The operator $P$ keeps just the first entry; $Q^k-P$ has diagonal zero at $n=0$ and $\rho^{kn}$ for $n\ge1$. Its norm is $|\rho|^k$, attained on $\mathrm{He}_1$. For $\rho=0$, $Q=P$, the norm difference is zero and the Hilbert--Schmidt norm is one, agreeing with both formulas.
\end{solution}

\section{From values to distributions}

Bohr's closed value set describes what zeta can approach, but does not say how often it approaches each region. Giving the independent prime phases Haar probability produces a random Euler product, and leads to Bohr--Jessen's distribution question. Volume~15, Chapter~11, starts from that model and studies large, small and typical values on vertical lines. The bounds and kernels proved here are useful preparation; our proofs do not require that later theory.
