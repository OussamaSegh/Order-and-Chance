\chapter*{Preface}
Volume~7 built the stage: the real numbers, limits, measures, and the integral. This book asks what happens when a function is treated as a point of a space. The space has infinite dimension, and the old words of linear algebra come back with new meanings. A basis, a dual, an eigenvalue, a projection: each one still has a definition, and each one can fail in a way that finite dimension forbids. The closed unit ball need not be compact. A linear map need not be continuous. An operator need not have an eigenvalue at all.

The first two chapters are Banach spaces and Hilbert spaces. Hahn--Banach is proved there, and so are the three theorems that rest on Baire's theorem from Volume~7. Pythagoras then works for functions, not only for arrows in the plane. Spectral theory opens on matrices, the finite case: the spectral theorem, singular values, and a graph read through its eigenvalues. Cuts, mixing, and expansion come next, then expanders and the Riemann hypothesis for graphs. Those spectra are still finite. Operators on a Banach space follow, with Wiener's lemma, and after them the spectral theorem for a compact self-adjoint operator. That is the theorem that hears a vibrating string.

The next chapters write a function as a sum of waves. The Fourier transform on $\R^d$ brings Plancherel, Poisson summation, the uncertainty principle, and Shannon's sampling theorem. Distributions give a derivative to every locally integrable function, and Sobolev spaces are where many differential equations find their solutions. On a finite abelian group the same transform is a fast algorithm, and it proves quadratic reciprocity. Haar measure and Pontryagin duality come next, with the $p$-adic numbers as a group that is isomorphic to its own dual. The ad\`eles, which see $\R$ and every $\Q_p$ at once, are built in Volume~13.

The part ends in the complex plane. Cauchy's theorem is proved in Dixon's form, using the winding number and the Jordan curve theorem of Volume~7. The growth of entire functions and Mellin inversion follow. Tauberian theorems then recover the behaviour of a sequence from a generating function, and one of them proves the prime number theorem again. The last chapter is a set of problems on the values of the zeta function. The tools of the book meet there.

The book assumes Volume~7, the Fourier series and the complex analysis of Volume~5, the groups of Volume~3, and the linear algebra of Volume~1. Six appendices hold the further linear algebra the main chapters use: duality, determinants as volume, eigenvalues, inner products, inequalities between matrices, and linear dynamics. A reader who already knows that material can read the sixteen chapters straight through. Each chapter and each appendix ends with exercises, and each exercise has a hint and a complete solution.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random and still obey a law. This volume is the set of beams: the compact spectral theorem, Fourier analysis on the groups the series meets, and Ikehara's proof of the prime number theorem. The next volume asks how the rooms connect. The spectral theorem proved here stops at compact operators.
