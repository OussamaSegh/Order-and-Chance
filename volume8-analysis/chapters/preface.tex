\chapter*{Preface}
Volume~7 built the stage: real numbers, limits, measures and integrals. This book is about what happens on it when functions are
treated as points. A function becomes a vector in a space of infinite dimension, and the old words of linear algebra, basis,
dual, eigenvalue, projection, return with new meanings and new surprises. Infinite dimension is not just ``more of the same'':
the unit ball stops being compact, linear maps can be discontinuous, and an operator can have no eigenvalue at all.

The first two chapters are Banach and Hilbert spaces: Hahn--Banach and the three great
consequences of Baire's theorem, then Pythagoras for functions. Spectral Theory opens with matrices, the concrete case: the spectral theorem for matrices, singular values, and graphs heard through their eigenvalues. Cuts, mixing and expansion come next, then expanders and the Riemann hypothesis for graphs, still finite spectra. Operators and the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators follow, and one hears the shape of a vibrating string.

The next chapters are harmonic analysis, the art of writing a function as a superposition of waves. The Fourier transform
on $\R^d$, with Plancherel, Poisson summation, the uncertainty principle and Shannon's sampling theorem; distributions, which give
a derivative to every function, and Sobolev spaces, where differential equations find their solutions; Fourier analysis on
finite abelian groups, where it becomes the fast Fourier transform and proves quadratic reciprocity; and Haar measure on locally
compact groups and Pontryagin duality, with the $p$-adic numbers as a self-dual group. The adèles, which see $\R$ and every $\Q_p$ at once, are built in Volume~13.

The part ends in the complex plane: Cauchy's theorem in its final form, using the winding number and the Jordan curve theorem of Volume~7, the growth of
entire functions and Mellin inversion. Tauberian theorems then recover the behaviour of a
sequence from its generating function, and lead once more to the prime number theorem. The last chapter is a chapter of problems on the values of the zeta function, where the tools of the book meet.

The book assumes Volume~7, the Fourier series and complex analysis of Volume~5, the groups of Volume~3 and the linear algebra of Volume~1. The six appendices hold the further linear algebra the main chapters use: duality, determinants as volume, eigenvalues, inner products, inequalities between matrices and linear dynamics. A reader who knows that material reads the sixteen chapters straight through. Each chapter and each appendix ends with
exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random yet obey laws. This floor is the beams: the compact spectral theorem, Fourier analysis on every group the series meets, and Ikehara's proof of the prime number theorem. The next floor takes up how rooms connect. The spectral theorem proved here stops at compact operators.
