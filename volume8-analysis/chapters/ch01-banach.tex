\chapter{Banach Spaces}\label{ch:banach}

\begin{center}\itshape
A unit ball in infinite dimension need not be compact. A linear map need not be continuous. Unit vectors may converge weakly to zero without getting shorter. Which parts of linear algebra can we keep?
\end{center}

In the 1930s, Banach and his friends did mathematics at the Scottish Caf\'e on Akademicka Street in Lw\'ow. They wrote on its marble tables in pencil. A session could last seventeen hours; the waiters wiped the tables clean at night. In 1935 \L ucja Banach bought a thick notebook and left it with the head waiter. It became the \emph{Scottish Book}, a collection of almost two hundred problems. Authors offered prizes: beer, wine, even a fondue in Geneva.

Mazur's Problem~153, dated 6 November 1936, offered a live goose. Its question about continuous functions of two variables led to an equivalent question about operators: can every compact operator on a Banach space be approximated by operators of finite rank? In 1972 Per Enflo found a counterexample. Thirty-six years after the problem was written, he went to Warsaw and received the goose from Mazur. Polish television recorded the ceremony. Enflo was also a concert pianist.

Banach's \emph{Th\'eorie des op\'erations lin\'eaires} appeared in 1932. Its spaces of ``type~(B)'' are the complete normed spaces we now call Banach spaces. Three basic results come from Baire's theorem in Volume~7: pointwise bounded operators are uniformly bounded; continuous linear surjections are open; linear maps with closed graphs are continuous. Hahn--Banach supplies the linear forms that measure vectors. Hahn found the extension theorem in Vienna in 1927, two years before supervising G\"odel's thesis; Banach found it independently in 1929. These tools will identify dual spaces, detect divergent Fourier series and recover compactness in a topology weaker than the norm topology.

\section{Normed spaces and bounded operators}

A norm assigns a length $\|x\|$ to each vector of $E$. Precisely, $\|\cdot\|:E\to[0,\infty[$ satisfies $\|x\|=0\iff x=0$, $\|\lambda x\|=|\lambda|\|x\|$ and $\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|$. The distance is $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$. Completeness for this distance makes $E$ a \emph{Banach space}. Volume~7 already gave us $\R^n$ with any norm, $C_b(X)$ with the supremum norm (Chapter~2), and $L^p(\mu)$ for $1\le p\le\infty$ (Theorem~10.4). Keep these further examples in mind:
\begin{itemize}
\item The sequence space $\ell^p$, for $1\le p<\infty$, consists of $(x_n)_{n\ge1}$ with $\|x\|_p=(\sum_n|x_n|^p)^{1/p}<\infty$. It is $L^p$ for counting measure on $\N^*$. The bounded sequences form $\ell^\infty$, with the supremum norm.
\item The sequences tending to zero form $c_0$. This subspace is closed in $\ell^\infty$, so it is complete.
\item For a compact metric space $K$, the continuous functions form $C(K)$, with $\|f\|_\infty=\max_K|f|$.
\end{itemize}

An \emph{operator} is a linear map $T:E\to F$ between normed spaces. It is bounded when a constant $C$ satisfies $\|Tx\|\le C\|x\|$ for every $x$. Its norm is the smallest such constant:
\[\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|\le1}\|Tx\|.\]
A scalar-valued operator is a \emph{linear form}. All bounded linear forms on $E$ together make up its \emph{dual space} $E^*$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:boundedcont}
For a linear map $T:E\to F$, these conditions are equivalent: (a) continuity on $E$; (b) continuity at $0$; (c) boundedness. The bounded maps form a normed space $\mathcal L(E,F)$. If $F$ is Banach, so is $\mathcal L(E,F)$; in particular, $E^*$ is Banach for every normed $E$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Condition (a) includes continuity at $0$. Under (b), choose $\delta>0$ so that $\|Tx\|\le1$ whenever $\|x\|\le\delta$. Scaling a nonzero $x$ to $\delta x/\|x\|$ gives $\|Tx\|\le\delta^{-1}\|x\|$, which is (c). Conversely, boundedness gives $\|Tx-Ty\|=\|T(x-y)\|\le C\|x-y\|$. Thus $T$ is Lipschitz and hence continuous.

Positivity and homogeneity of the operator norm follow from those of the norm on $F$. For its triangle inequality, use $\|(S+T)x\|\le\|Sx\|+\|Tx\|\le(\|S\|+\|T\|)\|x\|$. Now suppose $F$ is complete and $(T_n)$ is Cauchy in operator norm. For fixed $x$, the estimate $\|T_nx-T_mx\|\le\|T_n-T_m\|\|x\|$ makes $(T_nx)$ Cauchy. Define $Tx=\lim_nT_nx$. Passing to limits in sums and scalar multiples shows that $T$ is linear. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\|T_n-T_m\|\le\varepsilon$ for $n,m\ge N$. Letting $m$ tend to infinity yields $\|T_nx-Tx\|\le\varepsilon\|x\|$. Consequently $T_n-T$ is bounded, $T$ is bounded, and $\|T_n-T\|\le\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$.
\end{proof}

In finite dimension, all norms are equivalent and every linear map is bounded (Volume~7, Corollary~4.6). The unit ball gives us a way to recognise when that finite-dimensional picture has ended.

\begin{lemma}[Riesz's lemma]\label{lem:riesz}
Suppose $Y\ne E$ is a closed linear subspace of a normed space $E$, and $0<\theta<1$. Some unit vector $x$ satisfies $d(x,Y)\ge\theta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose $z\notin Y$. Closedness gives $d=d(z,Y)>0$. The definition of infimum supplies $y_0\in Y$ with $\|z-y_0\|\le d/\theta$. Set $x=(z-y_0)/\|z-y_0\|$. For any $y\in Y$, the vector $y_0+\|z-y_0\|y$ still belongs to $Y$, so
\[\|x-y\|=\frac{\|z-(y_0+\|z-y_0\|y)\|}{\|z-y_0\|}\ge\frac d{\|z-y_0\|}\ge\theta.\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Riesz]\label{thm:rieszcompact}
A normed space has a compact closed unit ball exactly when its dimension is finite.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\dim E=n$, a linear bijection $\mathbb K^n\to E$ is a homeomorphism by Volume~7, Corollary~4.6. The unit ball corresponds to a closed bounded set in finite dimension, which is compact. If $E$ is infinite-dimensional, choose unit vectors inductively. The space $Y_n=\operatorname{span}(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is finite-dimensional, hence complete and closed, and cannot equal $E$. Riesz's lemma gives a unit $x_{n+1}$ at distance at least $\frac12$ from $Y_n$. Distinct vectors in this sequence are at least $\frac12$ apart. No subsequence converges, so Volume~7, Theorem~4.8, rules out compactness.
\end{proof}

For $\ell^2$, the usual unit vectors already do the job: $\|e_n-e_m\|_2=\sqrt2$ whenever $n\ne m$.

\begin{think}
Finite-dimensional linear maps are continuous. Can a linear form on $\ell^2$ be discontinuous? What would its values on unit vectors have to do?
\end{think}

\section{The Hahn--Banach theorem}

Coordinates give plenty of bounded linear forms on $\R^n$. An arbitrary normed space has no preferred coordinates, so even finding one nonzero bounded form needs an argument. Hahn--Banach extends a form from a subspace without increasing its norm. Its real version uses a \emph{sublinear} map $p:E\to\R$, meaning $p(x+y)\le p(x)+p(y)$ and $p(tx)=tp(x)$ for $t\ge0$. Norms satisfy these conditions. So do $x\mapsto\limsup x_n$ on $\ell^\infty$ and the convex-set gauges used below.

\begin{theorem}[Hahn--Banach, analytic form]\label{thm:hb}
Take a real vector space $E$, a sublinear map $p$ on $E$, and a linear form $g:G\to\R$ on a subspace $G$. If $g\le p$ on $G$, then a linear extension $f:E\to\R$ exists with $f\le p$ throughout $E$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Adding one vector.} For $x_0\notin G$, an extension to $G\oplus\R x_0$ is determined by $c=f(x_0)$. We need $g(y)+tc\le p(y+tx_0)$ for all $y\in G$ and real $t$. When $t>0$, divide by $t$ and rename $y/t$: this requires $c\le p(y+x_0)-g(y)$ for every $y\in G$. When $t<0$, divide by $-t$ to obtain $c\ge g(y')-p(y'-x_0)$ for every $y'\in G$. Each lower requirement is at most each upper requirement, because
\[g(y)+g(y')=g(y+y')\le p(y+y')\le p(y+x_0)+p(y'-x_0).\]
Thus a real $c$ can be chosen between the two families of bounds.

\emph{Extending to the whole space.} Order all admissible extensions $(H,h)$ by inclusion of their domains and agreement of the forms. A chain has an upper bound: unite its domains and use the form on each domain. These forms agree wherever their domains overlap. Zorn's lemma (Volume~7, Theorem~1.19) provides a maximal extension. If its domain missed a vector of $E$, the one-vector construction would extend it again. Its domain must therefore be $E$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For separable normed $E$ and $p\le C\|\cdot\|$, a dense sequence $(x_n)$ replaces Zorn's lemma. Extend successively to $G+\operatorname{span}(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ and take their union. On this dense subspace, the extension satisfies $|f(x)|\le C\|x\|$, hence is uniformly continuous. Volume~7, Lemma~2.8, extends it to $E$. The sublinear bound makes $p$ continuous, so $f\le p$ survives the limit. Choice matters in the general theorem: in some models without it, every linear form on $\ell^\infty$ vanishing on $c_0$ is zero.
\end{remark}

A complex-linear form is determined by its real part. Indeed $f(ix)=if(x)$ gives $\operatorname{Im}f(x)=-\operatorname{Re}f(ix)$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hbnorm}
Let $G$ be a subspace of a normed space $E$ over $\mathbb K$. (a) Each $g\in G^*$ has an extension $f\in E^*$ with $\|f\|=\|g\|$. (b) If $E\ne\{0\}$, each $x_0\in E$ admits $f\in E^*$ with $\|f\|=1$ and $f(x_0)=\|x_0\|$. For every $E$, including $\{0\}$, one has $\|x\|=\max_{\|f\|\le1}|f(x)|$, and $E^*$ separates points. (c) If $G$ is closed and $x_0\notin G$, a form $f\in E^*$ vanishes on $G$ and satisfies $f(x_0)=1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(a) Over $\R$, apply Hahn--Banach with $p(x)=\|g\|\|x\|$. Applying the bound to both $x$ and $-x$ gives $|f(x)|\le\|g\|\|x\|$. Restriction to $G$ gives the reverse norm inequality. Over $\C$, first extend the real-linear form $u=\operatorname{Re}g$ to $U$, with $|U(x)|\le\|g\|\|x\|$, and put $f(x)=U(x)-iU(ix)$. Real linearity and $f(ix)=U(ix)-iU(-x)=if(x)$ make $f$ complex-linear; it agrees with $g$ on $G$. For nonzero $f(x)$ choose $\theta$ with $f(x)=e^{i\theta}|f(x)|$. Then $f(e^{-i\theta}x)$ is real, and
\[|f(x)|=U(e^{-i\theta}x)\le\|g\|\|x\|.\]
The zero case satisfies the same bound, so again the extension preserves the norm.

(b) For $x_0\ne0$, extend $g(\lambda x_0)=\lambda\|x_0\|$, whose norm on $\mathbb Kx_0$ is $1$. If $x_0=0$ and $E\ne\{0\}$, take the norm-one form obtained from any nonzero vector. Every form of norm at most $1$ satisfies $|f(x)|\le\|x\|$, and the constructed form attains equality. When $E=\{0\}$, both sides of the maximum formula are zero. Applying that formula to a nonzero difference shows that the dual separates points.

(c) On $G\oplus\mathbb Kx_0$, define $g(y+\lambda x_0)=\lambda$. Since $G$ is closed, $d(x_0,G)>0$, and $|\lambda|d(x_0,G)\le\|y+\lambda x_0\|$. Thus $g$ is bounded and (a) extends it.
\end{proof}

Linear forms also separate convex sets. For an open convex $C$ containing $0$, define its \emph{gauge} by $p_C(x)=\inf\{t>0:x\in tC\}$. A ball $B(0,r)\subseteq C$ gives $p_C(x)\le\|x\|/r$. Scaling proves positive homogeneity. If $x\in sC$ and $y\in tC$, convexity gives
\[x+y=(s+t)\left(\frac{s}{s+t}\frac xs+\frac{t}{s+t}\frac yt\right)\in(s+t)C,\]
which proves subadditivity by taking infima. Moreover $C=\{p_C<1\}$. For $x\in C$, openness puts $(1+\varepsilon)x$ in $C$ for some $\varepsilon>0$, so $p_C(x)<1$. Conversely, $p_C(x)<1$ yields $x\in tC$ with $t<1$; convexity and $0\in C$ then give $x\in C$.

\begin{theorem}[Hahn--Banach, geometric form]\label{thm:separation}
Suppose $A$ and $B$ are disjoint nonempty convex subsets of a real normed space $E$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] When $A$ is open, some $f\in E^*$ and $\alpha\in\R$ satisfy $f(a)<\alpha\le f(b)$ for all $a\in A$ and $b\in B$.
\item[(b)] When $A$ is closed and $B$ is compact, some $f\in E^*$ and $\alpha<\beta$ satisfy $f(a)\le\alpha<\beta\le f(b)$ for all $a\in A$ and $b\in B$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Begin with $0\in A$ and $B=\{x_0\}$. On $\R x_0$, set $g(tx_0)=t$. For positive $t$, the gauge gives $p_A(tx_0)=tp_A(x_0)\ge t$, since $x_0\notin A$. For nonpositive $t$, $g(tx_0)\le0\le p_A(tx_0)$. Extend $g$ by Hahn--Banach to $f\le p_A$. The gauge bound, applied at $x$ and $-x$, makes $f$ bounded, and $f(a)\le p_A(a)<1=f(x_0)$.

For general $A,B$, fix $a_0\in A$ and $b_0\in B$, and put $x_0=b_0-a_0$ and $C=A-B+x_0$. This set is open, convex and contains zero. It excludes $x_0$ because $A$ and $B$ are disjoint. The first case gives $f(a)-f(b)+f(x_0)<f(x_0)$, hence $f(a)<f(b)$ for every pair. A nonzero linear form is open: choose $u$ with $f(u)\ne0$ and vary $t$ in $f(a+tu)=f(a)+tf(u)$. Therefore $f(A)$ is an open interval. Its finite supremum $\alpha$ is not attained, and $f(a)<\alpha\le f(b)$ follows.

(b) The positive continuous function $b\mapsto d(b,A)$ has a positive minimum $2r$ on compact $B$. The open convex sets $A_r=A+B(0,r)$ and $B_r=B+B(0,r)$ are disjoint. Part (a) supplies $f\ne0$ with $f(a+u)\le f(b+v)$ whenever $\|u\|,\|v\|<r$. Taking the supremum in $u$ and the infimum in $v$ gives $f(a)+r\|f\|\le f(b)-r\|f\|$. Set $\alpha=\sup_Af$ and $\beta=\alpha+2r\|f\|$ to obtain the required gap.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Every closed convex set is the intersection of the closed half-spaces containing it. If a point lies outside, separation from that singleton supplies a half-space that excludes it. Thus linear forms describe the same convex set as its points do. Convex optimisation uses this description to pair minimisation problems with dual maximisation problems.
\end{beauty}

\section{Three consequences of Baire's theorem}

A nonempty complete metric space cannot be a countable union of closed sets with empty interior (Volume~7, Theorem~5.2). Banach and Steinhaus used this fact in 1927 to turn separate bounds at individual vectors into one bound for a whole family of operators.

\begin{theorem}[uniform boundedness, Banach--Steinhaus]\label{thm:ubp}
Let $E$ be Banach, let $F$ be normed, and take a family $(T_i)_{i\in I}$ in $\mathcal L(E,F)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $\sup_i\|T_ix\|<\infty$ for each $x\in E$, then $\sup_i\|T_i\|<\infty$.
\item[(b)] If $\sup_i\|T_i\|=\infty$, the vectors $x$ satisfying $\sup_i\|T_ix\|=\infty$ form a dense $G_\delta$ in $E$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For each positive integer $n$, put $F_n=\{x:\|T_ix\|\le n\text{ for all }i\}$. These sets are closed. Suppose $F_n$ contains $\overline B(x_0,r)$. Whenever $\|z\|\le r$,
\[\|T_iz\|\le\|T_i(x_0+z)\|+\|T_ix_0\|\le2n,\]
so every $\|T_i\|$ is at most $2n/r$. Thus unbounded operator norms force every $F_n$ to have empty interior. Baire makes $E\setminus\bigcup_nF_n=\bigcap_n(E\setminus F_n)$ dense; it is a $G_\delta$, and its points are precisely those in (b). Under the hypothesis of (a), the sets $F_n$ cover $E$, so this alternative is impossible.
\end{proof}

Fourier series give a striking application. Dirichlet proved convergence for piecewise smooth functions in 1829. Continuity alone seemed a plausible substitute, until du Bois-Reymond constructed a counterexample in 1873. Uniform boundedness proves that the failures actually form a large set.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:fourierdiverge}
Equip the continuous $2\pi$-periodic functions $C(\mathbb T)$ with the supremum norm, and write $S_Nf(0)=\sum_{|n|\le N}\hat f(n)$. The functions $f$ for which $\sup_N|S_Nf(0)|=\infty$ form a dense $G_\delta$. Thus a generic continuous function has a Fourier series that diverges at $0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Volume~5, Lemma~6.4, writes $\Lambda_Nf=S_Nf(0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)D_N(t)\,dt$, where $D_N(t)=\sin((N+\frac12)t)/\sin(t/2)$. Set $L_N=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|D_N|$. The integral inequality gives $\|\Lambda_N\|\le L_N$. To attain this bound in the limit, take the continuous functions $f_k=\max(-1,\min(1,kD_N))$. They are bounded by $1$ and tend to $\operatorname{sgn}D_N$ almost everywhere. Dominated convergence gives $\Lambda_Nf_k\to L_N$, hence $\|\Lambda_N\|=L_N$. The estimate $|\sin(t/2)|\le|t|/2$ and substitution $u=(N+\frac12)t$ give
\[L_N\ge\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{|\sin((N+\frac12)t)|}t\,dt\ge\frac2\pi\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}\frac{|\sin u|}{(k+1)\pi}\,du=\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{k=1}^N\frac1k.\]
The harmonic sums diverge. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:ubp}(b) to $(\Lambda_N)$.
\end{proof}

These $L_N$ are called \emph{Lebesgue constants}. Rounded to three decimals, $L_1=1.436$, $L_{10}=2.223$, $L_{100}=3.139$ and $L_{1000}=4.070$. Their logarithmic growth is slow: multiplying $N$ by ten adds about $4\ln10/\pi^2=0.933$. Kolmogorov constructed an integrable function with an everywhere-divergent Fourier series in 1926. Carleson's theorem of 1966 goes the other way: every $L^2$ function has an almost-everywhere convergent Fourier series. We quote that deep result here. Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert} will prove convergence in $L^2$ norm. Fej\'er's theorem (Volume~5, Theorem~6.9) already gives uniform convergence of the Ces\`aro means for continuous functions. Ordinary partial sums and averaged partial sums can behave very differently.

\begin{theorem}[open mapping]\label{thm:openmapping}
A bounded linear surjection $T:E\to F$ between Banach spaces is open. More explicitly, some $\delta>0$ satisfies $B_F(0,\delta)\subseteq T(B_E(0,1))$. If $T$ is also injective, its inverse is bounded.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Finding a ball in the closure.} Surjectivity gives $F=\bigcup_n\overline{T(B_E(0,n))}$. Baire and scaling imply that $C=\overline{T(B_E(0,1))}$ contains a ball $B_F(y_0,4\delta)$. The set $C$ is convex and symmetric, so it also contains
\[\tfrac12\bigl(B_F(y_0,4\delta)+B_F(-y_0,4\delta)\bigr)\supseteq B_F(0,4\delta).\]
Scaling again gives $\overline{T(B_E(0,2^{-k}))}\supseteq B_F(0,2^{2-k}\delta)$ for every $k\ge0$.

\emph{Replacing approximation by equality.} Take $\|y\|<\delta$. The preceding inclusion with $k=2$ gives $x_1$ with $\|x_1\|<\frac14$ and $\|y-Tx_1\|<\delta/2$. Apply the next scaled inclusion to this error to obtain $\|x_2\|<\frac18$ and error below $\delta/4$. Repetition gives
\[\|x_k\|<2^{-k-1},\qquad\|y-T(x_1+\cdots+x_k)\|<2^{-k}\delta.\]
Completeness lets us sum $x=\sum_kx_k$, with $\|x\|<\frac12<1$. Continuity gives $Tx=y$, proving the ball inclusion. Translating and scaling that inclusion proves openness. If $T$ is bijective, its unique preimage of any $y\in B_F(0,\delta)$ lies in the unit ball. Scaling yields $\|T^{-1}y\|\le\delta^{-1}\|y\|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[closed graph]\label{thm:closedgraph}
Suppose $E$ and $F$ are Banach and $T:E\to F$ is linear. A closed graph $\{(x,Tx):x\in E\}\subseteq E\times F$ forces $T$ to be bounded.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The product norm $\|(x,y)\|=\|x\|+\|y\|$ makes $E\times F$ Banach. Its closed subspace $\Gamma$, the graph, is therefore Banach too. The map $\pi:\Gamma\to E$, $(x,Tx)\mapsto x$, is a bounded linear bijection. Its inverse is bounded by the open mapping theorem, giving $\|x\|+\|Tx\|\le C\|x\|$. In particular $\|Tx\|\le C\|x\|$.
\end{proof}

To use the closed graph theorem, assume both $x_n\to x$ and $Tx_n\to y$, then prove $y=Tx$. That suffices for continuity. Completeness cannot be omitted: differentiation on $C^1[0,1]$ with the supremum norm is unbounded but has a closed graph. Choice also enters here. The models mentioned after Hahn--Banach have every linear map between Banach spaces continuous (Garnir and Wright); without a Hamel basis, the construction in Exercise~\ref{ex:discontinuous} is unavailable.

\section{Duals of the classical spaces}

For $1/p+1/q=1$, H\"older's inequality gives a linear form $\varphi_g(f)=\int fg\,d\mu$ on $L^p$ from each $g\in L^q$, with $\|\varphi_g\|\le\|g\|_q$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.2). Does this produce every bounded linear form? Riesz answered yes for $1<p<\infty$ in 1910; Steinhaus treated $p=1$ on $[0,1]$ in 1919. Radon--Nikodym supplies a proof for general $\sigma$-finite measures.

\begin{theorem}[duality of $L^p$]\label{thm:lpdual}
For a $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu$, an exponent $1\le p<\infty$ and its conjugate $q$, the map $g\mapsto\varphi_g$ is a linear isometric bijection $L^q(\mu)\to L^p(\mu)^*$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We first work over $\R$, then check the complex case explicitly.

\emph{Preserving the norm.} If $p>1$ and $g\ne0$, test on $f=|g|^{q-1}\operatorname{sgn}g$. The identity $|f|^p=|g|^q$ gives $\|f\|_p=\|g\|_q^{q/p}$, while $\varphi_g(f)=\|g\|_q^q=\|g\|_q\|f\|_p$. If $p=1$, choose $0<\varepsilon<\|g\|_\infty$. The set $\{|g|>\|g\|_\infty-\varepsilon\}$ has positive measure. By $\sigma$-finiteness it contains $A$ with $0<\mu(A)<\infty$. Testing on $\mathbf 1_A\operatorname{sgn}g$ gives $\varphi_g(f)\ge(\|g\|_\infty-\varepsilon)\|f\|_1$. Let $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero. Together with H\"older, these tests prove $\|\varphi_g\|=\|g\|_q$; the zero function causes no exception.

\emph{Representing a form when $\mu$ is finite.} For $\varphi\in(L^p)^*$, define $\nu(A)=\varphi(\mathbf 1_A)$. If $A=\bigsqcup_kA_k$, then
\[\|\mathbf 1_A-\mathbf 1_{A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_n}\|_p=\mu\Bigl(\bigcup_{k>n}A_k\Bigr)^{1/p}\longrightarrow0.\]
Here $p<\infty$ and $\mu$ is finite. Continuity of $\varphi$ proves countable additivity of $\nu$. Also $\nu$ vanishes on $\mu$-null sets. Apply the Jordan decomposition (Volume~7, Corollary~11.3) and Radon--Nikodym (Theorem~11.4) to its finite positive parts. Their densities have difference $g\in L^1(\mu)$ with $\varphi(\mathbf 1_A)=\int_Ag\,d\mu$. Linearity gives $\varphi(f)=\int fg$ for simple $f$. Uniform approximation by simple functions gives the same identity for bounded measurable $f$: uniform convergence implies $L^p$ convergence here, and $g\in L^1$ controls the integrals.

We must still prove $g\in L^q$. For $p>1$, use the bounded functions $f_n=|g|^{q-1}\operatorname{sgn}g\,\mathbf 1_{\{|g|\le n\}}$. Then
\[\int_{\{|g|\le n\}}|g|^q=\varphi(f_n)\le\|\varphi\|\left(\int_{\{|g|\le n\}}|g|^q\right)^{1/p}.\]
If the integral is positive, divide by its $1/p$ power; if it is zero, the resulting bound is immediate. Monotone convergence now yields $\|g\|_q\le\|\varphi\|$. For $p=1$, take $A=\{g>\|\varphi\|+\varepsilon\}$. We have
\[(\|\varphi\|+\varepsilon)\mu(A)\le\int_Ag=\varphi(\mathbf 1_A)\le\|\varphi\|\mu(A),\]
so $A$ is null. Applying the same argument to $-g$ proves $\|g\|_\infty\le\|\varphi\|$. Both $\varphi$ and $\varphi_g$ are now bounded and agree on the dense simple functions (Volume~7, Proposition~10.6), hence agree on $L^p$.

\emph{Passing to a $\sigma$-finite space.} Choose increasing sets $E_n$ of finite measure covering $E$. Restrict $\varphi$ to functions supported in $E_n$. The finite-measure result gives $g_n$ there, with $\|g_n\|_q\le\|\varphi\|$. Uniqueness makes these densities agree almost everywhere on overlaps. Define a measurable $g$ using $g_n$ on $E_n\setminus E_{n-1}$, with $E_0=\varnothing$. It agrees almost everywhere with each $g_n$ on $E_n$. For finite $q$, monotone convergence gives $\|g\|_q\le\|\varphi\|$; for $q=\infty$, the same bound holds outside a countable union of null sets. The identity holds for every function supported in one $E_n$. Since $f\mathbf 1_{E_n}\to f$ in $L^p$ by dominated convergence, it holds for all $f\in L^p$.

\emph{Complex scalars.} Define $\operatorname{sgn}z=z/|z|$ for $z\ne0$ and $\operatorname{sgn}0=0$. Thus $z\,\overline{\operatorname{sgn}z}=|z|$. H\"older still gives the upper norm bound for complex $g$.

For a complex-linear $\varphi$, restrict to real-valued inputs $u$. The forms $u\mapsto\operatorname{Re}\varphi(u)$ and $u\mapsto\operatorname{Im}\varphi(u)$ are bounded real-linear forms, so the real case gives real densities $g_{\mathrm R},g_{\mathrm I}\in L^q$. Put $g=g_{\mathrm R}+ig_{\mathrm I}$. Then $\varphi(u)=\int ug$ for real $u$. Every complex $f=u+iv\in L^p$ has $u,v\in L^p$, and complex linearity yields $\varphi(f)=\varphi(u)+i\varphi(v)=\int fg$.

Estimating the two densities separately would only give $\|g\|_q\le2\|\varphi\|$. To obtain the exact norm, test on a complex function. For $p>1$, set $f=|g|^{q-1}\overline{\operatorname{sgn}g}$. Now $|f|^p=|g|^q$ and $fg=|g|^q$, so
\[\|g\|_q^q=\varphi(f)\le\|\varphi\|\|g\|_q^{q/p}.\]
For nonzero $g$, division and $q-q/p=1$ give $\|g\|_q\le\|\varphi\|$; for zero $g$ this already holds. If $p=1$, let $A$ be any finite-measure subset of $\{|g|>\|\varphi\|+\varepsilon\}$ and test on $\mathbf 1_A\overline{\operatorname{sgn}g}$. This gives $(\|\varphi\|+\varepsilon)\mu(A)\le\varphi(f)\le\|\varphi\|\mu(A)$, so $\mu(A)=0$. By $\sigma$-finiteness, the entire level set is null, giving $\|g\|_\infty\le\|\varphi\|$. H\"older proves equality of the norms in both cases. Finally, if $\varphi_g=\varphi_h$, this norm identity applied to $g-h$ gives $\|g-h\|_q=0$. The density is unique as an element of $L^q$.
\end{proof}

Counting measure gives $(\ell^p)^*=\ell^q$ for $1\le p<\infty$. At $p=\infty$, the proposed identification fails: $(L^\infty)^*$ contains more than $L^1$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:linftydual}). Banach limits provide another example of these extra forms (Exercise~\ref{ex:banachlimit}); choice enters their construction.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:c0dual}
A bounded linear form on $c_0$ has exactly one representation $\varphi(x)=\sum_na_nx_n$ with $a\in\ell^1$. Its norm is $\|a\|_1$, so $c_0^*=\ell^1$ isometrically.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $a_n=\varphi(e_n)$. The vector $x=\sum_{n\le N}\overline{\operatorname{sgn}a_n}\,e_n$ has norm at most $1$, so $\sum_{n\le N}|a_n|=\varphi(x)\le\|\varphi\|$. Hence $a\in\ell^1$ and $\|a\|_1\le\|\varphi\|$. For $x\in c_0$, its finite truncations converge in supremum norm because $x_n\to0$. Continuity gives $\varphi(x)=\lim_N\sum_{n\le N}a_nx_n=\sum_na_nx_n$. The series bound gives $|\varphi(x)|\le\|a\|_1\|x\|_\infty$. Evaluating at $e_n$ also proves uniqueness of the coefficients.
\end{proof}

For $C(K)$, measures take the place of sequences. A finite signed Borel measure has a Jordan decomposition $\nu=\nu^+-\nu^-$ into mutually singular finite measures (Volume~7, Chapter~11). Define its total variation norm by $\|\nu\|=\nu^+(K)+\nu^-(K)$. Write $M(K)$ for these signed measures. Identifying them with the dual will also prove that $M(K)$ is complete.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz--Markov]\label{thm:rieszmarkov}
For compact metric $K$, each $\varphi\in C(K,\R)^*$ is integration against exactly one finite signed Borel measure $\nu$. Moreover $\|\varphi\|=\|\nu\|$, giving an isometric identification $C(K)^*=M(K)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Separating a form into positive parts.} For continuous $f\ge0$, define $\varphi^+(f)=\sup\{\varphi(g):g\in C(K),\ 0\le g\le f\}$. This lies between $0$ and $\|\varphi\|\|f\|_\infty$. To prove additivity, first add any $g_1\le f_1$ and $g_2\le f_2$, both nonnegative; their sum is an admissible test for $f_1+f_2$, giving the inequality $\ge$. In the other direction, split any $0\le g\le f_1+f_2$ as
\[g_1=\min(g,f_1),\qquad g_2=g-g_1=\max(g-f_1,0).\]
These functions satisfy $0\le g_i\le f_i$, so $\varphi(g)\le\varphi^+(f_1)+\varphi^+(f_2)$. Positive homogeneity follows by scaling the tests. Extend to all real continuous functions by $\varphi^+(f)=\varphi^+(f^+)-\varphi^+(f^-)$. This does not depend on the nonnegative decomposition: if $f=u-v$, then $u+f^-=v+f^+$, and additivity gives the same difference. The extension is linear and positive. So is $\varphi^-=\varphi^+-\varphi$, since testing $g=f\ge0$ gives $\varphi^+(f)\ge\varphi(f)$.

\emph{Producing the measure.} Volume~7, Theorem~12.3, represents $\varphi^\pm$ by finite measures $\mu^\pm$. Their difference $\nu=\mu^+-\mu^-$ represents $\varphi$. For uniqueness, suppose integration against a signed measure is zero on all continuous functions. Its Jordan parts have the same integrals, hence are equal by the uniqueness part of that theorem. The signed measure is zero.

\emph{Matching the norms.} Integration immediately gives $|\int f\,d\nu|\le\|f\|_\infty\|\nu\|$. For the reverse inequality, if one Jordan part is zero, use the constant test $1$ or $-1$. Otherwise choose $0<\varepsilon<\min(\nu^+(K),\nu^-(K))$. Take a Hahn decomposition $K=P\sqcup N$ (Volume~7, Theorem~11.2) and put $\lambda=\nu^++\nu^-$. Inner regularity on compact metric $K$ (Volume~7, Theorem~12.1) supplies nonempty closed $F_P\subseteq P$ and $F_N\subseteq N$ with $\lambda(P\setminus F_P)<\varepsilon$ and $\lambda(N\setminus F_N)<\varepsilon$. The function
\[h(x)=\frac{d(x,F_N)-d(x,F_P)}{d(x,F_N)+d(x,F_P)}\]
is continuous, lies in $[-1,1]$, and equals $1$ on $F_P$ and $-1$ on $F_N$. Its denominator is positive because the closed sets are disjoint. Using $\nu^+(N)=\nu^-(P)=0$, the missed mass in either part is less than $\varepsilon$, and
\[\varphi(h)\ge(\nu^+(F_P)-\varepsilon)-(-\nu^-(F_N)+\varepsilon)\ge\|\nu\|-4\varepsilon.\]
Let $\varepsilon$ tend to zero to conclude $\|\varphi\|\ge\|\nu\|$.
\end{proof}

Reflexivity asks whether the dual's dual contains any new vectors. The canonical map $J:E\to E^{**}$ is $J(x)(\varphi)=\varphi(x)$. Corollary~\ref{cor:hbnorm}(b) gives $\|J(x)\|=\sup_{\|\varphi\|\le1}|\varphi(x)|=\|x\|$. Thus $J$ is an isometry. We call $E$ \emph{reflexive} when $J$ is onto.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reflexive}
If $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite and $1<p<\infty$, then $L^p(\mu)$ is reflexive. In contrast, $c_0$ is not reflexive.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given $\Phi\in(L^p)^{**}$, the rule $g\mapsto\Phi(\varphi_g)$ is a bounded linear form on $L^q$. Since $q<\infty$, duality again supplies $f\in L^p$ with $\Phi(\varphi_g)=\int fg=\varphi_g(f)$. Every element of $(L^p)^*$ is some $\varphi_g$, so $\Phi=J(f)$. For $c_0$, the preceding dual identifications give $c_0^{**}=(\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty$. The canonical map becomes the inclusion $c_0\subseteq\ell^\infty$, which misses the constant sequence $1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Weak topologies}

Norm convergence asks all directions to be controlled at once. Weak convergence tests one linear form at a time: write $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ when $\varphi(x_n)\to\varphi(x)$ for every $\varphi\in E^*$. On the dual, weak* convergence means $\varphi_n(x)\to\varphi(x)$ for every $x\in E$, written $\varphi_n\overset*\rightharpoonup\varphi$. The weak* topology is the coarsest topology making every evaluation $\varphi\mapsto\varphi(x)$ continuous. Equivalently, view $E^*$ inside the product $\mathbb K^E$ with the product topology of Volume~7, Chapter~3. Fewer open sets make compactness possible again.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weakbounded}
Every weakly convergent sequence is norm bounded. If $x_n\rightharpoonup x$, then $\|x\|\le\liminf\|x_n\|$. When $E$ is Banach, every weak* convergent sequence in $E^*$ is norm bounded too.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The forms $J(x_n)$ on the Banach space $E^*$ are pointwise bounded, since $J(x_n)(\varphi)=\varphi(x_n)$ converges for each $\varphi$. Uniform boundedness gives $\sup_n\|x_n\|=\sup_n\|J(x_n)\|<\infty$. Choose a form of norm at most $1$ with $\varphi(x)=\|x\|$, as in Corollary~\ref{cor:hbnorm}. Taking real parts if necessary gives $\|x\|=\lim\operatorname{Re}\varphi(x_n)\le\liminf\|x_n\|$. For weak* convergence, apply uniform boundedness directly to the forms $\varphi_n$ on Banach $E$.
\end{proof}

In $\ell^p$ for $1<p<\infty$, the unit vectors converge weakly to zero. Indeed each dual form has coefficients $a\in\ell^q$, so its value at $e_n$ is $a_n\to0$. Their norms stay equal to $1$: the limit's norm can be smaller. Oscillation gives a function-space example. On $[0,2\pi]$, put $f_n(x)=\operatorname{sgn}\sin(nx)$. Although $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$, these functions converge weak* to zero in $L^\infty=(L^1)^*$. The primitive $F_n(x)=\int_0^xf_n(t)\,dt$ rises and falls between $0$ and $\pi/n$ in each period. Thus for any interval $I=[a,b]$, $|\int_If_n|=|F_n(b)-F_n(a)|\le\pi/n$. Thus $\int f_ng\to0$ for step functions $g$. Approximate an arbitrary $g\in L^1$ by a step function $h$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.7) and use $|\int f_n(g-h)|\le\|g-h\|_1$. The fixed weight cannot follow the increasingly rapid oscillations.

\begin{theorem}[Banach--Alaoglu]\label{thm:alaoglu}
The dual's closed unit ball $B^*\subseteq E^*$ is weak* compact. If $E$ is separable, every norm-bounded sequence in $E^*$ admits a weak* convergent subsequence.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A form $\varphi\in B^*$ satisfies $|\varphi(x)|\le\|x\|$, placing the ball in
\[P=\prod_{x\in E}D_x,\qquad D_x=\{\lambda\in\mathbb K:|\lambda|\le\|x\|\}.\]
Each factor is compact. Tychonoff's theorem (Volume~7, Theorem~4.11) makes $P$ compact, and the induced topology on $B^*$ is weak*. The ball is closed in $P$: its points are exactly the $\omega\in P$ satisfying $\omega(x+y)=\omega(x)+\omega(y)$ and $\omega(\lambda x)=\lambda\omega(x)$ for all $x,y,\lambda$. Each equation defines a closed set, since it uses finitely many continuous coordinate maps. These equations give linearity, and membership in $P$ gives the norm bound.

For the sequential assertion, take a dense sequence $(x_k)$ in $E$ and a bound $\|\varphi_n\|\le M$. A diagonal extraction (Volume~7, Theorem~4.15) gives a subsequence $(\psi_n)$ convergent at every $x_k$. For arbitrary $x$, choose $\|x-x_k\|<\varepsilon$. Then
\[|\psi_n(x)-\psi_m(x)|\le2M\varepsilon+|\psi_n(x_k)-\psi_m(x_k)|.\]
Consequently $(\psi_n(x))$ is Cauchy at every $x$. Its pointwise limit $\psi$ is linear and satisfies $|\psi(x)|\le M\|x\|$, so it belongs to $E^*$ and $\psi_n\overset*\rightharpoonup\psi$.
\end{proof}

For compact metric $K$, the space $C(K)$ is separable (Volume~7, Lemma~12.6). A sequence of probability measures therefore has a weak* convergent subsequence in $C(K)^*=M(K)$. Testing the limit on nonnegative functions and on $1$ shows that it is still a probability measure. This recovers the compact-space case of Prokhorov's theorem (Volume~7, Theorem~12.7). The compact object in the general theorem is a closed dual ball; a bounded subset need only be relatively weak* compact. For reflexive Banach spaces, the closed unit ball is weakly compact. Exercise~\ref{ex:reflexiveweak} gives its sequential consequence under separability of the dual. These compactness arguments will supply minimisers and weak solutions of differential equations in Chapter~\ref{ch:distributions}.

\begin{think}
The unit vectors converge weakly to zero in $\ell^2$. Why do they fail to do so in $\ell^1$? Test them on the constant sequence $1\in\ell^\infty$. Schur proved in 1921 that every weakly convergent sequence in $\ell^1$ actually converges in norm.
\end{think}

\section{Linear forms, then orthogonality}

The closed unit ball detects finite dimension (Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszcompact}), while Exercise~\ref{ex:discontinuous} shows why linearity alone cannot guarantee continuity. Hahn--Banach supplies forms that measure each vector's norm and separate convex sets. Baire then controls operators: pointwise bounds give a uniform bound, surjections are open, and closed graphs give continuity. These arguments identify the duals of $L^p$ and $C(K)$ and produce continuous functions with divergent Fourier series. Banach--Alaoglu restores compactness to closed dual balls by changing the topology.

In $L^2$, a norm also measures angles through an inner product. Can a closed subspace have a nearest point to every vector, just as a plane in $\R^3$ does? Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert} proves that it can and uses the answer to construct Fourier expansions.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:banachlimit}
\emph{Assigning limits to bounded sequences.} On real $\ell^\infty$, set $p(x)=\limsup_n(x_1+\cdots+x_n)/n$ and $(Sx)_n=x_{n+1}$. (a) Prove sublinearity of $p$, then construct a linear form $L\le p$ that agrees with the ordinary limit on every convergent sequence. (b) Prove shift invariance $L(Sx)=L(x)$, positivity for $x\ge0$, and $\|L\|=1$. (c) Find $L(1,0,1,0,\dots)$. (d) Prove that no $a\in\ell^1$ represents $L$ by $L(x)=\sum_na_nx_n$. This shows $(\ell^\infty)^*\ne\ell^1$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:discontinuous}
In an infinite-dimensional normed space $E$, construct a discontinuous linear form using a Hamel basis (Volume~7, Theorem~1.21). Prove that its kernel is dense.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:twonorms}
(a) Suppose two norms make $E$ Banach and satisfy $\|x\|_a\le C\|x\|_b$. Prove the reverse inequality up to another constant. (b) Use this to prove that the integral norm $\|f\|_1=\int_0^1|f|$ does not make $C[0,1]$ complete.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:linftydual}
Construct $\Lambda\in L^\infty([0,1])^*$ agreeing with evaluation at zero on continuous functions. Show that it cannot be integration against any $g\in L^1$. Deduce that the canonical copy of $L^1$ is a proper subspace of its bidual $(L^\infty)^*$, so $L^1$ is not reflexive.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:mazur}
\emph{Mazur's lemma.} If $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ in a normed space, prove that convex combinations of the $x_n$ approximate $x$ in norm. Work out such combinations for the unit vectors in $\ell^2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:reflexiveweak}
For a reflexive Banach space $E$ with separable dual, prove that each bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence. Verify the assumptions for $L^p(\R^d)$ when $1<p<\infty$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:l1quotient}
\emph{A quotient description of every separable Banach space.} Let $(x_n)$ be dense in the closed unit ball of a separable Banach space $E$. Prove that $T(a)=\sum_na_nx_n$ defines a bounded map $\ell^1\to E$ of norm at most $1$. Show that every point of the closed unit ball has a preimage of norm at most $2$. Deduce that $T$ is onto and open and that $E$ is isomorphic to $\ell^1/\ker T$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c01a}
On Euclidean $\R^2$, let $T(x,y)=(2x,y/2)$. Compute $\|T\|$ and $\|T^{-1}\|$. Find unit vectors attaining both norms, and describe the image of the unit circle.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c01b}
In $C[0,1]$, put $f_n(x)=x^n$. Compute $\|f_n\|_\infty$, $\int_0^1f_n$ and $f_n(1)$. Prove that no subsequence converges weakly in $C[0,1]$, even though the integrals tend to zero.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c01c}
Let $C=\{(x,y):x,y\ge0\}$ and $b=(-1,-2)$. Find a continuous linear form $f$ and numbers $\alpha<\beta$ with $f(c)\le\alpha<\beta\le f(b)$ for every $c\in C$. Compute the norm of your form for the Euclidean norm.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c01d}
Prove that $e_n\rightharpoonup0$ in $c_0$. Then prove that $(e_n)$ has no weakly convergent subsequence in $\ell^1$. Explain why looking only at individual coordinates cannot distinguish these two behaviours.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:banachlimit}
\idea The Ces\`aro limsup gives a sublinear bound that already forces positivity and shift invariance. Hahn--Banach supplies a linear form under it.
\writeup (a) The inequalities $\limsup(a_n+b_n)\le\limsup a_n+\limsup b_n$ and $\limsup ta_n=t\limsup a_n$ for $t\ge0$ prove sublinearity. On the subspace $c$ of convergent sequences, take $g(x)=\lim x_n$. Ces\`aro means have the same limit (Volume~1, Chapter~3), so $g=p$ on $c$. Extend $g$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:hb}. (b) For $y=x-Sx$, its first $n$ terms have average $(x_1-x_{n+1})/n\to0$. Thus $p(y)=p(-y)=0$, forcing $L(y)=0$. If $x\ge0$, then $-L(x)=L(-x)\le p(-x)\le0$. Applying the bound to both signs of $x$ gives $|L(x)|\le\|x\|_\infty$. Since $L(1,1,\dots)=1$, its norm is $1$. (c) Here $x+Sx=(1,1,\dots)$, so $2L(x)=1$ and $L(x)=1/2$. (d) Each $e_n$ tends to zero, so $L(e_n)=0$. A representation by $a\in\ell^1$ would give every $a_n=0$ and hence $L=0$, contradicting its value on the constant sequence.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:discontinuous}
\idea Values on a basis determine a linear form. Assigning unbounded values on unit basis vectors prevents continuity; a closed kernel would force continuity back.
\writeup Normalise a Hamel basis $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ to unit length. Choose distinct $i_1,i_2,\dots$, assign $f(e_{i_n})=n$ and zero to the other basis vectors, then extend linearly. Its values on unit vectors are unbounded. Its kernel $H$ is a hyperplane: for $f(x_0)=1$, $E=H\oplus\mathbb Kx_0$. Since $\overline H$ is a subspace containing $H$, it is $H$ or $E$. If $H$ were closed, $d=d(x_0,H)>0$ and $|f(h+\lambda x_0)|=|\lambda|\le d^{-1}\|h+\lambda x_0\|$, as in Corollary~\ref{cor:hbnorm}(c). This would bound $f$, a contradiction. Thus $\overline H=E$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:twonorms}
\idea The bounded inverse theorem reverses the norm inequality. A sequence on which the two norms differ by an unbounded factor then detects incompleteness.
\writeup (a) Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:openmapping} to the identity from $(E,\|\cdot\|_b)$ onto $(E,\|\cdot\|_a)$. Its bounded inverse gives $\|x\|_b\le C'\|x\|_a$. (b) On $C[0,1]$, $\|f\|_1\le\|f\|_\infty$, and the supremum norm is complete. If the integral norm were complete too, (a) would give equivalence. But $f_n(x)=x^n$ has supremum norm $1$ and integral norm $1/(n+1)$, ruling out the reverse bound.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:linftydual}
\idea Point evaluation is bounded on continuous functions. Its extension to $L^\infty$ survives shrinking continuous tents, whereas integration against an $L^1$ density does not.
\writeup For continuous $f$, its maximum absolute value equals its essential supremum: a strictly smaller essential bound would, by continuity, be exceeded on an interval of positive measure. Thus $C[0,1]$ embeds isometrically in $L^\infty$. Evaluation at zero has norm $1$, and Corollary~\ref{cor:hbnorm}(a) extends it to $\Lambda$ of that norm. If $\Lambda(f)=\int fg$ for $g\in L^1$, choose continuous $0\le f_n\le1$ with $f_n(0)=1$ and $f_n=0$ on $[1/n,1]$. Dominated convergence gives $\int f_ng\to0$, since $f_ng\to0$ almost everywhere and $|f_ng|\le|g|$. But $\Lambda(f_n)=1$ for every $n$. Under $(L^1)^*=L^\infty$, the canonical image $J(L^1)$ consists precisely of these integral forms. It therefore misses $\Lambda$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:mazur}
\idea Separation would turn a positive norm distance from the convex hull into a persistent difference seen by one linear form. Weak convergence forbids that difference.
\writeup Regard $E$ as real. Continuous real-linear forms are real parts of complex-linear forms when the scalars are complex, so they preserve weak convergence. Let $C$ be the closed convex hull of $\{x_n\}$. If $x\notin C$, Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}(b), with the singleton $\{x\}$, gives a real form $f$ and $\alpha<\beta$ with $f(x_n)\le\alpha$ and $f(x)\ge\beta$. This contradicts $f(x_n)\to f(x)$. Hence $x\in C$. For unit vectors in $\ell^2$, the averages $(e_1+\cdots+e_n)/n$ have norm $1/\sqrt n\to0$, giving the required norm approximation explicitly.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:reflexiveweak}
\idea Reflexivity identifies $E$ with the dual of $E^*$. Weak* subsequences in that dual become weak subsequences in $E$.
\writeup A bound $\|x_n\|\le M$ also bounds $J(x_n)$ in $E^{**}=(E^*)^*$. Since $E^*$ is separable, Banach--Alaoglu gives a weak* convergent subsequence with limit $\Phi\in E^{**}$. Reflexivity writes $\Phi=J(x)$. Evaluating at each $\varphi\in E^*$ gives $\varphi(x_{n_k})\to\varphi(x)$, exactly weak convergence in $E$. For $L^p(\R^d)$, Proposition~\ref{prop:reflexive} gives reflexivity, and its dual is $L^q(\R^d)$ with finite $q$. Step functions on rational boxes with rational real and imaginary parts are dense, by Volume~7, Proposition~10.6 and Theorem~10.7, so that dual is separable. Eberlein--\v Smulian, quoted here, removes the dual-separability assumption. Reflexivity remains necessary: the sequence $e_n$ in $\ell^1$ has no weakly convergent subsequence.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:l1quotient}
\idea Repeatedly approximate a vector and then its rescaled remainder. The resulting coefficients form an absolutely summable geometric series.
\writeup For $a\in\ell^1$, completeness and $\|x_n\|\le1$ give a convergent series with $\|T(a)\|\le\sum_n|a_n|$. Thus $T$ is linear and $\|T\|\le1$. Given $\|y\|\le1$, choose $\|y-x_{n_1}\|<1/2$. Approximate $2(y-x_{n_1})$ by $x_{n_2}$ within $1/2$, obtaining an error below $1/4$ after dividing back by $2$. Induction gives
\[\left\|y-\sum_{k\le m}2^{1-k}x_{n_k}\right\|<2^{-m}.\]
Set $a=\sum_k2^{1-k}e_{n_k}$, adding coefficients if an index repeats. Then $\|a\|_1\le\sum_k2^{1-k}=2$ and $Ta=y$. Scaling proves surjectivity; the open mapping theorem gives openness. The induced map $\ell^1/\ker T\to E$ is a bounded bijection, and the same preimage estimate bounds its inverse by $2$.
\end{solution}

\idea A diagonal map stretches the two axes separately. Its largest stretch gives an upper bound that an axis vector attains.
\writeup We have $\|T(x,y)\|_2^2=4x^2+y^2/4\le4(x^2+y^2)$, with equality at $(1,0)$. Thus $\|T\|=2$. The inverse is $(x,y)\mapsto(x/2,2y)$, with norm $2$ attained at $(0,1)$. The unit circle maps to the ellipse $u^2/4+4v^2=1$, whose semiaxes have lengths $2$ and $1/2$.

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c01b}
\idea Weak convergence must preserve every point evaluation, not just one integral. The forced pointwise limit is discontinuous.
\writeup Directly, $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$, $\int_0^1f_n=1/(n+1)$ and $f_n(1)=1$. Each evaluation $f\mapsto f(x)$ is a bounded form of norm $1$ on $C[0,1]$. If a subsequence converged weakly to a continuous $f$, then for every $x<1$ we would have $f(x)=\lim_kx^{n_k}=0$, whereas $f(1)=1$. This contradicts continuity. Hence there is no weakly convergent subsequence.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c01c}
\idea A form with negative coefficients is bounded above on the first quadrant and positive at the specified point.
\writeup Take $f(x,y)=-x-y$, $\alpha=0$ and $\beta=3$. For $c\in C$, $f(c)\le0$, while $f(b)=3$. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $|f(x,y)|\le\sqrt2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, with equality at the unit vector $(-1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2)$. Thus $\|f\|=\sqrt2$.

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c01d}
\idea Weak convergence depends on the whole dual. The constant coefficient sequence is allowed for $\ell^1$, but it is not in the dual of $c_0$.
\writeup A form on $c_0$ has coefficients $a\in\ell^1$, so $\varphi(e_n)=a_n\to0$. This proves $e_n\rightharpoonup0$ there. In $\ell^1$, each coordinate form gives zero as the limit along any subsequence, so any weak subsequential limit would be the zero vector. But the bounded form $x\mapsto\sum_nx_n$, represented by the constant sequence $1\in\ell^\infty$, takes value $1$ at every $e_n$. No subsequence can converge weakly to zero. Both spaces have coordinatewise convergence to zero, so coordinate tests alone miss the distinction.
\end{solution}
