\chapter{The Spectral Theorem for Compact Operators}\label{ch:spectral}

\begin{center}\itshape
A compact self-adjoint operator chooses orthogonal directions of stretch.\\
The remaining directions form its kernel.
\end{center}

Pluck a string fixed at both ends. Its motion separates into modes, each with its own frequency. For a uniform string those modes are sines. Add a varying restoring term, and the modes change, but an orthogonal expansion still exists. The differential operator itself is unbounded; its inverse will be a compact integral operator, where we can prove the expansion directly.

Hilbert's work on integral equations in 1904--1910 extended symmetric-matrix methods to infinitely many coordinates. Weyl then used variational comparisons to study eigenvalue growth for differential equations. On an interval, the comparison is particularly concrete: adding a bounded potential moves each eigenvalue by a bounded amount, so the leading counting term still reveals the interval's length.

There is a probabilistic counterpart. A covariance kernel is positive, and Mercer's theorem expands a continuous positive kernel into eigenfunctions with absolute uniform convergence. For $\min(s,t)$ those eigenfunctions are half-integer sine modes. Independent Gaussian coefficients give the corresponding mean-square expansion of Brownian motion. The deterministic kernel proof comes before the random series.

Let $H$ be a complex Hilbert space. Statements using extrema over unit vectors assume $H\ne\{0\}$; otherwise there are no unit vectors. Empty expansions on the zero space are harmless, but an eigenvalue always requires a nonzero eigenvector.

\section{Self-adjoint operators}

Recall that $T$ is self-adjoint if $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$ for all $x,y$. In particular $\langle Tx,x\rangle$ is real. On unit vectors this quadratic form is the Rayleigh quotient. Its largest absolute value determines the operator norm.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sanorm}
For a bounded self-adjoint operator,
\[
\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|=1}|\langle Tx,x\rangle|.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Call the supremum $M$. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $M\le\|T\|$. Self-adjointness also gives the polarisation identity
\[
\langle T(x+y),x+y\rangle-\langle T(x-y),x-y\rangle
=4\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,y\rangle.
\]
The left side is at most $M(\|x+y\|^2+\|x-y\|^2)=2M(\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2)$. For unit $x$ with $Tx\ne0$, choose $y=Tx/\|Tx\|$. The right side becomes $4\|Tx\|$, while the bound is $4M$. Thus $\|Tx\|\le M$; the zero-image case satisfies the same bound. Taking the supremum proves equality.
\end{proof}

Call a bounded operator \emph{positive}, and write $T\ge0$, when $\langle Tx,x\rangle\ge0$ for all $x$. Over $\C$ this condition forces self-adjointness: polarisation recovers the full sesquilinear form from its real diagonal and gives $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:saspec}
Let $T$ be bounded and self-adjoint, and put
$m=\inf_{\|x\|=1}\langle Tx,x\rangle$,
$M=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\langle Tx,x\rangle$.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Every eigenvalue is real, and eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
\item[(b)] $\sigma(T)\subseteq[m,M]$, with both endpoints belonging to $\sigma(T)$.
\item[(c)] $\|T\|=\max(|m|,|M|)=r(T)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) A unit eigenvector gives $\lambda=\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\R$. For eigenvectors with distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda,\mu$, self-adjointness gives $(\lambda-\mu)\langle x,y\rangle=0$, proving orthogonality.

(b) First note a useful invertibility test. If $\|Ax\|\ge c\|x\|$ for some $c>0$ and $\ker A^*=\{0\}$, then $A$ is injective with closed range: convergence of $Ax_j$ forces $x_j$ to be Cauchy. Its range is also dense by Proposition~\ref{prop:adjoint}, so it is onto and its inverse has norm at most $1/c$.
For $\lambda=a+ib$ with $b\ne0$,
\[
\|(T-\lambda)x\|\|x\|\ge
|\operatorname{Im}\langle(T-\lambda)x,x\rangle|=|b|\|x\|^2.
\]
The same estimate holds for the adjoint $T-\bar\lambda$, so the test gives invertibility. For real $\lambda>M$, the quadratic bound for $\lambda-T$ gives $\|(\lambda-T)x\|\ge(\lambda-M)\|x\|$; this operator equals its adjoint, so the test applies again. The case $\lambda<m$ follows by reversing signs. This proves the spectral inclusion.
To reach the endpoint, let $A=M-T\ge0$. The positive Hermitian form $B(x,y)=\langle Ax,y\rangle$ satisfies Cauchy--Schwarz, including when it is only semidefinite, as proved in Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert}. Taking $y=Ax$ gives
\[
\|Ax\|^4\le\langle Ax,x\rangle\,\|A\|\|Ax\|^2,
\quad\text{hence}\quad
\|Ax\|^2\le\|A\|\langle Ax,x\rangle.
\]
The last inequality also holds when $Ax=0$. Choose unit $x_j$ with $\langle Tx_j,x_j\rangle\to M$. Then $Ax_j\to0$, excluding a bounded inverse of $A$. Thus $M\in\sigma(T)$. Applying the argument to $-T$ gives $m\in\sigma(T)$.

(c) The norm lemma gives the maximum of the two endpoint moduli, and (b) makes it the spectral radius.
\end{proof}

In particular $\|T^{2^j}\|=\|T\|^{2^j}$, by repeated use of $\|T^*T\|=\|T\|^2$ on self-adjoint powers. The proposition then shows that positivity is equivalent to self-adjointness with spectrum contained in $[0,\infty)$.

\section{The spectral theorem for compact operators}

Compactness turns an approximate extreme eigenvector into an actual one.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:eigenvalue}
On a nonzero Hilbert space, a compact self-adjoint operator $K$ has an eigenvalue equal to $\|K\|$ or $-\|K\|$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $K=0$, every nonzero vector is a zero eigenvector. Otherwise the norm lemma gives unit vectors $x_j$ with $\langle Kx_j,x_j\rangle\to\lambda$ for one fixed $\lambda\in\{\|K\|,-\|K\|\}$. Then
\[
\|Kx_j-\lambda x_j\|^2
\le2\lambda^2-2\lambda\langle Kx_j,x_j\rangle\longrightarrow0.
\]
Compactness supplies a subsequence with $Kx_j\to y$. It follows that $\lambda x_j\to y$ and $\|y\|=|\lambda|>0$. Continuity now gives $Ky=\lim_jK(\lambda x_j)=\lim_j\lambda Kx_j=\lambda y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[spectral theorem, Hilbert 1906]\label{thm:spectralcompact}
A compact self-adjoint operator $K$ has a finite or countable orthonormal family $(e_n)$ with nonzero real eigenvalues $\lambda_n$, ordered by decreasing absolute value, such that
\[
Kx=\sum_n\lambda_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n\quad(x\in H).
\]
If the family is infinite, $\lambda_n\to0$. Moreover
$H=\ker K\oplus\overline{\operatorname{span}}(e_n)$.
The list gives all nonzero eigenvalues with their finite multiplicities. If $H$ is separable, it has a Hilbert basis consisting of eigenvectors of $K$, including kernel vectors when needed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $K=0$, take an empty family. Otherwise choose a unit extreme eigenvector $e_1$ by the lemma. Its orthogonal complement is invariant, since $\langle Kx,e_1\rangle=\langle x,Ke_1\rangle=0$ for $x\perp e_1$. The restriction there is compact and self-adjoint. Repeat whenever that restriction is nonzero, obtaining $e_n$ with
$|\lambda_n|=\|K|_{\{e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1}\}^\perp}\|$.
These absolute values decrease.
If the process continues forever, they must tend to zero. Otherwise some $\delta>0$ would satisfy $|\lambda_n|\ge\delta$ for all $n$, while
$\|Ke_n-Ke_m\|^2=\lambda_n^2+\lambda_m^2\ge2\delta^2$ for $n\ne m$, contradicting compactness.

For arbitrary $x$, remove its first $N$ orthogonal components. The remainder has norm at most $\|x\|$ and lies in the next restricted space, giving
\[
\left\|Kx-\sum_{n\le N}\lambda_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n\right\|
\le|\lambda_{N+1}|\|x\|.
\]
If the process stopped, the remainder image is zero instead. This proves the expansion in both cases.
Put $F=\overline{\operatorname{span}}(e_n)$. A kernel vector is orthogonal to every $e_n$, because $\lambda_n\langle x,e_n\rangle=\langle Kx,e_n\rangle=0$. Conversely the expansion kills every vector in $F^\perp$. Thus $\ker K=F^\perp$, giving the orthogonal decomposition by Corollary~\ref{cor:orthdecomp}.

If $Kx=\mu x$ with $\mu\ne0$, then $x=\mu^{-1}Kx\in F$, and its coefficient at $e_n$ can be nonzero only when $\lambda_n=\mu$. At least one must be nonzero, so $\mu$ occurs in the list. Only finitely many listed values can equal a fixed nonzero $\mu$, because their absolute values tend to zero in the infinite case. The corresponding $e_n$ therefore span its entire eigenspace. Finally, for separable $H$, the closed kernel is separable and has a countable Hilbert basis (Corollary~\ref{cor:separable}). Adding that basis to the $e_n$ gives the asserted eigenbasis.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The unit sphere has ceased to be compact, but we did not need a convergent sequence on the sphere alone. We first obtained convergence of the images under $K$. The approximate eigenvector equation then pulled the vectors themselves into a convergent subsequence. That is the exact work done by compactness in this proof.
\end{beauty}

\begin{think}
Volterra's kernel $\mathbf1_{y<x}$ is square-integrable, so its operator is compact by Example~\ref{ex:hilbertschmidt}. It still has no eigenvalue, as Example~\ref{ex:volterraex} proved. Which estimate in the extreme-eigenvalue lemma depended on self-adjointness? Compactness alone cannot replace it.
\end{think}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:normal}
A compact normal operator on a separable Hilbert space has a Hilbert basis of eigenvectors. If the basis is infinite, its complex eigenvalues tend to zero along any enumeration.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Write $K=A+iB$ with $A=(K+K^*)/2$ and $B=(K-K^*)/(2i)$. Both are compact and self-adjoint, and normality gives $AB=BA$. Each eigenspace of $A$, including its kernel, is invariant under $B$. On each, apply the compact self-adjoint theorem to $B$, adding its kernel basis. The resulting vectors are simultaneous eigenvectors of $A,B$, hence of $K$. The spectral theorem for $A$ shows that together they fill $H$. If infinitely many of their $K$-eigenvalues had modulus at least a fixed $\delta>0$, the corresponding orthonormal images would remain at least $\sqrt2\delta$ apart. Compactness excludes this for every $\delta$, proving the convergence claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Min--max}

For a compact positive operator, the largest remaining stretch can be found by optimising quadratic forms on subspaces. This lets us compare eigenvalues without matching the eigenvectors of two different operators.

\begin{theorem}[Courant--Fischer]\label{thm:minmax}
Let $K\ge0$ be compact, and list its nonzero eigenvalues in decreasing order with multiplicities, appending zeros if the list is finite. Whenever $\dim H\ge n$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_n&=\max_{\dim V=n}\ \min_{x\in V,\ \|x\|=1}\langle Kx,x\rangle\\
&=\min_{\dim W\le n-1}\ \sup_{x\perp W,\ \|x\|=1}\langle Kx,x\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The spectral expansion gives $\langle Kx,x\rangle=\sum_k\lambda_k|\langle x,e_k\rangle|^2$. If the positive list ends before $n$, add kernel vectors to extend the first $n$ orthonormal directions; the dimension assumption guarantees enough of them. On the orthogonal complement of the first $n-1$ directions, Bessel's inequality gives $\langle Kx,x\rangle\le\lambda_n\|x\|^2$.
Every $n$-dimensional $V$ contains a nonzero vector orthogonal to those $n-1$ directions, by rank--nullity. Its minimum unit-vector value is therefore at most $\lambda_n$. On the span of the first $n$ directions, every unit-vector value is at least $\lambda_n$ and the last direction attains it. The minimum exists on each finite-dimensional sphere, so this proves the first formula with its maximum attained.
For the second formula, taking $W$ to be the first $n-1$ directions gives supremum at most $\lambda_n$. Any competing $W$ of dimension at most $n-1$ has an orthogonal complement meeting the first $n$-dimensional span nontrivially. At a unit vector in that intersection the quotient is at least $\lambda_n$. Both inequalities give the asserted minimum.
\end{proof}

When $H$ is finite-dimensional, use zero entries beyond its dimension in comparisons of eigenvalue lists. The variational formula itself is only asserted within the dimension range where its unit vectors exist.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:weylpert}
For compact positive $K,L$ and every index $n$, with that zero-extension convention,
$|\lambda_n(K)-\lambda_n(L)|\le\|K-L\|$.
If $L-K\ge0$, then also $\lambda_n(K)\le\lambda_n(L)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Within the dimension range, every unit vector satisfies
$\langle Kx,x\rangle\le\langle Lx,x\rangle+\|K-L\|$.
Take the subspace minimum and then the maximum in Courant--Fischer. Interchange $K,L$ for the absolute bound. In the ordered case the same comparison needs no error term. Beyond finite dimension both appended entries are zero, so the assertions remain true.
\end{proof}

The bound controls absolute eigenvalue errors in operator norm. It does not give a uniform relative-error guarantee near zero. The finite-matrix version was already proved in Chapter~\ref{linear:ch:spectral}; the Jordan block in Figure~\ref{fig:pseudospectra} shows why self-adjointness matters for such estimates. Exercise~\ref{ex:weylineq} proves a sum inequality for the compact positive case.

\section{Sturm--Liouville problems}

For a string fixed at the endpoints, a mode leads to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sl}
-u''+qu=\lambda u,\qquad u(0)=u(1)=0.
\end{equation}
Here $q$ is a continuous real potential; in the vibration model it supplies a local restoring term, and $\lambda=\omega^2$ is squared frequency. Assume $q\ge0$. A constant shift would handle any continuous real $q$ bounded below. Sturm and Liouville's work in 1836--1837 studied these eigenvalue problems. We will build the inverse equation first, as an integral operator with a continuous symmetric kernel.

The initial-value argument must also allow $q-\lambda$, which need not be non-negative. We prove it for any continuous complex coefficient.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ivp}
For a continuous $p:[0,1]\to\C$, any $x_0\in[0,1]$, and any $a,b\in\C$, there is a unique $u\in C^2([0,1])$ satisfying $u''=pu$, $u(x_0)=a$ and $u'(x_0)=b$. In particular this holds for $p=q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Integrating twice gives the equivalent equation $u=a+b(x-x_0)+Wu$, where
\[
Wu(x)=\int_{x_0}^x(x-t)p(t)u(t)\dd t.
\]
This formula uses oriented integrals when $x<x_0$. Induction, estimating along the segment between $x_0$ and $x$, gives
\[
|W^nu(x)|\le\frac{\|p\|_\infty^n|x-x_0|^{2n}}{(2n)!}\|u\|_\infty.
\]
The induction step uses $\int_0^s(s-t)t^{2n-2}\dd t=s^{2n}/((2n-1)2n)$. Thus $\sum_nW^n$ converges in operator norm and telescoping shows it inverts $I-W$. This gives a unique continuous solution. Differentiating the integral equation twice makes it $C^2$ and gives $u''=pu$ with the prescribed data. Conversely every classical solution satisfies that integral equation, proving uniqueness among them as well.
\end{proof}

As with Volterra's operator, the factorial estimate works on the whole interval; no smallness assumption on the coefficient is required.

\begin{lemma}[Green's function]\label{lem:green}
Let $\varphi''=q\varphi$, $\varphi(0)=0$, $\varphi'(0)=1$, and let $\psi''=q\psi$, $\psi(1)=0$, $\psi'(1)=-1$. The quantity $c=\psi\varphi'-\psi'\varphi$ is a nonzero constant. Define
\[
G(x,y)=\frac{\varphi(\min(x,y))\psi(\max(x,y))}{c}.
\]
For every continuous $f$, the function $\mathcal Gf(x)=\int_0^1G(x,y)f(y)\dd y$ is the unique $C^2$ solution of $-u''+qu=f$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Differentiating gives $c'=\psi\varphi''-\psi''\varphi=0$, and evaluating at $1$ gives $c=\varphi(1)$. If it vanished, integration by parts with the two zero boundary values would yield
\[
0=\int_0^1\bar\varphi(\varphi''-q\varphi)
=-\int_0^1(|\varphi'|^2+q|\varphi|^2).
\]
Non-negativity of $q$ would force $\varphi'=0$, contradicting its initial derivative. So division by $c$ is legitimate.
Split the integral at $x$:
\[
cu(x)=\psi(x)\int_0^x\varphi(y)f(y)\dd y
+\varphi(x)\int_x^1\psi(y)f(y)\dd y.
\]
The boundary values vanish. Differentiating once cancels the two moving-endpoint terms. Differentiating again gives
\[
cu''=qcu+(\psi'\varphi-\varphi'\psi)f=c(qu-f),
\]
proving the equation and the required differentiability. For uniqueness, the difference of two solutions has zero boundary values and satisfies the homogeneous equation. The same non-negative energy identity forces its derivative and then the function itself to vanish.
\end{proof}

At $q=0$, we have $\varphi(x)=x$, $\psi(x)=1-x$ and $c=1$. Thus $G(x,y)=\min(x,y)-xy$, also the Brownian-bridge covariance from Volume~4, Chapter~12. To prove that the inverse has no kernel, we need a dense family of smooth boundary-zero test functions.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sines}
The sequence $s_n(x)=\sqrt2\sin(n\pi x)$, $n\ge1$, is a Hilbert basis of $L^2([0,1])$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The identity $2\sin(n\pi x)\sin(m\pi x)=\cos((n-m)\pi x)-\cos((n+m)\pi x)$ gives orthonormality after integration. If $f$ is orthogonal to all the sines, extend it oddly to $[-1,1]$. Its integrals against all sine modes vanish by the assumption, and against all cosine modes, including the constant, by oddness. It is therefore orthogonal to all $e^{in\pi x}$. Rescale $t=\pi x$ and apply the completeness of the Fourier basis in Theorem~\ref{thm:fourierl2}. The extension vanishes almost everywhere, and so does $f$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Sturm--Liouville]\label{thm:sturm}
For real continuous $q\ge0$ on $[0,1]$, there is a Hilbert basis of real $C^2$ functions $e_n$ satisfying $e_n(0)=e_n(1)=0$ and $-e_n''+qe_n=\lambda_ne_n$, with
\[
0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\cdots,\qquad
\pi^2n^2\le\lambda_n\le\pi^2n^2+\max q.
\]
Every nonzero $C^2$ solution of~\eqref{eq:sl} is a multiple of one of these $e_n$, with the corresponding parameter $\lambda_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The compact inverse.} The real continuous symmetric kernel $G$ gives a compact self-adjoint operator on $L^2$ by Example~\ref{ex:hilbertschmidt}. Uniform continuity of $G$ gives
$|\mathcal Gf(x)-\mathcal Gf(x')|\le\|G(x,\cdot)-G(x',\cdot)\|_2\|f\|_2$,
so its images have continuous representatives. For continuous $f$, let $u=\mathcal Gf$. Integration by parts gives
\[
\langle\mathcal Gf,f\rangle=\int_0^1(|u'|^2+q|u|^2)\ge0.
\]
Continuous functions are dense in $L^2$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.7), so boundedness extends positivity to every $f\in L^2$.
If $\mathcal Gf=0$, take any finite sine sum $h$. Green's uniqueness gives $\mathcal G(-h''+qh)=h$, whence
$\langle f,h\rangle=\langle\mathcal Gf,-h''+qh\rangle=0$.
The sine lemma makes these tests dense, proving injectivity.

\emph{Modes and simplicity.} The compact spectral theorem now gives a Hilbert basis with $\mathcal Ge_n=\mu_ne_n$, $\mu_n>0$ and $\mu_n\to0$. There must be infinitely many, since the injective operator acts on infinite-dimensional $L^2$. First its mapping property makes $e_n$ continuous. Reusing Green's formula with this continuous input makes it $C^2$ with zero boundary values and differential eigenvalue $\lambda_n=1/\mu_n$.
Conversely, a solution $u$ of~\eqref{eq:sl} satisfies $u=\mathcal G(\lambda u)$. A nonzero solution has $\lambda\ne0$, and the inverse spectrum places it among these eigenfunctions.
For two solutions at the same $\lambda$, use the initial-value lemma with coefficient $p=q-\lambda$. A nonzero boundary-zero solution has nonzero derivative at zero; otherwise both initial data vanish. The combination $v'(0)u-u'(0)v$ has zero initial data and hence vanishes, proving proportionality. Thus all eigenvalues are simple and can be ordered increasingly. Real and imaginary parts solve the same real equation, so a nonzero real solution can be selected and normalised for each mode.

\emph{Variational comparison.} For a $C^2$ boundary-zero function, put
$Q_q(u)=\int_0^1(|u'|^2+q|u|^2)=\langle-u''+qu,u\rangle$.
Integration by parts gives $\langle-u''+qu,e_k\rangle=\lambda_k\langle u,e_k\rangle$. Parseval for the two $L^2$ functions therefore yields
\[
Q_q(u)=\sum_k\lambda_k|\langle u,e_k\rangle|^2.
\]
The paired-coefficient sum converges by Cauchy--Schwarz, so this identity is valid for every such $u$. Every $n$-dimensional space $V$ of these functions contains a unit vector orthogonal to $e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1}$. Its energy is at least $\lambda_n$, while the span of the first $n$ modes has maximum energy $\lambda_n$. Hence
\[
\lambda_n=\min_{\dim V=n}\ \max_{u\in V,\ \|u\|_2=1}Q_q(u),
\]
with the same class of boundary-zero spaces for every continuous $q$.
Now $Q_0(u)\le Q_q(u)\le Q_0(u)+(\max q)\|u\|_2^2$. Taking these extrema compares the respective eigenvalues. At $q=0$, positivity gives $\lambda>0$ and the equation has solutions proportional to $\sin(\sqrt\lambda x)$; the right boundary forces $\sqrt\lambda=n\pi$. Thus the reference eigenvalues are $\pi^2n^2$, giving both asserted bounds.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Weyl's law on an interval]\label{cor:weyllaw}
If $N(\lambda)$ counts the eigenvalues of~\eqref{eq:sl} at most $\lambda$, then $N(\lambda)\sim\sqrt\lambda/\pi$. For the zero-potential problem on an interval of length $\ell$, the eigenvalues are $\pi^2n^2/\ell^2$ and $N(\lambda)\sim\ell\sqrt\lambda/\pi$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $\lambda\ge\max q$, the bounds just proved give
\[
\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-\max q}}\pi-1
\le N(\lambda)\le\frac{\sqrt\lambda}\pi.
\]
Divide by $\sqrt\lambda/\pi$ and take limits. On $[0,\ell]$, the substitution $u(x)=v(x/\ell)$ converts the parameter to $\ell^2\lambda$, proving the rescaled formulas.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
The leading count recovers $\ell=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\pi N(\lambda)/\sqrt\lambda$. It cannot recover the bounded potential at that order. Inverse spectral theory asks what further data determine it. Ambartsumian proved in 1929 that a real continuous potential with Neumann eigenvalues exactly $n^2\pi^2$, $n\ge0$, must vanish. Borg's 1946 theorem recovers a real potential from two suitable spectra, for example the Dirichlet--Dirichlet and Dirichlet--Neumann spectra on the same interval; a single Dirichlet spectrum generally does not determine it. The boundary conditions are part of the data, not a detail to suppress.
\end{think}

\begin{remark}
Kac's question about hearing a drum asks whether a planar domain's Dirichlet spectrum determines its shape. Weyl's leading law recovers area. For sufficiently regular boundaries, further heat-trace coefficients encode boundary length and topological information. Gordon, Webb and Wolpert's 1992 isospectral polygons show that even the full eigenvalue list need not determine the shape. These are higher-dimensional results, quoted here to distinguish spectral invariants from uniqueness of reconstruction.
\end{remark}

\section{Hilbert--Schmidt operators and Mercer's theorem}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:hs}
For bounded $T$ on separable $H$, the sum $\|T\|_{HS}^2=\sum_n\|Te_n\|^2$, possibly infinite, is independent of the Hilbert basis. It equals the corresponding sum for $T^*$. If finite, $T$ is called Hilbert--Schmidt; it is compact and $\|T\|\le\|T\|_{HS}$.
On separable $L^2(\mu)$ with $\mu$ sigma-finite, these are exactly the integral operators with $L^2(\mu\otimes\mu)$ kernels, and $\|T_k\|_{HS}=\|k\|_2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For two orthonormal bases, Parseval and Tonelli give
\[
\sum_n\|Te_n\|^2=\sum_{n,m}|\langle Te_n,f_m\rangle|^2
=\sum_m\|T^*f_m\|^2.
\]
Fixing either basis proves independence from the other, and then equality for adjoints. Completing a unit vector $x$ to a basis gives $\|Tx\|^2\le\|T\|_{HS}^2$. If $P_N$ projects onto the first $N$ basis vectors, then
\[
\|T-TP_N\|^2\le\|T-TP_N\|_{HS}^2
=\sum_{n>N}\|Te_n\|^2\longrightarrow0.
\]
The finite-rank approximation proves compactness.
On $L^2(\mu)$, the product functions $e_m(x)\overline{e_n(y)}$ are orthonormal by Fubini. They are complete: finite linear combinations of finite-measure rectangle indicators are dense by Chapter~\ref{ch:operators}'s kernel approximation. Approximate each factor of a rectangle by finite basis sums, using $\|f(x)g(y)\|_2=\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$, to approximate its indicator by product basis sums. This proves density of their span.
For $k\in L^2$, the coefficient identity is
$\langle k,e_m\otimes\bar e_n\rangle=\langle T_ke_n,e_m\rangle$.
The integral is justified by Cauchy--Schwarz on the product space. Parseval in that product basis gives $\|k\|_2^2=\sum_{m,n}|\langle T_ke_n,e_m\rangle|^2=\|T_k\|_{HS}^2$. Conversely, a Hilbert--Schmidt $T$ supplies a square-summable array of these matrix entries, hence an $L^2$ kernel with that product-basis expansion. Its integral operator agrees with $T$ on every basis vector, and boundedness extends equality to all of $L^2$.
\end{proof}

An $L^2$ kernel is defined only almost everywhere on the product, so assigning values to its diagonal is generally meaningless. A continuous positive kernel has a well-defined diagonal, and that extra structure gives a sum formula for its eigenvalues. Require a nondegenerate interval $a<b$: on a one-point interval, Lebesgue integration cannot recover a nonzero continuous kernel.

\begin{theorem}[Mercer, 1909]\label{thm:mercer}
Let $I=[a,b]$ with $a<b$, and let $k\in C(I\times I)$ be a positive kernel: $k(y,x)=\overline{k(x,y)}$ and $\sum_{i,j}\bar c_ic_jk(x_i,x_j)\ge0$ for every finite choice of points and complex coefficients. For its integral operator $T$ on $L^2(I)$, the nonzero spectral modes of Theorem~\ref{thm:spectralcompact} have $\lambda_n>0$ and continuous representatives $e_n$. They satisfy
\[
k(x,y)=\sum_n\lambda_ne_n(x)\overline{e_n(y)},
\]
with absolute uniform convergence on $I\times I$. In particular, $\sum_n\lambda_n=\int_Ik(x,x)\dd x$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Positivity and continuous modes.} For continuous $f$, the quadratic integral $\langle Tf,f\rangle$ is a limit of non-negative Riemann sums from the kernel hypothesis, using coefficients given by sampled values of $f$ times the grid widths. Density of continuous functions makes $T\ge0$. The continuous kernel maps $L^2$ into $C(I)$ by the same uniform-continuity estimate used for Green's operator. Hence a nonzero eigenvector $e_n=\lambda_n^{-1}Te_n$ is continuous, with $\lambda_n>0$.

\emph{Control of the diagonal.} Remove the first $N$ spectral terms to form the continuous kernel $k_N$. Its operator is positive by the compact spectral expansion. A continuous Hermitian kernel with positive operator must have non-negative diagonal: if $\kappa(x_0,x_0)<0$, continuity makes its real part negative on $U\times U$ for a small relative interval $U$ of positive length. Testing the operator on $\mathbf1_U$ contradicts positivity. Thus
\[
\sum_{n\le N}\lambda_n|e_n(x)|^2\le k(x,x)\le C:=\max_Ik(x,x).
\]

\emph{Identify the limit.} Cauchy--Schwarz gives, for $M\le N$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mercercs}
\sum_{n=M}^N\lambda_n|e_n(x)e_n(y)|
\le\left(\sum_{n=M}^N\lambda_n|e_n(x)|^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_{n=M}^N\lambda_n|e_n(y)|^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $x$, the first factor tends to zero as $M\to\infty$, while the second is at most $\sqrt C$. Hence the kernel series converges uniformly in $y$ to a continuous function $\tilde k(x,y)$ for each fixed $x$.
We also need the spectral series for $Tf$ to converge uniformly in $x$. Its tail is at most
\[
\sqrt C\left(\sum_{n\ge M}\lambda_n|\langle f,e_n\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2},
\]
by Cauchy--Schwarz. This tends to zero by Bessel's inequality and boundedness of the $\lambda_n$. The uniform limit is continuous and equals $Tf$ in $L^2$ by the spectral theorem, hence equals its continuous representative everywhere. Integrating the uniformly convergent row series against $f\in L^2(I)\subset L^1(I)$ now gives
\[
\int_I\tilde k(x,y)f(y)\dd y
=\sum_n\lambda_ne_n(x)\langle f,e_n\rangle=Tf(x).
\]
The same identity holds with $k$ in place of $\tilde k$. Test their continuous row difference against its conjugate to see that it vanishes in $L^2$, and hence everywhere. In particular the diagonal series converges pointwise to $k(x,x)$.

\emph{Uniform tails and trace.} Its continuous partial sums increase to the continuous diagonal on compact $I$. Dini's theorem gives uniform convergence (Volume~7, Exercise~4.1). Directly, for each $\varepsilon>0$ the closed sets where the tail is at least $\varepsilon$ decrease to the empty set, so compactness makes one empty. The two diagonal tails in~\eqref{eq:mercercs} therefore tend to zero uniformly. This proves uniform absolute convergence on the product. Integrate the diagonal series to obtain $\int_Ik(x,x)\dd x=\sum_n\lambda_n$, since every $e_n$ has squared integral norm one.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[the covariance of Brownian motion]\label{ex:minkernel}
The kernel $k(s,t)=\min(s,t)$ is positive on $[0,1]$, since
\[
\sum_{i,j}\bar c_ic_j\min(t_i,t_j)
=\int_0^1\left|\sum_jc_j\mathbf1_{[0,t_j]}(u)\right|^2\dd u\ge0.
\]
For a nonzero eigenvalue $Tf=\lambda f$, continuity and splitting the integral give
\[
\lambda f(s)=\int_0^stf(t)\dd t+s\int_s^1f(t)\dd t.
\]
Differentiate to get $f(0)=0$, $f'(1)=0$ and $\lambda f''=-f$. Positivity gives $\lambda>0$. Thus the eigenfunction is a multiple of $\sin(s/\sqrt\lambda)$, and the derivative condition forces $1/\sqrt\lambda=(n-1/2)\pi$. Conversely each such sine satisfies the integral equation: the difference between its two sides has zero second derivative, zero value at zero and zero derivative at one. Normalising gives
\[
\lambda_n=\frac1{(n-1/2)^2\pi^2},\qquad
e_n(s)=\sqrt2\sin((n-1/2)\pi s).
\]
The compact spectral theorem lists all nonzero modes, so Mercer gives the absolute uniformly convergent identity
\[
\min(s,t)=\sum_{n\ge1}
\frac{2\sin((n-1/2)\pi s)\sin((n-1/2)\pi t)}{(n-1/2)^2\pi^2}.
\]
Putting $s=t=1$ gives $\sum_{n\ge1}(2n-1)^{-2}=\pi^2/8$, the odd-denominator part of Basel's sum.
\end{example}

Brownian motion from Volume~4, Chapter~12 is centred Gaussian with covariance $\min(s,t)$. Its coefficients
$\xi_n=\lambda_n^{-1/2}\int_0^1B_te_n(t)\dd t$
are jointly Gaussian: approximate any finite linear combination of these integrals by Riemann sums, which are Gaussian, and use the continuous covariance to pass to the mean-square limit. Their covariance is
\[
\mathbb E[\xi_n\xi_m]=
\frac{\int_0^1\!\int_0^1\min(s,t)e_n(s)e_m(t)\dd s\dd t}
{\sqrt{\lambda_n\lambda_m}}=\delta_{nm}.
\]
Thus they are independent standard Gaussians. The resulting \emph{Karhunen--Lo\`eve expansion} is $\sum_n\xi_n\sqrt{\lambda_n}e_n(t)$. Exercise~\ref{ex:kl} proves its mean-square convergence at fixed times and its covariance identity. Uniform almost-sure convergence is a stronger claim, proved later in Volume~12, Chapter~1.
The total integrated variance is $\int_0^1t\dd t=1/2$. Its first mode carries the fraction $\lambda_1/(1/2)=8/\pi^2$, about $81\%$. This is the function-space version of the variance fractions in principal component analysis.

\section{The continuous functional calculus}

For compact self-adjoint $K$, its orthogonal expansion already defines bounded functions of it:
\[
f(K)x=\sum_nf(\lambda_n)\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n+f(0)P_{\ker K}x.
\]
The series converges for each $x$ by Bessel's inequality. If zero is absent from the spectrum, the kernel is zero and its displayed term is omitted, so no value $f(0)$ is needed. But multiplication by $x$ on $L^2([0,1])$ has no eigenvectors. To define continuous functions of such an operator, approximate the function by polynomials instead.

\begin{theorem}[continuous functional calculus]\label{thm:funcalc}
For a bounded self-adjoint $T$, there is a unique linear multiplicative map $C(\sigma(T))\to\mathcal L(H)$, written $f\mapsto f(T)$, sending $1$ to $I$ and the coordinate function to $T$, with
$\|f(T)\|=\max_{\sigma(T)}|f|$.
It satisfies $f(T)^*=\bar f(T)$, commutes with every operator commuting with $T$, and is positive when $f\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Polynomial norm.} First prove $\sigma(p(T))=p(\sigma(T))$. For nonconstant $p$, factor $p(z)-\mu=c\prod_j(z-z_j)$ over $\C$. Its operator factors commute. A product of commuting factors is invertible exactly when each is: for two factors, $AB=BA$ gives a right inverse $B(AB)^{-1}$ and a left inverse $(BA)^{-1}B$ for $A$, which must coincide; proceed through the factors. Thus $p(T)-\mu I$ is singular exactly when some root $z_j$ is spectral for $T$. Constant polynomials satisfy the same identity directly.
Write $\bar p$ for coefficientwise conjugation. Self-adjointness gives $p(T)^*=\bar p(T)$, so $(\bar pp)(T)$ is self-adjoint. The adjoint norm identity and Proposition~\ref{prop:saspec} imply
\[
\|p(T)\|^2=\|p(T)^*p(T)\|
=r((\bar pp)(T))=\max_{t\in\sigma(T)}|p(t)|^2,
\]
since every spectral $t$ is real.

\emph{Continuous extension.} Two polynomials agreeing on $\sigma(T)$ therefore give the same operator. Polynomial restrictions are dense in $C(\sigma(T))$: apply the compact-subset Weierstrass theorem (Volume~7, Theorem~4.19) to real and imaginary parts. The norm identity and completeness of $\mathcal L(H)$ extend the polynomial map uniquely to an isometry. Linearity, multiplication, conjugation and commutation pass to norm limits. Any map with the asserted properties agrees on polynomials and is continuous by its isometry, proving uniqueness.
If $f\ge0$, take the continuous real function $g=\sqrt f$. Then $f(T)=g(T)^*g(T)$, whose quadratic form is $\|g(T)x\|^2\ge0$. This proves positivity.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[square roots]\label{cor:sqrt}
Each bounded positive operator $A$ has exactly one bounded positive square root $B$, satisfying $B^2=A$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply the continuous calculus to $\sqrt t$ on $\sigma(A)\subset[0,\infty)$, producing a positive $C$ with $C^2=A$. Suppose another positive $B$ squares to $A$. It commutes with $A$, hence with $C$. Put $S=B-C$, also self-adjoint. Since $B^2=C^2$,
\[
SBS+SCS=S(B+C)S=0.
\]
Both summands are positive: for example $\langle SBSx,x\rangle=\langle BSx,Sx\rangle\ge0$. Their sum zero forces each quadratic form to vanish everywhere, and polarisation makes both operators zero. Subtracting gives $S^3=S(B-C)S=0$. Repeated use of the self-adjoint norm identity gives
$\|S\|^4=\|S^4\|=\|SS^3\|=0$,
so $B=C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The full bounded self-adjoint spectral theorem gives a unitary identification with multiplication by a bounded real function on some $L^2$ space. Another formulation uses a projection-valued measure $E$ on $\sigma(T)$ and writes $T=\int\lambda\dd E(\lambda)$, defining $f(T)$ for bounded Borel $f$ too. That theorem needs more than our polynomial completion argument; one route combines it with Riesz--Markov (Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszmarkov}). Unbounded self-adjoint operators require further domain arguments, developed in von Neumann's work. Reed--Simon and Rudin in the sources treat these extensions. None is used in the proofs above.
\end{remark}

\section{Modes on the line}

The string's counting law recovered its length from high frequencies. Its inverse supplied the compact operator to which the spectral theorem applied. Covariance kernels gave another use for the same eigenfunction expansion, while polynomial approximation defined continuous functions of self-adjoint operators even without eigenvectors.

All our trigonometric modes so far have been discrete, indexed by integers. On the real line frequencies vary continuously. Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier} builds the Fourier transform that replaces a sum of modes by an integral.

A further circle example appears in Exercise~\ref{ex:toeplitz}: convolution by the Poisson kernel has Fourier modes as eigenvectors. Its trace is a geometric-series sum, so it gives a direct check of Mercer's diagonal formula.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:kl}
\emph{Karhunen--Lo\`eve expansion.} Take independent standard Gaussians $\xi_n$ and the modes $\lambda_n,e_n$ of Example~\ref{ex:minkernel}. Prove convergence in $L^2(P)$ of $X_t=\sum_n\xi_n\sqrt{\lambda_n}e_n(t)$ for each fixed $t$. Show that $X_0=0$, that the covariance is $\min(s,t)$, and that each $X_t$ is Gaussian. Find the analogous expansion for covariance $\min(s,t)-st$, the Brownian bridge from Volume~4, Chapter~12.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:toeplitz}
\emph{Convolution kernels.} Use $\mathbb T=\R/\Z$ in this exercise. For $g\in L^2(\mathbb T)$ define $Tf(x)=\int_0^1g(x-y)f(y)\dd y$. Show that $e_n(x)=e^{2\pi inx}$ has eigenvalue $\hat g(n)=\int_0^1g(x)e^{-2\pi inx}\dd x$. Characterise self-adjointness and positivity of $T$. For $P_r(x)=\sum_{n\in\Z}r^{|n|}e^{2\pi inx}$ with $0<r<1$, apply Mercer to the convolution kernel and verify its trace formula.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:weylineq}
\emph{Weyl inequalities.} For positive compact $K,L$, prove $\lambda_{m+n-1}(K+L)\le\lambda_m(K)+\lambda_n(L)$ for all positive indices, using the zero-extension convention in finite dimension. If positive $F$ has rank $r$, deduce $\lambda_n(K)\le\lambda_n(K+F)$ and $\lambda_{n+r}(K+F)\le\lambda_n(K)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:graphlaplacian}
\emph{Graph Laplacian.} Let $L=D-A$ for a finite simple graph. Prove $\langle Lx,x\rangle=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}|x_i-x_j|^2$ and identify its kernel as the functions constant on each connected component. For a connected graph with at least two vertices, derive the minimum Rayleigh quotient on $\sum_Vx_i=0$ for its second-smallest eigenvalue. Compute the cycle spectrum and show $\mu_2(C_n)\sim4\pi^2/n^2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:polar}
\emph{Polar decomposition.} Given bounded $T$, put $|T|=\sqrt{T^*T}$. Prove $\||T|x\|=\|Tx\|$. Construct the unique bounded $U$ satisfying $T=U|T|$ and $U=0$ on $\ker T$, and show that it is isometric on $(\ker T)^\perp$. Show it is unitary when $T$ is invertible, and compute these factors for the unilateral shift.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:basel}
\emph{Basel from a string.} Prove that $G(s,t)=\min(s,t)-st$ is a positive kernel. Use the zero-potential string and Mercer's trace formula to derive $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}=\pi^2/6$. Then use the Hilbert--Schmidt norm to derive $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-4}=\pi^4/90$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:volterranorm}
\emph{Volterra's norm.} Compute the adjoint of $Vf(x)=\int_0^xf$ on $L^2([0,1])$ and the kernel of $V^*V$. Identify its eigenvalues from Example~\ref{ex:minkernel}, and conclude $\|V\|=2/\pi$. Relate the answer to singular values rather than to the empty eigenvalue set of $V$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c09a}
Define $Ke_n=(-1)^{n+1}e_n/n$ on $\ell^2$. Prove compactness and self-adjointness, find its spectrum and decide whether zero is an eigenvalue. Compute the spectrum of $K^2$, and determine whether $K$ is Hilbert--Schmidt.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c09b}
For constant potential $q=c>0$, compute the Green kernel explicitly using $\alpha=\sqrt c$. Show that its limit as $c\downarrow0$ is $\min(x,y)-xy$. Find all string eigenvalues and compare them with the two general bounds.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c09c}
For $k(x,y)=1+xy$ on $[0,1]^2$, prove positivity and determine its two nonzero eigenvalues and normalised eigenfunctions. Write its finite Mercer expansion and verify both the trace and Hilbert--Schmidt norm formulas.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c09d}
Let $A=M_x$ on $L^2([0,1])$. Identify its positive square root and calculate $\|\sqrt A-A\|$. Prove that $A^nf\to0$ for every fixed $f\in L^2$, although $\|A^n\|=1$ for every $n$. Explain the difference between these two convergence assertions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:kl}
\idea Each covariance mode becomes a random term, and independent amplitudes make their covariances add.
\writeup For a fixed $t$, the partial sums $S_N(t)$ have squared tail norm
$\mathbb E|S_N(t)-S_M(t)|^2=\sum_{M<n\le N}\lambda_ne_n(t)^2$.
Mercer's diagonal identifies the full sum as $t$, so completeness of $L^2(P)$ gives a limit $X_t$ (Volume~7, Theorem~10.4). The means are zero, $X_0=0$, and continuity of the $L^2$ inner product gives
\[
\mathbb E[X_sX_t]=\lim_N\sum_{n\le N}\lambda_ne_n(s)e_n(t)=\min(s,t).
\]
For Gaussianity, $S_N(t)$ is Gaussian with variance tending to $t$, and
$|\mathbb E e^{iuX_t}-\mathbb E e^{iuS_N(t)}|\le|u|\|X_t-S_N(t)\|_2\to0$.
Its limiting characteristic function is $e^{-u^2t/2}$. Apply the same argument to each finite linear combination of the $X_t$ to prove joint Gaussianity as well.
For the bridge kernel, the identity
\[
\min(s,t)-st=\int_0^1(\mathbf1_{[0,s]}(u)-s)
(\mathbf1_{[0,t]}(u)-t)\dd u
\]
proves positivity directly. It is also Green's kernel at $q=0$. Its inverse-string modes are $\mu_n=1/(n\pi)^2$ and $\sqrt2\sin(n\pi t)$, so the same mean-square argument gives
\[
X_t=\sum_{n\ge1}\xi_n\frac{\sqrt2\sin(n\pi t)}{n\pi}
\]
with bridge covariance. Adding $t\xi_0$ for an independent standard Gaussian gives a centred Gaussian process with covariance $st+\min(s,t)-st=\min(s,t)$, hence Brownian finite-dimensional laws. This is the sine-series construction associated with Paley and Wiener; uniform path convergence is a separate result, not supplied by the fixed-time calculation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:toeplitz}
\idea In a complete Fourier basis, convolution has a diagonal matrix even when the basis is infinite.
\writeup Periodicity and Tonelli give $\int_0^1\int_0^1|g(x-y)|^2\dd x\dd y=\|g\|_2^2$, so this is a bounded compact integral operator. Substitution over one period gives $Te_n(x)=e^{2\pi inx}\int_0^1g(u)e^{-2\pi inu}\dd u=\hat g(n)e_n(x)$.
Completeness of the rescaled Fourier basis, from Theorem~\ref{thm:fourierl2}, gives $Tf=\sum_n\hat g(n)\langle f,e_n\rangle e_n$. Adjointing conjugates these diagonal entries. Thus self-adjointness means all $\hat g(n)$ are real, equivalently $g(x)=\overline{g(-x)}$ almost everywhere by uniqueness of Fourier coefficients. Positivity means all $\hat g(n)\ge0$: testing basis vectors proves necessity, and summing the diagonal quadratic form proves sufficiency.
For the Poisson kernel, absolute uniform convergence gives
\[
P_r(x)=\frac{1-r^2}{1-2r\cos(2\pi x)+r^2}.
\]
Its convolution kernel expands as $\sum_nr^{|n|}e_n(x)\overline{e_n(y)}$ and is positive, because every finite kernel quadratic form is $\sum_nr^{|n|}|\sum_jc_j\overline{e_n(x_j)}|^2\ge0$. Mercer applies. The eigenvalue sum is $1+2\sum_{n\ge1}r^n=(1+r)/(1-r)$, and the diagonal integral is $P_r(0)$, the same number. This checks both absolute summability and the trace value.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:weylineq}
\idea An upper bound on the quadratic form survives after excluding the union of two sets of large directions.
\writeup If finite dimension is smaller than $m+n-1$, the appended left side is zero and the inequality follows from positivity. Otherwise form $W$ from the first $m-1$ spectral directions of $K$ and first $n-1$ of $L$, adding no more than those that exist. Its dimension is at most $m+n-2$. For a unit $x\perp W$, the spectral expansions give $\langle Kx,x\rangle\le\lambda_m(K)$ and $\langle Lx,x\rangle\le\lambda_n(L)$. The min--sup formula therefore bounds $\lambda_{m+n-1}(K+L)$ by their sum.
For a rank-$r$ positive $F$, its $(r+1)$st entry is zero. Apply the sum bound with indices $n,r+1$ to get $\lambda_{n+r}(K+F)\le\lambda_n(K)$. Monotonicity under $K\le K+F$ gives the other inequality, with all appended-zero cases included.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:graphlaplacian}
\idea Zero edge energy means no change along any connected path.
\writeup Expanding $\langle(D-A)x,x\rangle$ groups each edge into
$|x_i|^2+|x_j|^2-x_j\bar x_i-x_i\bar x_j=|x_i-x_j|^2$.
This proves positivity. If $Lx=0$, the energy is zero, so coordinates agree across every edge and along every path. Conversely a function constant on components satisfies each coordinate equation $d_ix_i-\sum_{j\sim i}x_j=0$. This identifies the kernel without needing an additional positive-form argument.
For a connected graph on at least two vertices, choose an orthonormal eigenbasis with first vector $\mathbf1/\sqrt{|V|}$. On its orthogonal complement, the quotient is a weighted average of the remaining eigenvalues, minimised at an eigenvector for $\mu_2$. This gives the formula in the question.
For the cycle, the vector $x_j=\omega^{jk}$ with $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$ satisfies $Lx=(2-2\cos(2\pi k/n))x$. Finite geometric sums make these $n$ vectors orthogonal, as already used in Example~\ref{linear:exa:spectra}, so all eigenvalues are listed. The elementary cosine limit gives $\mu_2\sim4\pi^2/n^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:polar}
\idea Match the lengths on the range of $|T|$ before extending the orientation map.
\writeup Since $|T|$ is positive and squares to $T^*T$,
$\||T|x\|^2=\langle T^*Tx,x\rangle=\|Tx\|^2$.
Thus $\ker|T|=\ker T$. The rule $U_0(|T|x)=Tx$ is well defined, linear and isometric on $\operatorname{ran}|T|$. It extends uniquely to its closure, with image $\overline{\operatorname{ran}T}$: an isometry from a complete space has closed image, and the original range is dense in that closure. The adjoint range identity gives $\overline{\operatorname{ran}|T|}=(\ker T)^\perp$. Define $U$ by this extension there and by zero on $\ker T$. The orthogonal decomposition makes it bounded, and $U|T|=T$. Any other such bounded operator agrees on the range of $|T|$, hence its closure, and is prescribed as zero on the complementary kernel, proving uniqueness.
If $T$ is invertible, the initial and final spaces are both $H$, so $U$ is unitary. For $S$, $S^*S=I$ gives $|S|=I$ and $U=S$. Its failure to be onto shows why a general infinite-dimensional polar factor is only a partial isometry, not necessarily unitary. Here that term means exactly an isometry on the kernel's orthogonal complement and zero on the kernel.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:basel}
\idea The diagonal gives a first eigenvalue sum; the squared kernel gives a second.
\writeup The centred-indicator identity is
$G(s,t)=\int_0^1(\mathbf1_{[0,s]}-s)(\mathbf1_{[0,t]}-t)$.
A finite quadratic combination is the integral of an absolute square, proving positive-kernel status. At $q=0$, the inverse-string eigenvalues are $1/(\pi^2n^2)$. Mercer's trace gives
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{\pi^2n^2}=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\dd x=\frac16.
\]
For the Hilbert--Schmidt norm, use both its eigenbasis and its kernel:
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{\pi^4n^4}
=2\int_0^1\int_0^xy^2(1-x)^2\dd y\dd x
=\frac23\int_0^1x^3(1-x)^2\dd x=\frac1{90}.
\]
The last integral equals $1/60$, by expanding $x^3-2x^4+x^5$ and integrating. Multiplication by the displayed powers of $\pi$ gives both Basel identities.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:volterranorm}
\idea Reflection converts the positive product $V^*V$ into the Brownian covariance operator.
\writeup Fubini gives
$\langle Vf,g\rangle=\int_0^1f(y)\overline{\int_y^1g(x)\dd x}\dd y$,
so $V^*g(y)=\int_y^1g$. A second change of order gives
\[
V^*Vf(x)=\int_0^1(1-\max(x,s))f(s)\dd s.
\]
Reflection $Rf(x)=f(1-x)$ is unitary and turns $\min(x,s)$ into $\min(1-x,1-s)=1-\max(x,s)$. Thus $V^*V$ has eigenvalues $1/((n-1/2)^2\pi^2)$. Its norm is the largest, $4/\pi^2$, and $\|V\|^2=\|V^*V\|$ gives $\|V\|=2/\pi$. The singular values of $V$ are the square roots $1/((n-1/2)\pi)$ of these eigenvalues; the eigenvalues of $V^*V$ themselves are their squares. This norm coexists with $r(V)=0$ and no eigenvalues of $V$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c09a}
\idea Real diagonal entries give self-adjointness; their decay gives compactness and the spectral accumulation point.
\writeup The coordinate formula makes $K=K^*$. Its first-$N$ truncation differs in norm by $1/(N+1)$, proving compactness. Each listed entry $(-1)^{n+1}/n$ is an eigenvalue, and no other nonzero value is spectral: away from the entry set and zero, the reciprocal diagonal is uniformly bounded, by the finite-tail argument of Exercise~\ref{ex:v8c08b}. Zero is spectral since the entries tend to zero and the spectrum is closed. It is not an eigenvalue because none of the diagonal entries vanishes.
Squaring gives diagonal $1/n^2$, so $\sigma(K^2)=\{0\}\cup\{1/n^2:n\ge1\}$, also by polynomial spectral mapping. Finally $\|K\|_{HS}^2=\sum_n1/n^2=\pi^2/6$ by Exercise~\ref{ex:basel}. The operator is therefore Hilbert--Schmidt.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c09b}
\idea Constant coefficients leave the sine modes intact and permit explicit hyperbolic fundamental solutions.
\writeup Put $\alpha=\sqrt c$. The left fundamental solution is $\sinh(\alpha x)/\alpha$ and the right one $\sinh(\alpha(1-x))/\alpha$. Their normalising constant is the left solution at $1$, namely $\sinh\alpha/\alpha$. Thus
\[
G_c(x,y)=\frac{\sinh(\alpha\min(x,y))\sinh(\alpha(1-\max(x,y)))}{\alpha\sinh\alpha}.
\]
Divide each numerator factor by $\alpha$ and the denominator by $\alpha^2$. As $\alpha\downarrow0$, $\sinh(\alpha t)/\alpha\to t$ uniformly for $t\in[0,1]$, and $\sinh\alpha/\alpha\to1$. The kernel therefore tends uniformly to $\min(x,y)(1-\max(x,y))=\min(x,y)-xy$.
The differential equation becomes $-u''=(\lambda-c)u$, with Dirichlet modes $\sqrt2\sin(n\pi x)$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_n=\pi^2n^2+c$. These attain the general upper bound for every $n$, and exceed the lower bound by exactly $c$. Letting $c=0$ attains the lower bound too.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c09c}
\idea The kernel is the sum of two positive rank-one kernels; only a two-dimensional eigenproblem remains.
\writeup A finite kernel quadratic form equals $|\sum_jc_j|^2+|\sum_jc_jx_j|^2\ge0$. The operator is
$Tf=\int_0^1f+x\int_0^1yf(y)\dd y$,
so its range is $\operatorname{span}\{1,x\}$ and its kernel is that span's orthogonal complement. On $a+bx$ its coefficient matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1/3\end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic equation has trace $4/3$ and determinant $1/12$, giving
\[
\lambda_\pm=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{13}}6>0,\qquad
b_\pm=2(\lambda_\pm-1)=\frac{-2\pm\sqrt{13}}3.
\]
Set $e_\pm(x)=(1+b_\pm x)/(1+b_\pm+b_\pm^2/3)^{1/2}$. The denominator is the $L^2$ norm, and substitution in the coefficient equations verifies the eigenvalues. Distinct self-adjoint modes are orthogonal. Mercer therefore gives the finite identity
$1+xy=\lambda_+e_+(x)e_+(y)+\lambda_-e_-(x)e_-(y)$.
Its trace check is $\lambda_++\lambda_-=4/3=\int_0^1(1+x^2)\dd x$. Its squared Hilbert--Schmidt norm is
\[
\lambda_+^2+\lambda_-^2=(4/3)^2-2(1/12)=29/18
=\int_0^1\int_0^1(1+xy)^2\dd x\dd y.
\]
Expanding that integral gives $1+1/2+1/9=29/18$, as required.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c09d}
\idea Pointwise scalar functions describe these multipliers, but pointwise decay does not imply decay of their supremum norms.
\writeup Multiplication by $\sqrt x$ is positive and its square is $A$, so uniqueness of positive roots identifies it as $\sqrt A$. The difference multiplies by $\sqrt x-x$. Writing $s=\sqrt x$ gives $s-s^2=1/4-(s-1/2)^2$, with maximum $1/4$ at $x=1/4$. The multiplier norm is its essential supremum, hence $\|\sqrt A-A\|=1/4$.
For every fixed $f\in L^2$, $\|A^nf\|_2^2=\int_0^1x^{2n}|f(x)|^2\dd x\to0$ by dominated convergence: the integrand is measurable, tends to zero almost everywhere, and is bounded by the integrable $|f|^2$. On the other hand $\|A^n\|=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{[0,1]}x^n=1$. The first assertion tests one input at a time, called strong convergence; the second takes a supremum over all unit inputs and is operator-norm convergence. Inputs concentrated ever closer to $1$ prevent that stronger convergence.
\end{solution}
