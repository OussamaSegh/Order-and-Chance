\chapter{Operators, Spectra and Banach Algebras}\label{ch:operators}

\begin{center}\itshape
An operator may have no eigenvectors. Invertibility still leaves a spectrum,\\
and powers of the operator reveal its radius.
\end{center}

The shift on $\ell^2$ moves every coordinate one place to the right. It preserves lengths and has no eigenvalue. Yet its difference from $\lambda I$ fails to be invertible for every $|\lambda|\le1$. Finite matrices had taught us to expect those failures to come with eigenvectors. Infinite dimension separates the two ideas.

Wiener's 1932 lemma gives a different entrance to the same subject. Suppose $f(t)=\sum_na_ne^{int}$ has $\sum_n|a_n|<\infty$ and never vanishes. Must $1/f$ have an absolutely convergent Fourier series too? Smoothness can settle special examples, but the hypothesis allows much rougher functions. Gelfand's 1941 theory answers by treating these series as an algebra. Its multiplicative linear forms turn out to be evaluations at points of the circle. Checking that every one of them is nonzero on $f$ then proves invertibility inside the algebra itself.

There are two useful kinds of structure here. Compact operators retain enough finite-dimensional behaviour for the Fredholm alternative. General Banach algebras retain enough completeness for geometric series and enough complex analysis for a nonempty spectrum. Commutative algebras then let us interpret that spectrum through scalar-valued characters. Wiener's reciprocal theorem will follow from that interpretation.

We work over $\C$ unless a claim specifies otherwise. The symbols $H$ and $E$ denote a Hilbert space and a Banach space. When discussing the spectrum of an operator as an element of $\mathcal L(E)$, assume $E\ne\{0\}$; the normalised unit of a Banach algebra below excludes the zero algebra. Real scalars would not suffice for the nonempty-spectrum theorem: a right-angle rotation has no real spectral value.

\section{Adjoints}

For $T\in\mathcal L(H)$, the map $x\mapsto\langle Tx,y\rangle$ is a bounded linear functional for each fixed $y$. Riesz--Fr\'echet (Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszrep}) supplies a unique vector $T^*y$ satisfying
$\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^*y\rangle$ for all $x$.
Uniqueness and the scalar rules for the inner product show that $y\mapsto T^*y$ is linear. This \emph{adjoint} extends the conjugate transpose of a matrix and the adjoint used in Chapter~\ref{ch:hilbert}'s mean-ergodic proof.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:adjoint}
For bounded Hilbert-space operators,
\[
T^{**}=T,\quad (ST)^*=T^*S^*,\quad
\|T^*\|=\|T\|,\quad \|T^*T\|=\|T\|^2.
\]
Their kernels and ranges obey
$\ker T^*=(\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp$ and
$\overline{\operatorname{ran}T}=(\ker T^*)^\perp$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Move each factor across the inner product using the definition; uniqueness gives the double-adjoint and product identities. The estimate
$\|T^*y\|^2=\langle TT^*y,y\rangle\le\|T\|\|T^*y\|\|y\|$
gives $\|T^*\|\le\|T\|$, after division when $T^*y\ne0$. Apply the same estimate to $T^*$ to get equality. Submultiplicativity gives $\|T^*T\|\le\|T\|^2$, while
$\|Tx\|^2=\langle T^*Tx,x\rangle\le\|T^*T\|\|x\|^2$
gives the reverse inequality by taking the supremum over unit $x$.
Finally, $T^*y=0$ is equivalent to $\langle Tx,y\rangle=0$ for every $x$. This is precisely orthogonality to the range. Taking orthogonal complements and using Corollary~\ref{cor:orthdecomp} gives the closure identity.
\end{proof}

The conditions $T^*=T$, $T^*T=TT^*$ and $T^*T=TT^*=I$ define self-adjoint, normal and unitary operators. Orthogonal projections are self-adjoint, as are multiplication operators by bounded real functions on $L^2$. For the unilateral shift
$S(x_1,x_2,\ldots)=(0,x_1,x_2,\ldots)$,
the adjoint drops the first coordinate. Thus $S^*S=I$, while $SS^*$ kills the first coordinate and fixes the rest. The shift is an isometry onto a proper subspace, a possibility absent in finite dimension.

\section{Compact operators}

A bounded linear map $K:E\to F$ between Banach spaces is \emph{compact} if it sends the unit ball into a relatively compact set. Equivalently, every bounded sequence $(x_n)$ has a subsequence along which $Kx_n$ converges. Finite-rank operators have this property by finite-dimensional compactness. The infinite-dimensional identity does not, by Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszcompact}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compactops}
(a) Compact operators form a closed linear subspace of $\mathcal L(E,F)$. Composing a compact operator on either side with bounded operators preserves compactness.
(b) A Hilbert-space operator is compact exactly when it is a norm limit of finite-rank operators. Also $K$ is compact exactly when $K^*$ is compact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) For a sum, first select a convergent image subsequence under one compact map, then a further one under the other. Scalar multiples and composition follow from boundedness and continuity. To check closedness, suppose $K_j\to K$ in norm. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $j$ with $\|K-K_j\|<\varepsilon$ and a finite $\varepsilon$-net for $K_j(B_E)$. The same centres form a $2\varepsilon$-net for $K(B_E)$. Total boundedness in the complete space $F$ gives relative compactness (Volume~7, Theorem~4.8).

(b) If $K$ is compact, choose a finite $\varepsilon$-net $y_1,\ldots,y_m$ for its unit-ball image. Let $P$ be projection onto their span. For each unit-ball $x$, some $y_i$ satisfies $\|Kx-y_i\|<\varepsilon$. Since $Py_i=y_i$,
$\|(I-P)Kx\|=\|(I-P)(Kx-y_i)\|\le\|Kx-y_i\|<\varepsilon$.
Thus $PK$ has finite rank and approximates $K$ in norm. The converse follows from (a).
For a finite-rank $F$, its adjoint has finite rank: $\ker F^*=(\operatorname{ran}F)^\perp$, whose orthogonal complement has finite dimension, and $F^*$ vanishes on that kernel. Norm continuity of adjoints now sends a finite-rank approximation of $K$ to one of $K^*$. Apply the same argument to $K^*$ and use $K^{**}=K$ for the reverse implication.
\end{proof}

Finite-rank approximation need not hold for compact maps between arbitrary Banach spaces; Enflo's approximation-property counterexample, mentioned in Chapter~\ref{ch:banach}, explains the obstruction. Compactness of the Banach adjoint does survive. Exercise~\ref{ex:schauder} proves Schauder's theorem using Arzel\`a--Ascoli rather than Hilbert projections.

\begin{example}[integral operators]\label{ex:hilbertschmidt}
Assume $(X,\mu)$ is $\sigma$-finite, and take a kernel $k\in L^2(\mu\otimes\mu)$. The formula
$(Tf)(x)=\int k(x,y)f(y)\dd\mu(y)$
defines a compact operator on $L^2(\mu)$ with $\|T\|\le\|k\|_{L^2}$.
Indeed, Tonelli makes $k(x,\cdot)$ square-integrable for almost every $x$. Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the defining integral there, and integration gives
\[
\|Tf\|_2^2\le\int\!\int|k(x,y)|^2\dd\mu(y)\dd\mu(x)\,\|f\|_2^2.
\]
Measurability follows by applying the measurable integral theorem of Volume~7 to the positive and negative parts of the real and imaginary parts of $k(x,y)f(y)$. Set the result to zero on the measurable null set of sections where the absolute integral is infinite. Finite sums of kernels $c_i\mathbf1_{A_i}(x)\mathbf1_{B_i}(y)$ with finite-measure factors are dense in the product $L^2$ space: the product-measure construction approximates finite-measure sets by finite unions of such rectangles (Volume~7, Theorem~9.4), and simple functions approximate $L^2$ functions. Each resulting operator has finite rank, with range inside the span of the $\mathbf1_{A_i}$. The displayed bound makes kernel convergence imply operator-norm convergence. Proposition~\ref{prop:compactops} proves compactness. These \emph{Hilbert--Schmidt integral operators} include the kernels occurring in Fredholm equations.
\end{example}

\section{The Fredholm alternative}

For an arbitrary bounded operator, injectivity and surjectivity can separate, as the shift shows. Subtract a compact operator from the identity, and they agree again.

\begin{theorem}[Fredholm alternative]\label{thm:fredholm}
Let $K:H\to H$ be compact. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\ker(I-K)$ is finite-dimensional, and $\operatorname{ran}(I-K)$ is closed and equals $\ker(I-K^*)^\perp$;
\item[(b)] $I-K$ is injective if and only if it is surjective;
\item[(c)] $\dim\ker(I-K)=\dim\ker(I-K^*)$.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently either $x-Kx=y$ has exactly one solution for every $y$, or $x=Kx$ has a non-zero solution. In the latter case, the inhomogeneous equation is solvable precisely when $y$ is orthogonal to every solution of $z=K^*z$, a finite-dimensional space.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $T=I-K$ and $N=\ker T$.

\emph{Kernel and range.} On the closed space $N$, $K$ acts as the identity. Its unit ball is therefore relatively compact and closed, hence compact. Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszcompact} makes $N$ finite-dimensional.
Suppose $Tx_j\to y$. Subtract the projection of each $x_j$ onto $N$, leaving $Tx_j$ unchanged and arranging $x_j\perp N$. If these representatives were unbounded, pass to a subsequence with $\|x_j\|\to\infty$ and normalise $z_j=x_j/\|x_j\|$. Then $Tz_j\to0$. Compactness gives a further subsequence with $Kz_j\to w$, and hence $z_j=Tz_j+Kz_j\to w$. We would have $\|w\|=1$, $Tw=0$ and $w\perp N$, a contradiction. Thus the representatives are bounded. Pass to a subsequence with $Kx_j\to v$; then $x_j=Tx_j+Kx_j\to y+v=:x$, so $Tx=y$. The range is closed. The adjoint range identity in Proposition~\ref{prop:adjoint} gives its stated orthogonal complement.

\emph{Injectivity versus surjectivity.} Suppose $T$ is injective but not onto. Put $H_j=T^jH$. Expanding $(I-K)^j$ writes it as $I-K_j$ with $K_j$ compact, so (a) makes every $H_j$ closed. All inclusions $H_{j+1}\subset H_j$ are strict: equality would, by cancellation of the injective $T^j$, imply $TH=H$. Choose a unit vector $e_j\in H_j\cap H_{j+1}^\perp$. For $m<j$,
\[
Ke_m-Ke_j=e_m-\bigl(e_j+Te_m-Te_j\bigr).
\]
The bracket belongs to $H_{m+1}$ and is orthogonal to $e_m$. Thus $\|Ke_m-Ke_j\|\ge1$, contradicting compactness. We have proved injectivity implies surjectivity.
If $T$ is onto, then $\ker T^*=(\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp=\{0\}$. Apply the implication just proved to $I-K^*$, using compactness of $K^*$, to obtain surjectivity of $T^*$. Then $\ker T=(\operatorname{ran}T^*)^\perp=\{0\}$, proving the converse.

\emph{Equal nullities.} Set $N^*=\ker T^*$. Suppose $\dim N<\dim N^*$. Choose an injective map $A:N\to N^*$ whose image is proper, and put $K'=K+AP_N$. This is compact. If $(I-K')x=0$, then $Tx=AP_Nx$ belongs both to $\operatorname{ran}T$ and to its orthogonal complement $N^*$. Both sides vanish, so injectivity of $A$ gives $P_Nx=0$, while $Tx=0$ gives $x\in N$. Hence $x=0$. Part (b) would make $I-K'$ onto. But a non-zero vector in $N^*\cap A(N)^\perp$ is orthogonal to both terms of its range, which is impossible for an onto map. We conclude $\dim N\ge\dim N^*$. Interchanging $K,K^*$ gives the reverse bound, proving equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
For $I-K$, compactness replaces the dimension count used for square matrices. If injectivity failed to imply surjectivity, the proof would construct infinitely many vectors whose compact images remain a unit distance apart. The contradiction is geometric and works without counting the dimension of the ambient space.
\end{beauty}

\section{The spectrum}

A \emph{Banach algebra} is a Banach space with an associative bilinear multiplication, satisfying $\|ab\|\le\|a\|\|b\|$, and an identity $1$ with $\|1\|=1$. Composition makes $\mathcal L(E)$ an example. Pointwise multiplication makes $C(K)$ an example for nonempty compact $K$. On $\ell^1(\Z)$ use convolution,
$(a*b)_n=\sum_ka_kb_{n-k}$.
Tonelli for series gives $\|a*b\|_1\le\|a\|_1\|b\|_1$, and the identity is $\delta_0$. Completeness allows geometric series in all these algebras.

\begin{lemma}[Neumann series]\label{lem:neumann}
If $\|a\|<1$, then
\[
(1-a)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}a^n,\qquad
\|(1-a)^{-1}\|\le\frac1{1-\|a\|}.
\]
The invertible elements form an open set, and inversion is continuous on it.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Submultiplicativity makes the series absolutely convergent. Multiplying its partial sum by $1-a$ on either side gives $1-a^{N+1}$, which tends to $1$. Summing the scalar norm bounds gives the estimate.
If $b$ is invertible and $\|c\|<1/\|b^{-1}\|$, factor $b-c=b(1-b^{-1}c)$. The geometric series gives an inverse and
\[
\|(b-c)^{-1}-b^{-1}\|
\le\sum_{n\ge1}\|b^{-1}\|^{n+1}\|c\|^n\longrightarrow0
\quad(c\to0).
\]
This proves both openness and continuity.
\end{proof}

For $a$ in the algebra, its \emph{spectrum} is
$\sigma(a)=\{\lambda\in\C:\lambda1-a\text{ is not invertible}\}$.
We abbreviate $\lambda1$ to $\lambda$. The \emph{spectral radius} is $r(a)=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(a)}|\lambda|$. For matrices the spectrum is just the eigenvalue set. An operator's spectrum includes every eigenvalue, but failure to be onto can produce additional spectral values even when the kernel is zero.

\begin{theorem}[Gelfand]\label{thm:spectrum}
For any element $a$ of a complex Banach algebra, $\sigma(a)$ is nonempty and compact, lies in $\{|\lambda|\le\|a\|\}$, and satisfies
\[
r(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|a^n\|^{1/n}
=\inf_{n\ge1}\|a^n\|^{1/n}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
When $|\lambda|>\|a\|$, factor out $\lambda$ and apply the Neumann lemma:
\[
(\lambda-a)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{a^n}{\lambda^{n+1}}.\tag{$*$}
\]
This bounds the spectrum. Its complement is open by continuity of $\lambda\mapsto\lambda-a$ and openness of invertibility, so the spectrum is compact.
For $\varphi\in A^*$ set $f_\varphi(\lambda)=\varphi((\lambda-a)^{-1})$ on that complement. Near any $\lambda_0$ there, the same geometric expansion gives
\[
(\lambda-a)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}(\lambda_0-\lambda)^n(\lambda_0-a)^{-n-1}.
\]
Applying $\varphi$ gives a convergent scalar power series, proving holomorphy.

\emph{Nonemptiness.} If the spectrum were empty, $f_\varphi$ would be entire. Formula $(*)$ bounds its modulus by $\|\varphi\|/(|\lambda|-\|a\|)$ outside a large circle, so it tends to zero at infinity. Liouville's theorem (Volume~5, Theorem~9.16) makes it identically zero. At $\lambda=0$ this says $\varphi((-a)^{-1})=0$ for every bounded linear functional. Hahn--Banach (Corollary~\ref{cor:hbnorm}) then gives $(-a)^{-1}=0$, impossible for an inverse in an algebra with $\|1\|=1$.

\emph{The radius.} If $\lambda\in\sigma(a)$, then $\lambda^n\in\sigma(a^n)$. Indeed, $\lambda^n-a^n=(\lambda-a)(\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n-2}a+\cdots+a^{n-1})$ is a product of commuting factors. If that product had an inverse, multiplying by the other factor and the product inverse would give a two-sided inverse of $\lambda-a$. Thus $|\lambda|^n\le\|a^n\|$, and
$r(a)\le\inf_n\|a^n\|^{1/n}$.

For the reverse bound, $f_\varphi$ is holomorphic on $|\lambda|>r(a)$, with Laurent series $(*)$ after applying $\varphi$ on the smaller region $|\lambda|>\|a\|$. Uniqueness and convergence of Laurent expansions on an annulus (Volume~5, Theorem~9.22) extend this scalar series to all $|\lambda|>r(a)$. For each fixed such $\lambda$, its terms imply $\varphi(a^n/\lambda^n)\to0$ for every $\varphi\in A^*$. Apply uniform boundedness (Theorem~\ref{thm:ubp}) to the linear forms $J(a^n/\lambda^n)$ on the Banach space $A^*$. It gives $\|a^n\|\le C_\lambda|\lambda|^n$, using the norm-preserving canonical embedding from Hahn--Banach. Therefore
\[
\limsup_n\|a^n\|^{1/n}\le|\lambda|.
\]
Let $|\lambda|$ decrease to $r(a)$. Together with the lower bound by the infimum, this proves existence of the limit and both equalities.
\end{proof}

Invertibility is algebraic, so equivalent complete algebra norms give the same spectrum and radius. The norm formula identifies the exponential growth rate of powers; it does not assert a fixed multiplicative bound $\|a^n\|\le Cr(a)^n$ at the boundary rate. Exercise~\ref{ex:jacobi} uses a slightly larger rate to prove convergence of an iteration.

\begin{example}\label{ex:volterraex}
The Volterra operator $Vf(x)=\int_0^xf(t)\dd t$ on $L^2([0,1])$, or on $C([0,1])$, has spectrum $\{0\}$ and has no eigenvalue.
For $n\ge1$, repeated integration gives
\[
V^nf(x)=\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^x(x-t)^{n-1}f(t)\dd t,
\]
so $|V^nf(x)|\le x^{n-1}\|f\|_1/(n-1)!$. In either specified norm this gives $\|V^n\|\le1/(n-1)!$ and hence $r(V)=0$. Nonemptiness forces the stated spectrum.
If $Vf=\lambda f$ with $\lambda\ne0$, the equation first supplies a continuous representative of $f$, then a continuously differentiable one. It satisfies $\lambda f'=f$ and $f(0)=0$, whose only solution is zero, as multiplication by $e^{-x/\lambda}$ shows. If $Vf=0$, its continuous primitive vanishes everywhere, and the Lebesgue fundamental theorem of calculus (Volume~7, Theorem~11.9) gives $f=0$ almost everywhere. Thus even zero is not an eigenvalue. It is spectral because $V$ is not onto: its continuous image representatives vanish at zero, excluding the constant function one. Equality almost everywhere between two continuous representatives would force equality everywhere, so this also excludes one from the $L^2$ range.
\end{example}

\section{Gelfand's theory of commutative Banach algebras}

Assume now that $A$ is commutative. A \emph{character} is a non-zero complex-linear multiplicative map $\chi:A\to\C$. It necessarily satisfies $\chi(1)=1$: multiplicativity gives $\chi(a)=\chi(1)\chi(a)$ for every $a$, and some $\chi(a)$ is nonzero. We will treat characters as points and the values $\chi(a)$ as evaluations of $a$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gelfandmazur}
(a) A complex Banach algebra in which every non-zero element is invertible is $\C1$ (Gelfand--Mazur).
(b) Every character has norm $1$ and obeys $|\chi(a)|\le\|a\|$.
(c) A closed proper ideal $I$ gives a Banach algebra quotient with norm $\|a+I\|=\inf_{i\in I}\|a+i\|$.
(d) Every maximal proper ideal is closed.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) Choose $\lambda\in\sigma(a)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum}. The element $\lambda-a$ cannot be invertible, so the hypothesis forces it to be zero. Thus every $a$ is a scalar multiple of $1$.
(b) If $|\chi(a)|>\|a\|$, the Neumann lemma would make $\chi(a)1-a$ invertible. A character sends an invertible element to a non-zero scalar, but sends this one to zero, a contradiction. Hence $\|\chi\|\le1$; equality follows from $\chi(1)=1$.
(c) Closedness of $I$ makes the quotient seminorm a norm. The product is well defined because $I$ is an ideal. For representatives $a+i,b+j$, their product lies in $ab+I$. Submultiplicativity followed by separate infima gives the quotient product bound. Its unit has norm at most one. If $\|1+i\|<1$ for some $i\in I$, the Neumann lemma would make $-i=1-(1+i)$ invertible, forcing the ideal to contain $1$. Thus its unit norm is exactly one.
For completeness, take a Cauchy sequence of classes. Choose a subsequence $a_n+I$ whose successive quotient differences have norm less than $2^{-n}$. Choose representatives $d_n$ of these differences with $\|d_n\|<2^{-n}$. The convergent series $a_1+\sum_nd_n$ gives a limit for that subsequence in the quotient. The Cauchy property then gives the same limit for the whole original sequence.
(d) A proper ideal contains no invertible element. In particular, it misses the open unit ball about $1$, so its closure still has distance at least one from $1$. That closure is an ideal by continuity of multiplication and remains proper. Maximality therefore makes it equal to the original ideal.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gelfand, 1941]\label{thm:gelfand}
Let $\Delta$ be the character set of the commutative complex Banach algebra $A$, equipped with the weak* topology inherited from $A^*$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Characters correspond bijectively to maximal ideals by $\chi\mapsto\ker\chi$.
\item[(b)] An element $a$ is invertible exactly when every character has $\chi(a)\ne0$.
\item[(c)] $\sigma(a)=\{\chi(a):\chi\in\Delta\}$, and $r(a)=\max_{\chi\in\Delta}|\chi(a)|$.
\item[(d)] The space $\Delta$ is compact Hausdorff. The Gelfand transform $a\mapsto\hat a$, defined by $\hat a(\chi)=\chi(a)$, is an algebra homomorphism into $C(\Delta)$ and satisfies $\|\hat a\|_\infty=r(a)\le\|a\|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) A character's kernel has codimension one, since $a-\chi(a)1$ belongs to it, and hence is maximal. Conversely let $M$ be maximal. It is closed by the lemma, and $A/M$ is a Banach algebra. Every nonzero class is invertible: if $a\notin M$, maximality gives $M+aA=A$, so $ab\in1+M$ for some $b$. Gelfand--Mazur makes this quotient $\C1$. The scalar of $a+M$ defines a character with kernel $M$. Its value at $a$ is the unique scalar $\lambda$ with $a-\lambda1\in M$, so no second character has that kernel.
(b) Invertibility gives $\chi(a)\chi(a^{-1})=1$. If $a$ is not invertible, commutativity makes $aA$ a proper ideal. Apply Zorn's lemma to proper ideals containing it: the union of a chain is still proper because it cannot contain $1$. A maximal such ideal is a character kernel by (a), and that character vanishes on $a$.
(c) Apply (b) to $\lambda-a$ to identify the spectrum. Its compactness and nonemptiness, already proved, give the maximum for the modulus.
(d) All characters lie in the dual unit ball, weak* compact by Theorem~\ref{thm:alaoglu}. Within that ball they are exactly the functionals satisfying $\varphi(1)=1$ and $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ for every $a,b$. Each equality defines a weak* closed set because evaluation at a fixed algebra element is continuous. Thus $\Delta$ is closed in the compact ball. The weak* topology is Hausdorff, and its definition makes each $\hat a$ continuous. Linearity and multiplication pass through characters, proving the homomorphism claim; part (c) gives its norm formula.
\end{proof}

For $C(K)$ with nonempty compact metric $K$, the characters are evaluations at its points, proved in Exercise~\ref{ex:ckcharacters}. The resulting identification $K\to\Delta$ is a homeomorphism: it is continuous because every function in $C(K)$ is continuous, and a continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff is a homeomorphism. Thus the algebra recovers the space together with its topology.

\section{Wiener's lemma}\label{sec:wienerlemma}

Let $W$ consist of functions $f(t)=\sum_{n\in\Z}a_ne^{int}$ with norm $\|f\|_W=\sum_n|a_n|<\infty$. The series converges uniformly. Integrating against $e^{-imt}$ recovers $a_m$ by Fourier orthogonality, so the coefficients, and hence the norm, are well defined. Multiplying two series corresponds to convolving their coefficients, with rearrangement justified by Fubini for counting measure (Volume~7, Theorem~9.6). This identifies $W$ isometrically with the commutative Banach algebra $\ell^1(\Z)$, with unit the constant function $1$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:wcharacters}
Every character of $W$ is evaluation at a unique point $t_0$ of the circle $\mathbb T$, and every such evaluation is a character.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Evaluations are nonzero, linear and multiplicative. For a character $\chi$, put $z=\chi(e^{it})$. The functions $e^{it}$ and $e^{-it}$ are inverses and each has $W$-norm one. The character bound gives $|z|\le1$ and $|z^{-1}|\le1$, so $z=e^{it_0}$ for some circle point. Multiplicativity gives $\chi(e^{int})=e^{int_0}$ for all integers, positive or negative. The Fourier series converges in $W$-norm and $\chi$ is continuous, whence $\chi(f)=\sum_na_ne^{int_0}=f(t_0)$. The value on $e^{it}$ makes the circle point unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Wiener's lemma, 1932]\label{thm:wiener}
A nowhere-zero $f\in W$ has $1/f\in W$. Equivalently, the reciprocal of a nowhere-zero absolutely convergent Fourier series again has an absolutely convergent Fourier series.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every character is evaluation at a circle point, so none vanishes on $f$. Theorem~\ref{thm:gelfand}(b) gives an inverse $g$ within $W$. Pointwise multiplication then says $g(t)=1/f(t)$ everywhere.
\end{proof}

For $W$, the Gelfand transform is the original function on the circle. For its coefficient algebra $\ell^1(\Z)$, it is the Fourier series map. The corresponding convolution problem on $L^1(\R)$ leads to Wiener's Tauberian theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:tauberian}. A further theorem, Wiener--L\'evy, says that $F\circ f\in W$ whenever $F$ is holomorphic near the range of $f$. Its proof requires a holomorphic functional calculus, beyond what is developed here; we do not use it as a premise.

The disc algebra contains functions continuous on the closed disc and holomorphic inside. The function $f(z)=z$ is nonzero at every boundary point but is not invertible in this algebra. Which evaluation did the boundary test miss? This is why testing some characters is weaker than testing all of them.
\end{think}

\section{When eigenvectors return}

The shift forced us to define spectrum through invertibility, and the Volterra operator showed that a nonempty spectrum can coexist with no eigenvalues at all. Compactness did restore the matrix-like alternative for $I-K$. Commutativity restored a different picture: spectral values became values of characters, giving a reciprocal theorem for Fourier series.

Chapter~\ref{ch:spectral} combines compactness with self-adjointness. Its nonzero spectral values will be eigenvalues, and their orthogonal eigenspaces will account for the operator outside its kernel. The remaining task is to prove that they account for all of it.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:volterraeq}
For every complex $\lambda$ and continuous $g$ on $[0,1]$, prove that $f(x)-\lambda\int_0^xf(t)\dd t=g(x)$ has exactly one continuous solution. Express it as a convergent series and evaluate it when $g=1$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:fredholmrank1}
Determine all $L^2([0,1])$ solutions of $u(x)-\lambda\int_0^1xyu(y)\dd y=g(x)$ for every complex $\lambda$. Identify explicitly the solvability condition and the homogeneous solutions at the exceptional value, and compare with Fredholm's alternative.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:shift}
For the unilateral shift $S$ on $\ell^2$, prove:
(a) $S$ has no eigenvalues, whereas every $|\lambda|<1$ is an eigenvalue of $S^*$;
(b) both spectra are the closed unit disc;
(c) for the $N\times N$ matrix $J$ with ones immediately above its diagonal, $\sigma(J)=\{0\}$ but $\|(z-J)^{-1}\|\ge|z|^{-N}$ whenever $0<|z|<1$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:multiplication}
Let $\mu$ be $\sigma$-finite and $\varphi\in L^\infty(\mu)$. For $M_\varphi f=\varphi f$ on $L^2(\mu)$, prove $\|M_\varphi\|=\|\varphi\|_\infty$ and identify its spectrum as the essential range
$\{\lambda:\mu(|\varphi-\lambda|<\varepsilon)>0\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0\}$.
Prove that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $\mu(\varphi=\lambda)>0$. Apply this to multiplication by $x$ on $L^2([0,1])$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:jacobi}
\emph{Iterative solvers.} Let $M=D-N$ be a complex square matrix with invertible diagonal part $D$, and set $B=D^{-1}N$. Prove that the Jacobi iteration $x_{k+1}=D^{-1}(Nx_k+b)$ converges, for every initial vector, to the same unique solution of $Mx=b$ exactly when $r(B)<1$. Show that strict diagonal dominance $|m_{ii}|>\sum_{j\ne i}|m_{ij}|$ ensures this condition.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:jacobson}
In a Banach algebra, prove that invertibility of $1-xy$ implies invertibility of $1-yx$, with inverse $1+y(1-xy)^{-1}x$. Deduce equality of $\sigma(ab)$ and $\sigma(ba)$ away from zero, and equality of their radii. Give an example in which one spectrum contains zero and the other does not.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:ckcharacters}
For compact metric $K$, show that each character of $C(K)$ evaluates at one unique point of $K$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:schauder}
\emph{Schauder's theorem.} For a compact map $K:E\to F$ between Banach spaces, prove compactness of the Banach adjoint $K^*:F^*\to E^*$ defined by $K^*\varphi=\varphi\circ K$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c08a}
On $L^2([0,1])$, multiply by the function equal to $1$ on $[0,1/2]$ and $2$ on $(1/2,1]$. Find the spectrum and the eigenspaces, verify $M^2-3M+2I=0$, and prove that $M$ is not compact.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c08b}
Let $De_n=e_n/n$ on $\ell^2$. Prove compactness and compute the spectrum without using the next chapter's compact spectral theorem. Describe its range, and show that the range is dense but not closed. Is zero an eigenvalue?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c08c}
In the convolution algebra $\ell^1(\Z)$ let $a=\delta_0-r\delta_1$, where $|r|<1$. Find its inverse and the inverse's norm. Compute $\sigma(a)$ and $r(a)$ using the characters of the Wiener algebra.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c08d}
Define a weighted shift on $\ell^2$ by $Te_n=e_{n+1}/n$. Prove that it is compact, $\|T\|=1$ and $\|T^k\|=1/k!$ for every $k\ge1$. Find its spectrum and all its eigenvalues.
\end{exercise}

\section{Hints}

\begin{hint}{ex:volterraeq}
The factorial estimate on $V^n$ makes $\sum_n\lambda^nV^n$ converge for any fixed $\lambda$, even if $\|\lambda V\|\ge1$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:fredholmrank1}
All information fed back by the integral is the one number $c=\int_0^1yu(y)\dd y$. Substitute $u=g+\lambda cx$ to determine it.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:shift}
For $S^*$ try a geometric sequence. Adjointing an inverse conjugates the spectral parameter. For the finite block, inspect the top-right entry of the finite inverse series.
\end{hint}

Use finite positive-measure subsets of regions where $\varphi$ is close to a proposed spectral value. Outside the essential range, take the reciprocal multiplier.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:jacobi}
When a solution exists, the error satisfies $e_{k+1}=Be_k$. If $r(B)<1$, first use a Neumann inverse for $I-B$ to establish existence and uniqueness.
\end{hint}

Check the proposed inverse on both sides by multiplication. Compare $S^*S$ and $SS^*$ for an example at zero.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:ckcharacters}
If the kernel has no common zero, finitely many functions in it give a strictly positive sum of squared moduli on $K$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:schauder}
Restrict unit-ball functionals to the compact set $\overline{K(B_E)}$. They are uniformly bounded and $1$-Lipschitz there.
\end{hint}

Functions supported in each half give the two eigenspaces. Produce an orthonormal sequence in the first half to disprove compactness.

Truncate the diagonal. For $\lambda$ outside the proposed spectrum, bound the diagonal entries of the reciprocal uniformly.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v8c08c}
Convolution powers of $\delta_1$ are $\delta_n$. Characters send $\delta_1$ to every point of the unit circle.
\end{hint}

The $k$th power has weight $1/[n(n+1)\cdots(n+k-1)]$ on $e_n$. Locate its largest weight before applying the spectral-radius formula.
\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:volterraeq}
\idea Sum the resolvent series using factorial decay rather than a one-step norm bound.
\writeup For $n\ge1$, Example~\ref{ex:volterraex} gives $\|\lambda^nV^n\|\le|\lambda|^n/(n-1)!$. This summable bound proves norm convergence of $R=I+\sum_{n\ge1}\lambda^nV^n$. Multiplication of finite sums by $I-\lambda V$ telescopes on either side; the remainders tend to zero by the same bound. Thus $R$ is a two-sided inverse, and $f=Rg$ is the unique solution. For $g=1$, repeated integration gives $V^n1=x^n/n!$, so $f=e^{\lambda x}$. The identity $\lambda\int_0^xe^{\lambda t}\dd t=e^{\lambda x}-1$ checks the answer for $\lambda\ne0$; at $\lambda=0$ it reduces to $f=1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:fredholmrank1}
\idea Solve first for the scalar moment, then reconstruct the whole function.
\writeup Put $c=\int_0^1yu(y)\dd y$ and $h=\int_0^1yg(y)\dd y$. The equation gives $u=g+\lambda cx$, and taking its moment gives $(1-\lambda/3)c=h$.
If $\lambda\ne3$, this has $c=3h/(3-\lambda)$ and the unique solution $u=g+3\lambda hx/(3-\lambda)$. If $\lambda=3$, solvability requires $h=0$; under that condition every $c\in\C$ gives a solution $u=g+3cx$ and its moment is indeed $c$.
At the exceptional parameter, $Kf=3\langle f,x\rangle x$ is a self-adjoint rank-one projection because $\|x\|_2^2=1/3$. Both $I-K$ and its adjoint have kernel $\C x$. The condition $h=0$ is exactly orthogonality to that kernel, as Fredholm predicts.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:shift}
\idea The adjoint has geometric eigenvectors; closedness and adjoint invertibility recover both complete spectra.
\writeup (a) If $Sx=\lambda x$ and $\lambda\ne0$, its first coordinate forces $x_1=0$, then the remaining coordinate equations force all entries to vanish. At $\lambda=0$, injectivity of $S$ gives the same conclusion. For $|\lambda|<1$, the nonzero square-summable sequence $(1,\lambda,\lambda^2,\ldots)$ is an $S^*$-eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$.
(b) Both operators have norm one, so their spectra lie in the closed unit disc. The adjoint spectrum contains the open disc by (a), and its closedness includes the boundary. Adjointing a two-sided inverse shows that $\lambda I-T$ is invertible exactly when $\bar\lambda I-T^*$ is invertible. One direction follows by taking the adjoint of the inverse, the other by doing so again. Thus $\sigma(S)$ is the complex conjugate of $\sigma(S^*)$, giving the same closed disc.
(c) Nilpotence $J^N=0$ gives $(z-J)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}z^{-k-1}J^k$ for $z\ne0$, whereas $J$ itself is singular. In that inverse the $(1,N)$ entry is $z^{-N}$, including $N=1$ with $J^0=I$. The operator norm is at least the modulus of any matrix entry, proving the stated lower bound.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:multiplication}
\idea Positive-measure neighbourhoods of a value give approximate eigenvectors of norm one.
\writeup If the measure is identically zero, $L^2$ is the zero space, the multiplier norm and essential supremum norm are zero, the essential range is empty, and no eigenvector exists. Every scalar-shifted map on that zero space is invertible as its unique map, so the spectrum is empty too. This is the excluded zero-space case in the general Banach-algebra nonemptiness theorem.
Assume the measure is nonzero. The bound $\|\varphi f\|_2\le\|\varphi\|_\infty\|f\|_2$ gives one norm inequality. If $\|\varphi\|_\infty>0$, for any smaller positive $c$ the set $\{|\varphi|>c\}$ has positive measure. Sigma-finiteness supplies a subset of finite positive measure, whose indicator gives $\|M_\varphi\|\ge c$. Letting $c$ tend to the essential supremum proves equality. At essential supremum zero the upper bound already proves equality.
Outside the essential range, $|\varphi-\lambda|\ge\varepsilon$ almost everywhere for some $\varepsilon>0$. Its reciprocal multiplier is a bounded inverse of $M_\varphi-\lambda I$. Inside the essential range, for each $\varepsilon>0$ take a finite positive-measure $B\subseteq\{|\varphi-\lambda|<\varepsilon\}$ and normalise its indicator to $f$. Then $\|f\|_2=1$ and $\|(M_\varphi-\lambda I)f\|_2\le\varepsilon$. A bounded inverse would give $1\le C\varepsilon$ for all $\varepsilon$, impossible.
The eigenvector equation forces $f$ to vanish off $\{\varphi=\lambda\}$. A nonzero $L^2$ function supported there exists exactly when that set has positive measure, again using sigma-finiteness for an indicator if needed. For $\varphi(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$, the essential range is $[0,1]$ and each level set is null, so there are no eigenvalues.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:jacobi}
\idea A common attracting fixed point requires every error direction to decay.
\writeup If $r(B)<1$, the spectral-radius formula gives $\|B^k\|\le q^k$ eventually for some $r(B)<q<1$. Hence $\sum_kB^k$ converges and inverts $I-B$. Since $M=D(I-B)$, there is a unique solution $x$ for every $b$. Subtract its fixed-point equation to obtain $e_k=x_k-x=B^k(x_0-x)$, which tends to zero.
Conversely, if every initial vector converges to the same solution $x$ but $r(B)\ge1$, choose an eigenvector $v$ with eigenvalue $\mu$ of modulus at least one. Starting at $x+v$ gives error $\mu^kv$, which cannot tend to zero. This contradiction proves necessity.
Strict diagonal dominance makes each row sum of $|B|$ equal to $\sum_{j\ne i}|m_{ij}|/|m_{ii}|<1$. The induced maximum-coordinate norm is the largest of these row sums, so $r(B)\le\|B\|_\infty<1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:jacobson}
\idea The inverse formula can be verified algebraically, without requiring a convergent power series.
\writeup Put $c=(1-xy)^{-1}$. Multiplication gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
(1-yx)(1+ycx)&=1-yx+y(1-xy)cx=1,\\
(1+ycx)(1-yx)&=1-yx+yc(1-xy)x=1.
\end{aligned}
\]
Interchanging $x,y$ proves the equivalence of invertibilities. For $\lambda\ne0$ apply it with $x=a/\lambda$, $y=b$, and factor out $\lambda$. The spectra agree away from zero. Their nonemptiness and compactness then give equal maximal moduli, including the case in which both radii vanish.
Take $a=S^*$ and $b=S$. Their product $ab=I$ has spectrum $\{1\}$. The reverse product $ba$ is projection onto $\{x_1=0\}$, with nonzero kernel and nonzero range, so its spectrum is $\{0,1\}$. Outside these two scalars, its inverse is given by the two projection components, confirming there are no additional values.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:ckcharacters}
\idea A proper character kernel cannot contain a nowhere-zero function.
\writeup If $K$ is empty, there is no nonzero character, and the assertion is vacuous. Otherwise let $M=\ker\chi$. If it is not the evaluation kernel at any $x$, maximality prevents it from being contained in any of those kernels. For each $x$ choose $f_x\in M$ with $f_x(x)\ne0$. Continuity gives a neighbourhood where it stays nonzero, and compactness selects finitely many such neighbourhoods covering $K$. The function $g=\sum_i|f_{x_i}|^2$ lies in $M$, since each summand is $f_{x_i}\overline{f_{x_i}}$ and $M$ is an ideal. It is positive everywhere and bounded away from zero by compactness, so $1/g\in C(K)$. This would put an invertible element in $M$, a contradiction.
Thus $M$ equals an evaluation kernel at some $x$. Since $f-f(x)1\in M$, we get $\chi(f)=f(x)$. Distinct points are separated by continuous distance functions on the metric space, so the point is unique.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:schauder}
\idea Uniform convergence on the compact image becomes norm convergence after applying the adjoint.
\writeup Let $X=\overline{K(B_E)}$, a compact metric subset of $F$. Restrict any sequence $(\varphi_j)$ in the unit ball of $F^*$ to $X$. The restrictions are bounded by $\sup_{y\in X}\|y\|$ and satisfy $|\varphi_j(y)-\varphi_j(z)|\le\|y-z\|$. Arzel\`a--Ascoli (Volume~7, Theorem~4.15), applied to real and imaginary parts, gives a uniformly convergent subsequence on $X$. Along it,
\[
\|K^*\varphi_j-K^*\varphi_k\|
=\sup_{\|x\|\le1}|(\varphi_j-\varphi_k)(Kx)|
\le\sup_{y\in X}|\varphi_j(y)-\varphi_k(y)|\longrightarrow0.
\]
Completeness of $E^*$ gives a norm limit. The sequential criterion proves compactness of $K^*$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c08a}
\idea Each half interval supplies an entire eigenspace, rather than just one eigenvector.
\writeup Multiplication by the step function has essential range $\{1,2\}$, so this is its spectrum by Exercise~\ref{ex:multiplication}. The eigenspaces are the functions supported almost everywhere in the first and second halves, respectively. On either half the scalar identity $z^2-3z+2=0$ holds, proving the operator identity.
For noncompactness, set $f_j(x)=\sqrt2 e^{4\pi ijx}\mathbf1_{[0,1/2]}(x)$ for positive integers $j$. Integrating exponentials on the half interval shows that these functions are orthonormal. The multiplier fixes every $f_j$, so distinct images remain distance $\sqrt2$ apart. They have no convergent subsequence, contradicting the compactness criterion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c08b}
\idea The diagonal tends to zero, but inversion would multiply coordinates by an unbounded sequence.
\writeup Let $D_N$ keep the first $N$ diagonal entries and replace the rest by zero. It has finite rank and $\|D-D_N\|=1/(N+1)$, proving compactness. Each $1/n$ is an eigenvalue. Zero is spectral because $D$ is not onto: $y=(1/n)_{n\ge1}$ lies in $\ell^2$, but its only possible preimage is the nonsummable sequence of ones.
For $\lambda\notin\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}$, the distances $|\lambda-1/n|$ have a positive lower bound: for large $n$ they are at least $|\lambda|/2$, and the finitely many remaining ones are positive. The reciprocal diagonal therefore gives a bounded inverse of $\lambda I-D$. This proves the full spectrum.
Coordinatewise, $y$ belongs to the range exactly when $(ny_n)_n\in\ell^2$. Every finitely supported sequence belongs, so the range is dense. The truncations of $y=(1/n)$ belong and tend to $y$, which does not, so the range is not closed. All diagonal entries are nonzero, making $D$ injective; zero is not an eigenvalue.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c08c}
\idea The geometric inverse lives on distinct non-negative indices, and characters give its spectrum directly.
\writeup Since $\|r\delta_1\|_1=|r|<1$, Neumann's series gives
$a^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}r^n\delta_n$.
Its $\ell^1$ norm is $\sum_{n\ge0}|r|^n=1/(1-|r|)$. The Wiener characters send $a$ to $1-re^{it}$ as $t$ runs around the circle. Gelfand's theorem therefore gives $\sigma(a)=\{1-rz:|z|=1\}$ and $r(a)=1+|r|$. At $r=0$ the circle collapses to $\{1\}$, in agreement with the formula. For $r\ne0$, choosing $z$ so that $rz=-|r|$ attains the maximum modulus.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c08d}
\idea Orthogonal images make the norm equal to the largest weight at every power.
\writeup Truncate $T$ after its action on $e_N$. The difference has norm $1/(N+1)$, so $T$ is a norm limit of finite-rank maps and is compact. Its largest weight is one, giving $\|T\|=1$.
For $k\ge1$,
$T^ke_n=e_{n+k}/[n(n+1)\cdots(n+k-1)]$.
These images are orthogonal. The denominator increases with $n$, so the operator norm is attained at $n=1$ and equals $1/k!$. Since $k!\ge\lfloor k/2\rfloor^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}$, its $k$th root tends to infinity. The spectral-radius formula and nonemptiness give $\sigma(T)=\{0\}$.
Every weight is nonzero, so $T$ is injective and zero is not an eigenvalue. For a nonzero proposed eigenvalue, the first-coordinate equation forces $x_1=0$, and the successive equations force every coordinate to vanish. Thus there are no eigenvalues at all, despite compactness and a nonempty spectrum.
\end{solution}
