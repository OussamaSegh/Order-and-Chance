\chapter{Haar Measure, Duality and the \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-adic Numbers}\label{ch:haar}

\begin{center}\itshape
Move a set along the real line and its length stays the same. Haar measure asks for this simple rule on a group. Characters then supply its frequencies, even when the group is built from $p$-adic digits.
\end{center}

A ball can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two copies of itself. Banach and Tarski proved this in 1924, using the axiom of choice; the pieces escape any consistent assignment of volume. A year earlier, Banach had proved that a disc cannot suffer the same fate. He extended ordinary area to every bounded planar set, keeping finite additivity and invariance under rigid motions. Volume~7, Chapter~7, left the difference between the plane and space to be explained.

Von Neumann found the reason in 1929 by looking at the motions themselves. Planar motions are built from commutative groups, whose bounded functions admit invariant averages. Spatial rotations contain a free group, whose branches defeat such an average. He called groups with invariant averages \emph{messbar}; Day later chose \emph{amenable}, a name that also recalls a mean.

On a compact group an invariant average can be an ordinary integral. Its measure is Haar measure, named after Alfr\'ed Haar's 1933 construction; Weil established the general theory. Lebesgue measure in Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier} and counting measure in Chapter~\ref{ch:finite} are two examples. We will construct another on $\Q_p$, using the compactness of $\Z_p$ from Volume~7, Chapter~2. Its characters turn out to be indexed by $\Q_p$ itself. These local measures and characters are the ingredients Volume~13 needs for Poisson summation on the adèles.

\section{Topological groups and Haar measure}

A group is a \emph{topological group} when multiplication $(x,y)\mapsto xy$ and inversion $x\mapsto x^{-1}$ are continuous. Our topological groups will be metrizable, locally compact and $\sigma$-compact. Thus each point has a compact neighbourhood, and countably many compact sets cover the group. These assumptions keep us within Volume~7's measure theory. They hold for $\R^d$, the circle $\mathbb T=\{z\in\C:|z|=1\}$, countable discrete groups, $GL_n(\R)$, $\Q_p$ and $\Z_p$. The discussion of invariant means below concerns abstract groups and does not impose countability.

\begin{definition}
A Borel measure $\mu$ on $G$ is a \emph{left Haar measure} if compact sets have finite measure, every non-empty open set has positive measure, and
\[\mu(gE)=\mu(E)\qquad(g\in G,\ E\text{ Borel}).\]
Replacing $gE$ by $Eg$ defines a right Haar measure. For an abelian group we use additive notation and call either one a \emph{Haar measure}.
\end{definition}

Lebesgue measure, arc length and counting measure give the three familiar examples. Taking products gives Haar measures on product groups. Matrices provide an example where the group operation is not commutative.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:glnhaar}
On $GL_n(\R)$ the measure
\[\mu(E)=\int_E|\det X|^{-n}\dd X,\]
with $\dd X$ denoting Lebesgue measure on $\R^{n^2}$, is both left and right Haar measure.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix an invertible matrix $A$. Left multiplication applies $A$ separately to the $n$ columns, so its determinant as a linear map on $\R^{n^2}$ is $(\det A)^n$. Volume~7, Theorem~9.8, therefore gives
\[\mu(AE)=\int_E|\det(AY)|^{-n}|\det A|^n\dd Y=\int_E|\det Y|^{-n}\dd Y.\]
For right multiplication the same calculation applies to the rows. Finally, $|\det X|^{-n}$ is a positive continuous density on $GL_n(\R)$. It is bounded on each compact subset and bounded below by a positive number on a sufficiently small ball around any point. This proves the two remaining Haar properties.
\end{proof}

Left and right invariance can differ: the affine group $x\mapsto ax+b$, $a>0$, has densities $a^{-2}\dd a\dd b$ and $a^{-1}\dd a\dd b$, respectively (Exercise~\ref{ex:axb}). The general uniqueness theorem, quoted below, says that right translation of a left Haar measure has the form $\mu(Eg)=\Delta(g)\mu(E)$. This convention defines the positive \emph{modular function} $\Delta$. A group is \emph{unimodular} when $\Delta=1$; abelian, compact and discrete groups have this property. Here is the uniqueness proof in the abelian case, which is all our constructions will require.

\begin{theorem}[uniqueness]\label{thm:haaruniq}
If $\mu$ and $\nu$ are Haar measures on an abelian group, then $\mu=c\nu$ for some $c>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take continuous compactly supported functions $f,g$, and put $\check g(x)=g(-x)$. Compact support and $\sigma$-finiteness justify Fubini (Volume~7, Theorem~9.6). Translation invariance gives
\[\begin{aligned}
\int f\dd\mu\int g\dd\nu
 &=\iint f(x+y)g(y)\dd\mu(x)\dd\nu(y)\\
 &=\iint f(y)g(y-x)\dd\nu(y)\dd\mu(x)\\
 &=\int f\dd\nu\int\check g\dd\mu.
\end{aligned}\]
Choose $g\ge0$ with $g$ nonzero. Both $g$ and $\check g$ are positive on non-empty open sets, so their integrals are positive. We have proved
\[\int f\dd\mu=c\int f\dd\nu,\qquad c=\frac{\int\check g\dd\mu}{\int g\dd\nu}>0.
\]
To pass from functions to sets, let $K$ be compact and choose a compact neighbourhood $V$ of $K$. For a compatible metric $d$, the functions $f_n(x)=\max(0,1-nd(x,K))$ decrease to $\mathbf1_K$ and, for sufficiently large $n$, have support in $V$. Indeed, compactness makes the distance from $K$ to the complement of the interior of $V$ positive. Dominated convergence now gives $\mu(K)=c\nu(K)$.

Choose increasing compact sets $K_n$ covering $G$. Every closed set $F$ is the union of the compact sets $F\cap K_n$, so compact sets generate the Borel $\sigma$-algebra. They form a $\pi$-system containing a countable cover of finite measure. The uniqueness theorem for measures (Volume~7, Theorem~7.5) yields $\mu=c\nu$ on all Borel sets.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Haar's theorem gives a left Haar measure on every locally compact group, unique up to a positive scalar. Haar treated compact metrizable and separable locally compact groups in 1933; Weil developed the unrestricted theory in 1940. The noncommutative uniqueness argument must keep track of both kinds of translation. We quote that general result; existence for the compact abelian groups used here will follow from a fixed point argument.
\end{remark}

\section{Invariant means and amenability}

Let $G$ now be any abstract group. A \emph{mean} on the bounded real functions $\ell^\infty(G)$ is a linear form $m$ with $m(\mathbf1)=1$ and $m(f)\ge0$ whenever $f\ge0$. Write $\lambda_gf(x)=f(g^{-1}x)$. The mean is \emph{invariant} if $m(\lambda_gf)=m(f)$ for every $f,g$; a group admitting one is \emph{amenable}. Finite groups have the usual averaging mean. The Banach limit of Exercise~\ref{ex:banachlimit} is the analogous invariant average on the semigroup $\N$. For infinite groups the fixed point theorem below supplies means without an explicit formula.

\begin{theorem}[Markov and Kakutani, 1936--38]\label{thm:markovkakutani}
Let $E$ be a Banach space and $K$ a non-empty convex weak* compact subset of $E^*$. A commuting family $\mathcal T$ of weak* continuous affine maps $K\to K$ has a common fixed point. Here affine means preserving convex combinations.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $T\in\mathcal T$ and $n\ge1$, average its first $n$ iterates:
\[A_n^T=\frac1n(I+T+\cdots+T^{n-1}).\]
Convexity keeps this map inside $K$. Affineness and commutation imply that all these averages commute: both orders of $A_n^SA_m^T$ give the average of $S^iT^jx$ over $i<n,j<m$.

Each image $A_n^T(K)$ is compact. A finite collection of these images has non-empty intersection, since the composition of their maps has image inside every one of them. Compactness therefore supplies a point $x_0$ belonging to all the images.

For fixed $T,n$, choose $y\in K$ with $x_0=A_n^Ty$. Telescoping gives $Tx_0-x_0=(T^ny-y)/n$. For $e\in E$, let $M_e=\max_{z\in K}|\langle z,e\rangle|$. Then
\[|\langle Tx_0-x_0,e\rangle|\le 2M_e/n.\]
Let $n$ grow. Every pairing of $Tx_0-x_0$ with an element of $E$ vanishes, so $Tx_0=x_0$. The same point works for every $T$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:abelianamenable}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Each abelian group admits an invariant mean.
\item[(b)] Each compact metrizable abelian group admits exactly one Haar probability measure.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(a) Put all means in $K\subset\ell^\infty(G)^*$. Positivity gives $|m(f)|\le\|f\|_\infty$ and $\|m\|=1$. The conditions $m(\mathbf1)=1$ and $m(f)\ge0$ for $f\ge0$ are weak* closed, so Banach--Alaoglu (Theorem~\ref{thm:alaoglu}) makes $K$ compact. It is convex and contains point evaluation. The maps $m\mapsto m\circ\lambda_g$ preserve $K$, are affine and weak* continuous, and commute because $G$ is abelian. Their common fixed point is the desired mean.

(b) Use $C(G)$ in place of $\ell^\infty(G)$. Riesz--Markov (Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszmarkov}) identifies its positive normalised forms with Borel probabilities. They again form a non-empty compact convex set. The commuting translations, characterised by
\[\int f\dd(T_g\mu)=\int f(x+g)\dd\mu(x),\]
have a common fixed point $\mu$. It charges every non-empty open set $U$: otherwise finitely many translates of $U$ covering $G$ would imply $\mu(G)=0$. Thus $\mu$ is Haar, and Theorem~\ref{thm:haaruniq} makes its probability normalisation unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:amenableext}
Suppose $N$ is normal in $G$ and both $N$ and $G/N$ are amenable. Then $G$ is amenable. This includes finite extensions of abelian groups and extensions with both kernel and quotient abelian.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose invariant means $m_N,m_Q$ on $N$ and $Q=G/N$. For bounded real $f$ on $G$, first average along a coset:
\[F(gN)=m_N\bigl(n\mapsto f(gn)\bigr).\]
Replacing $g$ by $gn_0$ translates the argument on $N$ by $n_0^{-1}$, so $F$ is well defined. Also $|F|\le\|f\|_\infty$. The form $m(f)=m_Q(F)$ is positive and takes $\mathbf1$ to $1$.

For $h\in G$, applying this construction to $\lambda_hf$ replaces $F(gN)$ by $F(h^{-1}gN)$. Invariance of $m_Q$ gives $m(\lambda_hf)=m(f)$. Finally, finite groups are amenable by averaging, and abelian groups by Corollary~\ref{cor:abelianamenable}.
\end{proof}

For planar rigid motions, the translation subgroup is $\R^2$ and the quotient is $O(2)$. In $O(2)$, the rotation subgroup $SO(2)$ is abelian and has index two. The proposition therefore makes the entire motion group amenable when regarded as an abstract group. The same reasoning applies on the line.

\begin{theorem}[Banach, 1923]\label{thm:banachmeasure}
For $d=1,2$, Lebesgue measure extends to a non-negative finitely additive function on every bounded subset of $\R^d$, invariant under all rigid motions.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $V$ consist of the bounded real functions with bounded support. For $f\in V$ define its upper integral by
\[p(f)=\inf\left\{\int h\dd\lambda:h\in V\text{ measurable},\ h\ge f\right\}.\]
Bounded support makes the infimum finite. The function $p$ is sublinear and agrees with the integral on measurable functions. Hahn--Banach (Theorem~\ref{thm:hb}) extends that integral to a linear form $L$ on $V$ with $L\le p$. If $f\ge0$, then $L(-f)\le p(-f)\le0$, proving positivity.

Let $G=\mathrm{Isom}(\R^d)$. For bounded $A$ put $\varphi_A(g)=L(\mathbf1_{g^{-1}A})$. The invariance of Lebesgue measure makes $p$ invariant under motions, hence
\[0\le\varphi_A(g)\le p(\mathbf1_A).\]
Choose an invariant mean $m$ on $G$ and set $\mu(A)=m(\varphi_A)$. Disjoint unions give sums of indicators, so linearity proves finite additivity. The identity $\varphi_{hA}=\lambda_h\varphi_A$ proves invariance. For measurable $A$, the function $\varphi_A$ is constantly $\lambda(A)$, giving the required extension.
\end{proof}

A dissection of a disc of radius $r>0$ into two congruent discs would now force $\pi r^2=2\pi r^2$. To see what fails in space, consider the free group $F_2$ on $a,b$. Its elements are reduced words in $a^{\pm1},b^{\pm1}$, with adjacent inverse pairs cancelled during multiplication. Denote by $W(s)$ the words starting with $s$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:f2paradox}
The free group has the disjoint decompositions
\[\begin{aligned}
F_2&=\{e\}\sqcup W(a)\sqcup W(a^{-1})\sqcup W(b)\sqcup W(b^{-1}),\\
F_2&=W(a)\sqcup aW(a^{-1})=W(b)\sqcup bW(b^{-1}).
\end{aligned}\]
It follows that $F_2$ is not amenable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Sorting by the first letter gives the first line. A word $w$ outside $W(a)$ has $a^{-1}w\in W(a^{-1})$, while multiplying any word of $W(a^{-1})$ by $a$ produces a word outside $W(a)$. This proves the second line for $a$; the argument for $b$ is identical with the letters exchanged.

An invariant mean would assign the same mass to a set and its translate. The last two decompositions would give mass $1$ to $W(a)\sqcup W(a^{-1})$ and also mass $1$ to $W(b)\sqcup W(b^{-1})$. These are disjoint subsets of a set of mass $1$, contradicting positivity and finite additivity.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Hausdorff, 1914]\label{lem:freerot}
Let $\phi$ rotate $\R^3$ about the $z$-axis and $\rho$ about the $x$-axis, both through $\arccos(1/3)$. These two rotations generate a free group: no non-empty reduced word in $\phi^{\pm1},\rho^{\pm1}$ equals the identity.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The four matrices are
\[\phi^{\pm1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp2\sqrt2&0\\\pm2\sqrt2&1&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho^{\pm1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&1&\mp2\sqrt2\\0&\pm2\sqrt2&1\end{pmatrix}.\]
We claim that a reduced word $w$ of length $k\ge1$ whose rightmost letter is $\phi^{\pm1}$ sends $(1,0,0)$ to
\[3^{-k}(a,b\sqrt2,c),\qquad a,b,c\in\Z,\quad 3\nmid b.\]
This holds at length one, where $(a,b,c)=(1,\pm2,0)$. Left multiplication changes the integer coordinates by
\[\begin{aligned}
\phi^{\pm1}:&(a',b',c')\mapsto(a'\mp4b',b'\pm2a',3c'),\\
\rho^{\pm1}:&(a',b',c')\mapsto(3a',b'\mp2c',c'\pm4b').
\end{aligned}\]
Suppose the claim holds for shorter words, and inspect the first two letters $\sigma\tau$ of $w$. Write the coordinates after $\tau$ as $(a',b',c')$, and before it as $(a'',b'',c'')$. If $\sigma$ is a $\phi$ letter and $\tau$ a $\rho$ letter, then $3\mid a'$ and the new $b$ is congruent to $b'$ modulo $3$. If their roles are reversed, $3\mid c'$ gives the same conclusion. If both are $\phi$ letters, reducedness forces their signs to agree, and the formulas give $b=2b'-9b''\equiv2b'\pmod3$. For two $\rho$ letters the same calculation gives $b=2b'-9b''$. In every case induction preserves $3\nmid b$. At length two the coordinates before $\tau$ are simply $(1,0,0)$, so the calculation still applies.

Thus such a word cannot fix $(1,0,0)$. If instead a non-empty reduced identity word ended in $\rho^{\pm1}$, conjugate it by $\phi$. Only its leading boundary can cancel; the resulting reduced word is still non-empty and ends in $\phi^{-1}$. That contradicts what we just proved.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hausdorff, 1914]\label{thm:hausdorff}
No non-negative finitely additive function on all subsets of $S^2$ can be rotation invariant and agree with surface area on Borel sets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $F=\langle\phi,\rho\rangle$. Every nonidentity element of this countable free group is a rotation fixing exactly two sphere points. Collect these points in the countable set $D$, and put $X=S^2\setminus D$. Conjugating a rotation shows that $D$ and $X$ are $F$-invariant. No nonidentity element fixes a point of $X$.

Use choice to select one point from each orbit in $X$, forming $M$. Each point of $X$ is uniquely $gm$ with $g\in F,m\in M$. Indeed, equality within one orbit would give a nontrivial stabiliser unless the group elements agree. Transport Proposition~\ref{prop:f2paradox} along this identification:
\[\begin{aligned}
X&=M\sqcup W(\phi)M\sqcup W(\phi^{-1})M\sqcup W(\rho)M\sqcup W(\rho^{-1})M,\\
X&=W(\phi)M\sqcup\phi W(\phi^{-1})M
 =W(\rho)M\sqcup\rho W(\rho^{-1})M.
\end{aligned}\]
If $\mu$ satisfied the stated requirements, then $\mu(D)=0$, since $D$ is a countable Borel set of surface area zero. Hence $\mu(X)=4\pi$. The second line and rotation invariance assign mass $4\pi$ to each pair of branches in the first line. Their disjoint union would have mass $8\pi\le\mu(X)$, an impossibility.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
To reach the full Banach--Tarski paradox, one absorbs $D$ by a rotation, extends the sphere pieces along radii, and absorbs the missing centre using a rotation about an offset axis. Tomkowicz and Wagon give the constructions in Chapters~1--3 of \emph{The Banach--Tarski Paradox}. The relevant group distinction is already visible: rigid motions are amenable in dimensions at most two, whereas in higher dimensions they contain $F_2$. Tarski's 1938 theorem, quoted here, characterises the general obstruction: an action admits an invariant finitely additive probability on all subsets exactly when it has no paradoxical decomposition.
\end{remark}

\section{Characters and duality}

For a locally compact abelian group $G$, a \emph{character} is a continuous homomorphism $\chi:G\to\mathbb T$. Pointwise multiplication makes these characters a group $\widehat G$, the \emph{dual group}. In the finite case this recovers Chapter~\ref{ch:finite}. Continue to write $e(t)=e^{2\pi it}$.

\begin{theorem}[some dual groups]\label{thm:duals}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] The characters of $\R^d$ are precisely $x\mapsto e(\xi\cdot x)$, with a unique $\xi\in\R^d$ for each character; thus $\widehat{\R^d}\cong\R^d$.
\item[(b)] The characters of $\Z$ are precisely $n\mapsto z^n$, with a unique $z\in\mathbb T$ for each character; thus $\widehat\Z\cong\mathbb T$.
\item[(c)] The characters of $\mathbb T$ are precisely $z\mapsto z^k$, with a unique $k\in\Z$ for each character; thus $\widehat{\mathbb T}\cong\Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Restricting to each coordinate splits a character into a product of one-dimensional characters, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:dualprod}(a). For $\chi:\R\to\mathbb T$, continuity at zero supplies $h>0$ such that $c=\int_0^h\chi(t)\dd t\ne0$. The identity
\[c\chi(x)=\int_x^{x+h}\chi(t)\dd t\]
makes $\chi$ continuously differentiable. Differentiation yields $c\chi'(x)=\chi(x)(\chi(h)-1)$, so $\chi(x)=e^{\kappa x}$ for a constant $\kappa$. Modulus one forces $\kappa=2\pi i\xi$ with $\xi$ real. Differentiating at zero also recovers $\xi$ uniquely.

(b) A homomorphism from $\Z$ is fixed by its value at $1$, and any $z\in\mathbb T$ gives the homomorphism $n\mapsto z^n$.

(c) Composing a character of $\mathbb T$ with $t\mapsto e(t)$ gives $e(\xi t)$ by (a). Period one forces $\xi\in\Z$. Conversely, every integer power is a continuous character, and the powers are distinct.
\end{proof}

Taking the dual twice brings back each of these groups, just as in Theorem~\ref{thm:finiteduality}. The integers and the circle exchange places; Euclidean space stays Euclidean. The topology on a dual is uniform convergence on compact sets. In these examples the identifications have the displayed usual topologies: testing on $[0,1]$ forces real frequencies to converge, testing at $1$ gives convergence for characters of $\Z$, and distinct powers on $\mathbb T$ cannot converge uniformly to $1$.

\begin{remark}
Pontryagin--van Kampen duality asserts that the evaluation map $G\to\widehat{\widehat G}$ is an isomorphism of topological groups for every locally compact abelian $G$. Pontryagin proved the compact and discrete cases in 1934, and van Kampen the general case in 1935. We quote the general theorem rather than use it as a premise. Its proof develops Fourier transformation $\hat f(\chi)=\int_Gf\overline\chi\dd\mu$ and Plancherel on $G$, with a suitable Haar normalisation on $\widehat G$; see Folland's \emph{A Course in Abstract Harmonic Analysis}. The real, circle and finite transforms already encountered, and the $p$-adic transform below, give concrete cases.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
An integer $k$ labels a character $z^k$ on the circle. That small observation accounts for the indexing of Fourier series. Decay of the coefficients records smoothness, convolution multiplies coefficients, and Parseval compares the two norms. The $p$-adic field has the same kind of frequency dictionary, although its small neighbourhoods are open subgroups rather than intervals.
\end{beauty}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-adic numbers as a locally compact field}\label{sec:padic}

Recall Volume~7, Theorem~2.10 and Corollary~2.11. Completing $\Q$ for $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$ gives $\Q_p$. Its compact integer ring $\Z_p=\{x:|x|_p\le1\}$ consists of unique digit expansions $\sum_{k\ge0}a_kp^k$, where $0\le a_k<p$. Multiplying any element of $\Q_p$ by a sufficiently large power of $p$ brings it into $\Z_p$, so only finitely many negative powers are needed in a general expansion.

Every ball $a+p^k\Z_p$ is both compact and open. The ultrametric inequality makes two such balls either nested or disjoint (Volume~7, Exercise~2.1). Also $\Q_p=\bigcup_{m\ge0}p^{-m}\Z_p$, establishing local compactness and $\sigma$-compactness. Figure~\ref{fig:zp} pictures the digit choices for $p=2$. We now assign masses to these balls and identify their frequencies.

\centering

is an infinite path down the tree, and Haar measure gives each ball at depth $k$ the mass $2^{-k}$: it is the law of a sequence of fair coin tosses.}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:haarqp}
Exactly one Haar measure $\mu_p$ on $\Q_p$ satisfies $\mu_p(\Z_p)=1$. For every ball it gives
\[\mu_p(a+p^k\Z_p)=p^{-k},\]
and for every $a\in\Q_p^*$ and Borel set $E$ it satisfies
\[\mu_p(aE)=|a|_p\mu_p(E).\]
On $\R$, Lebesgue measure satisfies the corresponding identity $\lambda(aE)=|a|\lambda(E)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:abelianamenable}(b) gives the compact group $\Z_p$ its Haar probability $\mu_0$. For $k\ge0$, the $p^k$ cosets of $p^k\Z_p$ partition $\Z_p$ into translates, so their masses are all $p^{-k}$.

The quotient $\Q_p/\Z_p$ is countable: the finite negative part of a digit expansion supplies a representative in $\Z[1/p]$. Choose one representative $c$ per coset and set
\[\mu_p(E)=\sum_c\mu_0\bigl((E-c)\cap\Z_p\bigr).\]
This defines a Borel measure. A compact set meets only finitely many of the open cosets, hence has finite measure. Every non-empty open set contains a ball, which has positive mass. Translation permutes cosets, and the adjustment of representatives translates within $\Z_p$, preserving $\mu_0$. Thus $\mu_p$ is invariant. A ball of radius corresponding to $k<0$ partitions into $p^{-k}$ cosets of $\Z_p$, proving the remaining ball formula. Uniqueness follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:haaruniq} and normalisation.

Multiplication by $a\ne0$ is a group automorphism, so $E\mapsto\mu_p(aE)$ is another Haar measure, say $c(a)\mu_p$. Evaluate at $\Z_p$. Writing $a=p^{v_p(a)}u$ with $u$ a unit gives $a\Z_p=p^{v_p(a)}\Z_p$, hence $c(a)=p^{-v_p(a)}=|a|_p$. The real scaling law is Volume~7, Theorem~9.7, in dimension one.
\end{proof}

The absolute value therefore measures a geometric change: it is the factor by which multiplication changes Haar mass. Put $|\cdot|_\infty=|\cdot|$. A nonzero rational has the factorisation $a=\pm\prod_pp^{v_p(a)}$, with only finitely many nonzero exponents, so
\[\prod_{v\in\{\infty,2,3,5,\dots\}}|a|_v
 =\prod_pp^{v_p(a)}\prod_pp^{-v_p(a)}=1.
\]
This is the \emph{product formula}. A rational's expansion at some places is exactly balanced by its contraction at others.

\subsection*{Characters and self-duality}

For $x=\sum_{i\ge-m}a_ip^i$, define the \emph{$p$-adic fractional part} by
\[\{x\}_p=\sum_{i<0}a_ip^i\in\Z[1/p]\cap[0,1[.\]
Subtracting it leaves an element of $\Z_p$. Consequently $\{x+y\}_p-\{x\}_p-\{y\}_p$ belongs to $\Z[1/p]\cap\Z_p=\Z$, and
\[\psi_p(x)=e(\{x\}_p)\]
is a homomorphism to $\mathbb T$. It is constant on cosets of the open subgroup $\Z_p$, hence continuous. Its kernel is precisely $\Z_p$, since the only integer fractional part in $[0,1[$ is zero. Let $\psi_{p,a}(x)=\psi_p(ax)$ for $a\in\Q_p$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:psiperp}
For $k\in\Z$ and $a\in\Q_p$, the character $\psi_{p,a}$ is identically one on $p^k\Z_p$ exactly when $a\in p^{-k}\Z_p$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The kernel description turns triviality into $ap^k\Z_p\subset\Z_p$. This says $|a|_pp^{-k}\le1$, which is equivalent to the stated membership.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[$\Q_p$ is self-dual]\label{thm:qpselfdual}
Each character of $\Q_p$ has the form $\psi_{p,a}$ for exactly one $a\in\Q_p$. The map $a\mapsto\psi_{p,a}$ is an isomorphism $\Q_p\cong\widehat{\Q_p}$ of topological groups.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The map preserves addition. If $\psi_{p,a}=1$, the lemma puts $a$ in every ball $p^{-k}\Z_p$, forcing $a=0$; this proves injectivity.

Take any continuous character $\chi$ of $\Q_p$. The arc $\{z\in\mathbb T:\Re z>0\}$ contains no nontrivial subgroup: repeated powers of any $z\ne1$ leave that arc. Continuity puts $\chi(p^k\Z_p)$ inside it for some $k$. This image is a subgroup, so $\chi$ is trivial on $p^k\Z_p$.

For $n\ge\max(0,-k)$, the quotient $p^{-n}\Z_p/p^k\Z_p$ is cyclic of order $p^{n+k}$, and therefore has that many characters. Restrictions of $\psi_{p,a}$ with $a\in p^{-k}\Z_p$ give all of them: by the lemma two restrictions agree exactly when $a-a'\in p^n\Z_p$, leaving exactly $p^{n+k}$ choices. Choose $a_n$ representing $\chi$ on $p^{-n}\Z_p$. Agreement on the smaller subgroup gives $a_{n+1}-a_n\in p^n\Z_p$. Thus $(a_n)$ is Cauchy. Its limit $a$ is congruent to $a_n$ modulo $p^n\Z_p$, so $\psi_{p,a}$ agrees with $\chi$ on each $p^{-n}\Z_p$, and hence everywhere.

It remains to check the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets. Each compact subset of $\Q_p$ lies in some $p^{-n}\Z_p$. When $a-a'\in p^n\Z_p$, the corresponding characters agree on that whole subgroup. This proves continuity of the map. Conversely, suppose a character $\psi_{p,a}$ has positive real part throughout the compact subgroup $p^{-n}\Z_p$. Its image is a subgroup in the arc above, so it is trivial there, and the lemma gives $a\in p^n\Z_p$. These neighbourhood tests prove continuity of the inverse.
\end{proof}

Define the Fourier transform using this frequency parameter:
\[\hat f(y)=\int_{\Q_p}f(x)\overline{\psi_p(xy)}\dd\mu_p(x).\]
Here the convenient test functions are locally constant functions of compact support. Compactness lets us cover their supports by finitely many balls on which they are constant and refine to disjoint balls. Thus they are precisely finite linear combinations of ball indicators. Their space is the \emph{Schwartz--Bruhat space} $\mathcal S(\Q_p)$.

\begin{theorem}[Fourier analysis on $\Q_p$]\label{thm:qpfourier}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] The function $\mathbf1_{\Z_p}$ transforms into itself. For any ball,
\[\widehat{\mathbf1_{a+p^k\Z_p}}(y)=p^{-k}\overline{\psi_p(ay)}\,\mathbf1_{p^{-k}\Z_p}(y).\]
\item[(b)] Fourier transformation is a bijection of $\mathcal S(\Q_p)$ onto itself, and its square satisfies $\hat{\hat f}(x)=f(-x)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) The substitution $x=a+p^kt$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:haarqp} give
\[\widehat{\mathbf1_{a+p^k\Z_p}}(y)
 =p^{-k}\overline{\psi_p(ay)}\int_{\Z_p}\overline{\psi_p(p^kty)}\dd\mu_0(t).
\]
A character $\eta$ of a compact group has integral zero unless it is trivial: translation by $t_0$ gives $\int\eta=\eta(t_0)\int\eta$. For this character, Lemma~\ref{lem:psiperp} makes triviality equivalent to $y\in p^{-k}\Z_p$. In that case the integral is one, proving (a).

(b) The formula in (a) is locally constant with compact support, so the transform preserves the test space. To square it, put $g(y)=\overline{\psi_p(ay)}\mathbf1_{p^{-k}\Z_p}(y)$. Character orthogonality on the subgroup of mass $p^k$ gives
\[\hat g(x)=\int_{p^{-k}\Z_p}\overline{\psi_p(y(x+a))}\dd\mu_p(y)
 =p^k\mathbf1_{p^k\Z_p}(x+a).
\]
Multiplying by $p^{-k}$ yields $\hat{\hat f}(x)=\mathbf1_{-a+p^k\Z_p}(x)=f(-x)$ for ball indicators. Linearity proves it for every test function. Reflection then supplies an inverse, proving surjectivity.
\end{proof}

The Gaussian $e^{-\pi x^2}$ on $\R$ and the indicator $\mathbf1_{\Z_p}$ on $\Q_p$ both transform into themselves; compare equation~\eqref{eq:gaussft}. There is a sign choice to remember when assembling the places. Volume~13 uses $\psi_\infty(x)=e(-x)$, so its real transform $\int f(x)\overline{\psi_\infty(xy)}\dd x$ is Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}'s transform evaluated at $-y$. This choice makes the combined adelic character trivial on the diagonal copy of $\Q$.

\begin{beauty}
At the real place choose a Gaussian; at each prime choose $\mathbf1_{\Z_p}$. Each local Fourier transform preserves that choice. Volume~13 combines them on the adèle ring, where $\Q$ is discrete and the quotient by $\Q$ is compact. Its Poisson formula then gives the functional equation of $\zeta(s)$. Exercise~\ref{ex:localzeta} computes the local integrals that produce its Gamma and Euler factors.
\end{beauty}

\section{From invariant area to local Fourier transforms}

Banach's planar area rests on averaging: the motion group is assembled from amenable groups, and an invariant mean turns a Hahn--Banach extension into an invariant area. The free subgroup in spatial rotations prevents the same construction on the sphere. These arguments use finite additivity on arbitrary sets; Haar measure instead keeps countable additivity on Borel sets.

For the compact abelian case, Markov--Kakutani supplies Haar probability and Theorem~\ref{thm:haaruniq} supplies uniqueness. On $\Q_p$, ball masses determine the normalisation and multiplication scales them by $|a|_p$. Its characters are exactly $\psi_{p,a}$, so the field labels its own frequencies. Together with the real, circle and discrete examples, this gives several direct instances of the general duality theorem, whose full proof we have quoted.

The next chapter develops growth estimates for entire functions and the Mellin transform. Those estimates lead to the prime number theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:tauberian}; the local theory here returns in Volume~13.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:axb}
\emph{Two measures on affine maps.} Give $G=\{(a,b):a>0,b\in\R\}$ the product $(a,b)(a',b')=(aa',ab'+b)$, corresponding to composition of affine maps. Prove left invariance of $a^{-2}\dd a\dd b$ and right invariance of $a^{-1}\dd a\dd b$. Find $\Delta$ using the convention $\mu(Eg)=\Delta(g)\mu(E)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:localzeta}
\emph{Euler factors as integrals.} For real $s>0$, establish
\[\int_{\Z_p\setminus\{0\}}|x|_p^{s-1}\dd\mu_p(x)=\frac{1-p^{-1}}{1-p^{-s}}.\]
Use it to prove $\prod_p(1-p^{-1})^{-1}\int_{\Z_p}|x|_p^{s-1}\dd\mu_p(x)=\zeta(s)$ for $s>1$, where the value of the integrand at zero can be assigned arbitrarily. Evaluate the real counterpart $\int_\R e^{-\pi x^2}|x|^{s-1}\dd x$ as well.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:folner}
\emph{Nearly invariant finite sets.} A sequence of finite non-empty subsets $F_n$ of a group $G$ is a \emph{F{\o}lner sequence} if $|gF_n\triangle F_n|/|F_n|\to0$ for each $g\in G$. (a) Construct one in $\Z^d$. (b) Take a weak* limit point of the averaging means on $F_n$ to prove amenability of any group with such a sequence. (c) Infer that $F_2$ has no F{\o}lner sequence. For its word balls $B_n$, show directly that $|aB_n\triangle B_n|/|B_n|$ stays away from zero.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:qzdual}
\emph{Frequencies of the rational quotient.} Write $\hat\Z=\prod_p\Z_p$. For $a=(a_p)\in\hat\Z$, define
\[\chi_a(x)=e\left(\sum_p\{a_px\}_p\right)\qquad(x\in\Q).\]
Prove that this defines a character on discrete $\Q/\Z$ and that $a\mapsto\chi_a$ identifies $\hat\Z$ with $\widehat{\Q/\Z}$, including their topologies.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:circlerotation}
\emph{An irrational rotation.} Fix irrational $\alpha$ and let $Tz=e(\alpha)z$ on $\mathbb T$. (a) Compare Fourier coefficients to show that Haar probability is the only $T$-invariant Borel probability. (b) Adapt Theorem~\ref{thm:banachmeasure}, using a mean on $\Z$, to construct a $T$-invariant finitely additive probability on all subsets of the circle. (c) Choose representatives for the orbits of $T$ to show that no such extension can be countably additive.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c13a}
\emph{Convolving two balls.} In $\Q_p$ let $B=p^k\Z_p$ and define $(f*g)(x)=\int f(t)g(x-t)\dd\mu_p(t)$. For $a,b\in\Q_p$, prove
\[\mathbf1_{a+B}*\mathbf1_{b+B}=p^{-k}\mathbf1_{a+b+B}.\]
Check the identity by multiplying the two Fourier transforms. What normalisation makes $\mathbf1_B$ an idempotent under convolution?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c13b}
\emph{Dilation and frequency.} For $a\in\Q_p^*$ set $D_af(x)=f(ax)$. Prove, for $f\in\mathcal S(\Q_p)$ and $1\le r<\infty$,
\[\|D_af\|_r=|a|_p^{-1/r}\|f\|_r,\qquad
\widehat{D_af}(y)=|a|_p^{-1}\hat f(y/a).\]
Deduce that $U_af=|a|_p^{1/2}f(ax)$ is unitary on $L^2(\Q_p)$. Explain why its inverse is $U_{a^{-1}}$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c13c}
\emph{A finite dual inside a local field.} For $k\ge1$, find all characters of $\Z_p$ that are trivial on $p^k\Z_p$. Show that they are $x\mapsto\psi_p(bx/p^k)$, with $b$ running over the integers modulo $p^k$. For a function constant on cosets of $p^k\Z_p$, prove that its integral over $\Z_p$ is the average of its $p^k$ values. Recover the finite character orthogonality relation from this integral.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c13d}
\emph{Every orbit gives the same average.} With $T$ as in Exercise~\ref{ex:circlerotation}, prove that for every continuous $f$ on $\mathbb T$,
\[\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f(T^jz)\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb T}f\dd\mu\]
uniformly in $z$. If $f(z)=\sum_{|k|\le M}c_kz^k$, bound the error by
\[\frac2N\sum_{0<|k|\le M}\frac{|c_k|}{|1-e(k\alpha)|}.\]
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}
\begin{solution}{ex:axb}
\idea Translation changes the coordinates and the area element. Choose the density so that the two changes cancel.
\writeup For $g_0=(a_0,b_0)$, left translation is $(a,b)\mapsto(a_0a,a_0b+b_0)$, with Jacobian $a_0^2$. Its pullback changes $a^{-2}\dd a\dd b$ by the factor $a_0^{-2}a_0^2=1$. Right translation is $(a,b)\mapsto(aa_0,ab_0+b)$, whose Jacobian matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}a_0&0\\b_0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Thus its determinant is $a_0$, cancelled by the change in $a^{-1}$. Both densities are positive and continuous, hence locally finite and positive on non-empty open sets. Finally, right translation changes the left density by $a_0^{-2}a_0=a_0^{-1}$, giving $\Delta(a_0,b_0)=a_0^{-1}$. A convention using $Eg^{-1}$ would give its reciprocal.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:localzeta}
\idea Valuation makes the $p$-adic integral a geometric series; a quadratic substitution makes the real integral a Gamma function.
\writeup The singleton zero has measure zero, since it lies in balls of mass $p^{-v}$ for every $v$. The shell $p^v\Z_p^*$ has mass $p^{-v}(1-p^{-1})$ and $|x|_p=p^{-v}$. Hence
\[\int_{\Z_p\setminus\{0\}}|x|_p^{s-1}\dd\mu_p
 =(1-p^{-1})\sum_{v\ge0}p^{-vs}=\frac{1-p^{-1}}{1-p^{-s}}.
\]
For $s>1$, division by $1-p^{-1}$ and the Euler product (Volume~5, Theorem~10.3) give the asserted product. At the real place, evenness and $u=\pi x^2$ give
\[\int_\R e^{-\pi x^2}|x|^{s-1}\dd x
 =\pi^{-s/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}u^{s/2-1}\dd u
 =\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2).
\]
Thus the real factor times the Euler product is the completed zeta function $\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)$. Its symmetry under $s\mapsto1-s$ is proved by adelic Poisson summation in Volume~13.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:folner}
\idea Boundary errors vanish for large cubes. Compactness then turns nearly invariant averages into an invariant mean.
\writeup (a) Take $F_n=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^d$. A translation by $g$ changes at most $2d|g|_\infty n^{d-1}$ points in symmetric difference, so its relative error tends to zero.

(b) Define $m_n(f)=|F_n|^{-1}\sum_{x\in F_n}f(x)$. Changing variables in the translated average yields
\[|m_n(\lambda_gf)-m_n(f)|\le\frac{|g^{-1}F_n\triangle F_n|}{|F_n|}\|f\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.
\]
Let $M$ be the weak* compact set of means from Corollary~\ref{cor:abelianamenable}. For each $N$, take the closure $C_N$ in $M$ of $\{m_n:n\ge N\}$. These nonempty closed sets are nested, so compactness gives a mean $m\in\bigcap_N C_N$. Fix $f,g$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The displayed estimate is at most $\varepsilon$ on a sufficiently late tail. The same bound holds on its closure, because $\ell\mapsto\ell(\lambda_gf)-\ell(f)$ is weak* continuous. It therefore holds for $m$. Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ proves invariance for every $f,g$.

(c) An assumed F{\o}lner sequence in $F_2$ would contradict Proposition~\ref{prop:f2paradox}. For the direct count, there are $4\cdot3^{k-1}$ words of length $k\ge1$, hence $|B_n|=2\cdot3^n-1$. The $3^n$ words of length $n+1$ starting with $a$ belong to $aB_n\setminus B_n$. Therefore
\[\frac{|aB_n\triangle B_n|}{|B_n|}\ge\frac{3^n}{2\cdot3^n-1}>\frac12.
\]
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:qzdual}
\idea Prime-power denominators split $\Q/\Z$ into independent pieces. Their characters assemble into a product of $p$-adic integers.
\writeup Only primes dividing the denominator of $x$ can contribute a nonzero fractional part. Fractional parts are additive modulo $\Z$, so $\chi_a$ is a homomorphism. It is trivial on $\Z$ and continuous on the discrete quotient. Addition of parameters multiplies characters.

For $x=p^{-k}$, all terms except the $p$ term vanish. If $\chi_a=1$, then $\psi_{p,a_p}$ is trivial on $p^{-k}\Z$ and on $\Z_p$, and therefore on $p^{-k}\Z_p=p^{-k}\Z+\Z_p$. Lemma~\ref{lem:psiperp} gives $a_p\in p^k\Z_p$ for every $k$, so $a_p=0$. This proves injectivity.

Conversely, let $\chi$ be a character of $\Q/\Z$. The identities $\Z[1/p]+\Z_p=\Q_p$ and $\Z[1/p]\cap\Z_p=\Z$ identify its restriction to $\Z[1/p]/\Z$ with a character of $\Q_p/\Z_p$. Pulling back to $\Q_p$ gives a continuous character, since the kernel contains the open subgroup $\Z_p$. Theorem~\ref{thm:qpselfdual} and Lemma~\ref{lem:psiperp} represent it uniquely by $a_p\in\Z_p$. Partial fractions express each rational modulo integers as a finite sum of prime-power denominator terms. Thus $\chi=\chi_a$ for $a=(a_p)$.

For the topology, compact subsets of a discrete group are finite. Testing finitely many rational arguments depends on only finitely many residue classes of the $a_p$, so the map from $\prod_p\Z_p$ is continuous. That product is compact: enumerate the primes, use compactness in each coordinate and a diagonal subsequence for a compatible product metric. The character space is Hausdorff under pointwise convergence, so the continuous bijection has continuous inverse.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:circlerotation}
\idea Fourier coefficients settle uniqueness on Borel sets. An invariant mean extends the measure to all sets, but orbit representatives prevent countable additivity.
\writeup (a) If $\nu$ is invariant, put $c_k=\int z^{-k}\dd\nu$. Then $c_k=e(-k\alpha)c_k$, so irrationality forces $c_k=0$ for $k\ne0$, while $c_0=1$. These are Haar's coefficients. Trigonometric polynomials are dense in $C(\mathbb T)$ by Fej\'er or Stone--Weierstrass, so the measures integrate every continuous function equally. The measure-uniqueness argument in Theorem~\ref{thm:haaruniq} gives equality of the Borel measures.

(b) The upper-integral Hahn--Banach construction in Theorem~\ref{thm:banachmeasure} extends the Haar integral to a positive form $L$ on all bounded real functions, with $L(\mathbf1)=1$. If $m$ is an invariant mean on $\Z$, set
\[\nu(A)=m\bigl(n\mapsto L(\mathbf1_{T^{-n}A})\bigr).
\]
The argument of $m$ lies between zero and one. Linearity gives finite additivity, shifting $n$ gives $T$-invariance, and $\nu(\mathbb T)=1$. For Borel $A$ it agrees with Haar probability.

(c) Choose one point per orbit, forming $V$. Irrationality makes the sets $T^nV$, $n\in\Z$, disjoint, and they cover the circle. Invariance gives them all the same mass $c$. Countable additivity would give $1=\sum_{n\in\Z}c$, which is zero if $c=0$ and divergent if $c>0$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c13a}
\idea The convolution counts the intersection of two cosets of the same subgroup.
\writeup For fixed $x$, the permissible $t$ lie in $(a+B)\cap(x-b+B)$. This intersection is $a+B$ when $x\in a+b+B$, and empty otherwise, proving the formula. By Theorem~\ref{thm:qpfourier}, the product of the transforms is
\[p^{-2k}\overline{\psi_p((a+b)y)}\mathbf1_{p^{-k}\Z_p}(y),\]
which is the transform of the asserted convolution. The normalised indicator $p^k\mathbf1_B$ has integral one and satisfies $(p^k\mathbf1_B)*(p^k\mathbf1_B)=p^k\mathbf1_B$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c13b}
\idea Scaling the variable shrinks its Haar volume by the inverse absolute value and rescales the character's parameter.
\writeup With $t=ax$, Proposition~\ref{prop:haarqp} gives
\[\int|f(ax)|^r\dd\mu_p(x)=|a|_p^{-1}\int|f(t)|^r\dd\mu_p(t).
\]
The same substitution in the Fourier integral gives
\[\widehat{D_af}(y)=|a|_p^{-1}\int f(t)\overline{\psi_p(ty/a)}\dd\mu_p(t).
\]
These are the two formulas. The measure substitution applies to every non-negative Borel function, so the norm identity extends directly to all $L^2$ classes. It makes $U_a$ an isometry. Multiplicativity of $|\cdot|_p$ gives $U_aU_{a^{-1}}=I=U_{a^{-1}}U_a$, establishing surjectivity and unitarity.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c13c}
\idea A character trivial on the small ball factors through a finite cyclic quotient.
\writeup Each coset has an integer representative modulo $p^k$, making $\Z_p/p^k\Z_p$ cyclic of order $p^k$. Its characters are $j\mapsto e(bj/p^k)$, one per residue $b$. On integers these equal $\psi_p(bj/p^k)$, and the kernel condition extends the equality throughout each coset. Since every coset has mass $p^{-k}$, a function with values $F(j)$ integrates to $p^{-k}\sum_{j=0}^{p^k-1}F(j)$. Applied to the product of one character with the conjugate of another, this average is one for equal residues $b$ and zero otherwise, by the finite geometric sum.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c13d}
\idea Every nonconstant frequency averages away. Uniform approximation carries that fact from polynomials to continuous functions.
\writeup For $k\ne0$, irrationality gives $e(k\alpha)\ne1$, and
\[\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(T^jz)^k
 =\frac{z^k(1-e(Nk\alpha))}{N(1-e(k\alpha))}.
\]
Its modulus is at most $2/(N|1-e(k\alpha)|)$, independently of $z$. Summing with coefficients $c_k$ gives the required polynomial bound, since its Haar integral is $c_0$. For continuous $f$ choose a trigonometric polynomial $P$ with $\|f-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Both the orbit average and the Haar integral change by less than $\varepsilon$ when $f$ is replaced by $P$. The total error is therefore at most $2\varepsilon$ plus the polynomial error, uniformly in $z$. First let $N\to\infty$, then $\varepsilon\to0$.
\end{solution}
