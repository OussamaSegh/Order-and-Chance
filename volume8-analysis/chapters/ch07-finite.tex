\chapter{Fourier Analysis on Finite Abelian Groups}\label{ch:finite}

\begin{center}\itshape
On a finite group, frequency coordinates require only finite sums.\\
Their algebra still detects sparse signals, arithmetic patterns and linearity.
\end{center}

Take the $p\times p$ Fourier matrix $(\zeta^{jk})$, where $p$ is prime and $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/p}$. Choose equally many rows and columns in any way. Must the resulting square matrix be invertible? At size five there are already $251$ nonempty square minors to consider. Chebotarev's 1926 lemma answers all sizes at once. Its proof detects divisibility by $p$ in a determinant's leading Taylor coefficient.

Tao used that lemma to give a sharp support uncertainty principle for prime cyclic groups. A signal with only a few nonzero values cannot have many zero Fourier coefficients. In particular, sufficiently many exact frequency samples determine a sparse signal uniquely. Stable recovery from fewer random samples asks further questions; Cand\`es, Romberg and Tao's work on compressed sensing developed that theory. Uniqueness alone is not a recovery algorithm or a noise estimate.

We use the finite-character basis from Volume~3, Section~11.4, and the power-of-two FFT from Volume~5, Chapter~7. Subgroup duality will organise averages and fast transforms. Exact Gauss sums will turn the Chinese remainder theorem into quadratic reciprocity. Pattern counts will then give a cap-set bound and the Blum--Luby--Rubinfeld linearity test. The arguments share Fourier identities, but each has its own quantitative hypotheses.

\section{Characters and duality}

Let $G$ be a finite abelian group, written additively. A character is a homomorphism $\chi:G\to\C^*$. Since $|G|x=0$, each value satisfies $\chi(x)^{|G|}=1$; in particular its modulus is one and its inverse is its conjugate. Characters form a group $\widehat G$ under pointwise multiplication, with $|\widehat G|=|G|$ by Volume~3, Section~11.4. Write $e(t)=e^{2\pi it}$. On $\Z/n\Z$ the characters are $\chi_b(x)=e(bx/n)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dualprod}
(a) Restricting a character to the two factors gives an isomorphism
$\widehat{G\times H}\to\widehat G\times\widehat H$,
$\chi\mapsto(\chi(\cdot,0),\chi(0,\cdot))$.
(b) Every finite abelian group is isomorphic to its dual.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The restriction map preserves products, and $\chi(x,y)=\chi(x,0)\chi(0,y)$ proves injectivity. Conversely a pair of characters gives $(x,y)\mapsto\psi(x)\phi(y)$, proving surjectivity. For a cyclic group, choosing an $n$th root for $\chi(1)$ determines exactly one character, so $b\mapsto\chi_b$ is an isomorphism from $\Z/n\Z$ to its dual. Decompose a finite abelian group into cyclic factors (Volume~3, Corollary~7.7) and apply (a).
\end{proof}

The single-dual isomorphism depends on choices of cyclic generators and their corresponding characters. Evaluation into the double dual needs none of them.

\begin{theorem}[duality]\label{thm:finiteduality}
The evaluation map $\operatorname{ev}:G\to\widehat{\widehat G}$, defined by $\operatorname{ev}(x)(\chi)=\chi(x)$, is an isomorphism.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication of characters makes $\operatorname{ev}(x)$ itself a character of the dual. The character identity in $x$ makes evaluation a homomorphism. If $x\ne0$, orthogonality gives $\sum_{\chi\in\widehat G}\chi(x)=0$ (Volume~3, Theorem~11.14). Thus not all characters take value one at $x$, proving its kernel is zero. Both groups have $|G|$ elements, so injectivity gives surjectivity.
\end{proof}

Chapter~\ref{ch:haar} states Pontryagin duality for locally compact abelian groups and proves the self-duality of $\Q_p$. The finite version already identifies the dual of a quotient and the characters extending from a subgroup.

\begin{definition}
For $H\le G$, its annihilator is the subgroup
$H^\perp=\{\chi\in\widehat G:\chi(h)=1\text{ for every }h\in H\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:annihilator}
For a subgroup $H\le G$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $H^\perp\cong\widehat{G/H}$, so $|H^\perp|=|G|/|H|$.
\item[(b)] Every character of $H$ extends to $G$, and restriction identifies $\widehat G/H^\perp$ with $\widehat H$.
\item[(c)] Under evaluation into the double dual, $(H^\perp)^\perp=H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) A character trivial on $H$ factors uniquely through $G/H$. Composition with the quotient map reverses this construction, giving the asserted isomorphism and size.
(b) Restriction has kernel $H^\perp$. Its image therefore has size $|\widehat G|/|H^\perp|=|H|=|\widehat H|$, hence is all of $\widehat H$. The first isomorphism theorem gives the quotient assertion.
(c) Every $h\in H$ is annihilated by every character in $H^\perp$, so $H\subseteq(H^\perp)^\perp$. Applying (a) within $\widehat G$ gives the latter subgroup size $|\widehat G|/|H^\perp|=|H|$. Equal finite sizes make the inclusion equality.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The Fourier transform}

Keep Volume~3's normalisation: average on the original group and sum on the dual. Set
\[
\hat f(\chi)=\mathbb E_x f(x)\overline{\chi(x)},\qquad
\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb E_x f(x)\overline{g(x)},\qquad
(f*g)(x)=\mathbb E_yf(y)g(x-y),
\]
where $\mathbb E_x=|G|^{-1}\sum_{x\in G}$. The character basis gives $f(x)=\sum_\chi\hat f(\chi)\chi(x)$ and $\mathbb E|f|^2=\sum_\chi|\hat f(\chi)|^2$ (Volume~3, Theorem~11.14).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:finiteconv}
For functions $f,g$ on $G$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_\chi\hat f(\chi)\overline{\hat g(\chi)}$.
\item[(b)] $\widehat{f*g}=\hat f\hat g$.
\item[(c)] Translation $\tau_af(x)=f(x-a)$ has transform $\overline{\chi(a)}\hat f(\chi)$, and multiplication by a character $\psi$ has transform $\widehat{\psi f}(\chi)=\hat f(\bar\psi\chi)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand both functions in the orthonormal character basis to get (a). For (b), substitute $x=y+z$ in the double average; $\overline{\chi(y+z)}=\overline{\chi(y)}\overline{\chi(z)}$ separates the two sums. For translation substitute $x=y+a$. For multiplication use $\psi(x)\overline{\chi(x)}=\overline{(\bar\psi\chi)(x)}$. All sums are finite, so no interchange conditions are missing.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Poisson summation]\label{thm:poissonfinite}
For $H\le G$, any function $f$ and any $x\in G$,
\[
\mathbb E_{h\in H}f(x+h)=\sum_{\chi\in H^\perp}\hat f(\chi)\chi(x).
\]
At $x=0$, this identifies the subgroup average with $\sum_{\chi\in H^\perp}\hat f(\chi)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Average the inversion formula for $f(x+h)$. A restricted character on $H$ has average one if trivial and zero otherwise: multiplying the average by a value $\chi(h_0)\ne1$ leaves it unchanged after reindexing $h\mapsto h+h_0$, forcing it to vanish. Thus exactly the annihilator terms remain.
\end{proof}

For $H=\{0\}$ we recover inversion; for $H=G$ we recover the mean. In $\Z/n\Z$, the subgroup generated by $d\mid n$ has annihilator frequencies divisible by $n/d$. Its coset averages can therefore be read from that smaller frequency set. The coding application in Exercise~\ref{ex:macwilliams} applies the same identity to a product of coordinate weights.

\begin{beauty}
A subgroup average erases precisely the characters that vary on that subgroup. The surviving frequencies are its annihilator. This is the finite form of the lattice summation in Theorem~\ref{thm:poissonlattice}; Haar measure in Chapter~\ref{ch:haar} extends the averaging operation to infinite groups. The adelic version belongs to Volume~13.
\end{beauty}

\section{A fast transform for every finite abelian group}

The defining sum costs quadratically many operations in $|G|$. A subgroup splits it into smaller transforms, provided its characters have been extended in advance.

\begin{lemma}[splitting along a subgroup]\label{lem:split}
Let $T$ be a set of representatives for $G/H$. For each $\psi\in\widehat H$, choose an extension $\chi_\psi\in\widehat G$. Let $F_t$ be the Fourier transform on $H$ of $h\mapsto f(t+h)$. Every character of $G$ occurs exactly once as $\chi_\psi\eta$, with $\eta\in H^\perp$, and
\[
\hat f(\chi_\psi\eta)=\mathbb E_{t\in T}
\overline{\eta(t)}\,\overline{\chi_\psi(t)}F_t(\psi).
\]
For fixed $\psi$, this is a Fourier transform on $G/H$, identifying $H^\perp$ with its dual.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For any $\chi$ restricting to $\psi$, the quotient $\chi\bar\chi_\psi$ is trivial on $H$, giving its unique $\eta$. Each group element is uniquely $t+h$, so the transform becomes
$\mathbb E_t\overline{\chi(t)}\mathbb E_hf(t+h)\overline{\chi(h)}$.
The inner average is $F_t(\psi)$, and splitting the outer character factor gives the formula. Since $\eta$ is trivial on $H$, its value depends only on the represented coset, as required for the quotient transform.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[fast Fourier transform]\label{thm:fftgeneral}
If $|G|=p_1\cdots p_r$ is its prime factorisation with repetitions, the Fourier transform can be computed in at most $|G|\sum_{i=1}^r(p_i+2)$ operations once suitable character values are tabulated. An operation here allows one complex multiplication and one addition.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The trivial group needs zero operations. Otherwise choose a prime-index subgroup $H$, of index $p_1$: the cyclic-factor decomposition supplies it by multiplying one factor by a prime dividing that factor's order. The splitting lemma uses $p_1$ transforms on $H$, then $|G|$ multiplications by the tabulated factors $p_1^{-1}\overline{\chi_\psi(t)}$, then $|H|$ direct transforms of length $p_1$. If $C(G)$ denotes the cost, induction gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
C(G)&\le p_1C(H)+|G|+|H|p_1^2\\
&\le|G|\left(\sum_{i=2}^r(p_i+2)+1+p_1\right)
\le|G|\sum_{i=1}^r(p_i+2).
\end{aligned}
\]
The convention for one operation overestimates any isolated multiplication, so these counts are valid upper bounds.
\end{proof}

For a $2$-group of order $2^k$, the bound is $4k2^k$. It recovers Cooley--Tukey splitting for cyclic groups and the Walsh--Hadamard transform for $\mathbb F_2^n$, used again in Section~\ref{sec:blr}.

\portrait{hadamard}{Jacques Hadamard (1865--1963). Photograph. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

A large prime factor makes this particular bound expensive. Rader's 1968 reduction improves the prime cyclic case: using a primitive root, write nonzero input indices as $g^u$ and nonzero frequency indices as $g^{-v}$. The phase then depends only on $u-v$ modulo $p-1$, turning the nonzero part into a cyclic convolution of length $p-1$. Compute that convolution by zero-padding a linear convolution to a power-of-two length at least $2(p-1)-1$, applying the already available power-of-two FFT, and folding the result back cyclically. The padded length is less than $4(p-1)$, so the cost is $O(p\log p)$, without assuming an arbitrary-length FFT in the reduction. Subgroup splitting with these prime transforms gives $O(|G|\sum_i\log p_i)=O(|G|\log|G|)$ for all finite abelian groups. The fixed twiddle multiplications add only $O(|G|)$ per prime stage, absorbed because $\log p_i\ge\log2$.

\section{Uncertainty}

On a finite group, support size measures concentration directly. The transform cannot make both supports too small.

\begin{theorem}[Donoho and Stark, 1989]\label{thm:donohostark}
A nonzero function on $G$ satisfies
$|\operatorname{supp}f|\,|\operatorname{supp}\hat f|\ge|G|$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $A=\operatorname{supp}f$ and $B=\operatorname{supp}\hat f$. Inversion, Cauchy--Schwarz and Parseval give, for every $x$,
\[
|f(x)|\le\sum_{\chi\in B}|\hat f(\chi)|
\le\sqrt{|B|}\,(\mathbb E|f|^2)^{1/2}
\le\sqrt{\frac{|A||B|}{|G|}}\,\|f\|_\infty.
\]
Take a maximum point and cancel its positive norm to obtain the bound.
\end{proof}

For a subgroup indicator, character orthogonality gives $\widehat{\mathbf1_H}=(|H|/|G|)\mathbf1_{H^\perp}$. Its support product is exactly $|G|$. Translation and multiplication by a character preserve the two support sizes, so every $c\psi\mathbf1_{a+H}$ with $c\ne0$ is also extremal. Exercise~\ref{ex:dsequality} proves the converse classification.

\begin{think}
For a prime $p\ge7$, could both support sizes be $\lceil\sqrt p\rceil$? The product bound permits it. The sharper sum bound below rules it out. The small primes $2,3,5$ are exceptions to this particular question; at $p=5$, Exercise~\ref{ex:v8c12b} constructs supports of size three on both sides.
\end{think}

Chebotarev's proof needs the rectangular determinant identity Cauchy--Binet. Here is its derivation before use. For $n\times m$ matrices $P,Q$, expand the columns of $PQ^{\mathsf T}$ by multilinearity of the determinant. Terms choosing a repeated column of $P$ vanish. For a fixed $n$-element column set $S$, summing over its orderings gives $\det(P_S)\det(Q_S)$ by the permutation formula. Thus $\det(PQ^{\mathsf T})=\sum_{|S|=n}\det(P_S)\det(Q_S)$. This uses a transpose, without conjugation; complex entries cause no change.

\begin{lemma}[Chebotarev, 1926]\label{lem:chebotarev}
Let $p$ be prime and $\zeta=e(1/p)$. Choose distinct $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ and distinct $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ from $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$. Then $\det(\zeta^{a_ib_j})\ne0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $D(x)=\det(x^{a_ib_j})\in\Z[x]$ and $m=n(n-1)/2$. Write $V(a)=\prod_{i<j}(a_j-a_i)$, and similarly for $b$.
Expand each exponential through order $K\ge m$. Cauchy--Binet applied to the column arrays $(a_i^k)$ and $(b_j^k)$ gives the determinant as a sum over distinct indices $k_1<\cdots<k_n$, with the factor $t^{k_1+\cdots+k_n}/\prod_jk_j!$, up to $O(t^{K+1})$. The smallest index sum is $m$, attained only at $0,1,\ldots,n-1$. Its two determinants are Vandermonde determinants, so
\[
D(e^t)=ct^m+O(t^{m+1}),\qquad
c\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}k!=V(a)V(b)\ne0.
\]
The entrywise exponential remainders give the same $O(t^{K+1})$ determinant remainder because the determinant is a finite polynomial in the entries.
Factor out the vanishing order at $x=1$. Since $e^t-1=t+O(t^2)$, the last expansion shows
$D(x)=(x-1)^mQ(x)$ with $Q(1)=c$. Monic division leaves $Q\in\Z[x]$, so $c$ is an integer. No difference in the Vandermonde products is divisible by $p$, and none of $0!,\ldots,(n-1)!$ is either. Thus $p\nmid c$.
If $D(\zeta)=0$, the minimal polynomial $\Phi_p(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}$ divides $D$ over $\Q$ (Volume~3, Proposition~10.9). It is coprime to $(x-1)^m$ because $\Phi_p(1)=p\ne0$, hence divides $Q$. Monic division again gives $Q=\Phi_pR$ with $R\in\Z[x]$. Evaluating at one yields $c=pR(1)$, the contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Tao, 2005]\label{thm:tao}
For prime $p$ and nonzero $f:\Z/p\Z\to\C$,
$|\operatorname{supp}f|+|\operatorname{supp}\hat f|\ge p+1$.
Conversely, any nonempty sets $A,B\subseteq\Z/p\Z$ with $|A|+|B|=p+1$ occur as exactly those two supports for some function.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Identify frequencies with $b\in\Z/p\Z$, so $p\hat f(b)=\sum_xf(x)\zeta^{-bx}$. Let $A$ have size $k$. If the transform support had at most $p-k$ points, choose $k$ frequencies where it vanishes. Their equations for the $k$ values of $f$ on $A$ form a square Fourier submatrix, invertible by Chebotarev after representing $-b$ by residues. All those values would be zero, a contradiction.
For the converse, functions supported inside $A$ form a space of dimension $|A|$. The requirement to vanish at the $p-|B|=|A|-1$ frequencies outside $B$ leaves a nonzero solution by rank--nullity. Its two supports are contained in $A,B$. The inequality just proved forces their size sum to be $p+1$, so both inclusions are equalities.
\end{proof}

For support size $k$, the prime-order bound forces at least $p+1-k$ frequency values to be nonzero. If two functions each have at most $k$ nonzero entries and agree on any $2k$ distinct Fourier samples, their difference has support at most $2k$ and transform support at most $p-2k$. The inequality forces that difference to vanish. This assertion assumes $2k\le p$, so that such a sample set exists; knowing the entire transform already gives uniqueness otherwise. Composite lengths admit subgroup obstructions, explored in Exercise~\ref{ex:composite}.

\section{Quadratic reciprocity from the Chinese remainder theorem}

Volume~2, Theorem~6.5 proved reciprocity by a lattice-point count, and Exercise~6.7 used Gauss sums. We now combine group structure with the exact sign evaluation from Volume~5, Theorem~8.14. Write
\[
G(a;n)=\sum_{x\bmod n}e(ax^2/n),\qquad
G(1;n)=\varepsilon_n\sqrt n\quad(n\text{ odd}),
\]
where $\varepsilon_n=1$ for $n\equiv1\pmod4$ and $i$ for $n\equiv3\pmod4$. The latter identity was proved analytically by Poisson summation. In finite Fourier language, $G(a;n)/n$ is a coefficient of the square-root-counting function $y\mapsto\#\{x:x^2=y\}$, at frequency $-a$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gaussa}
For an odd prime $p$ and $p\nmid a$, $G(a;p)=\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)G(1;p)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The number of square roots of $y$ is $1+(\frac yp)$, including $y=0$. The sum of $e(ay/p)$ over all residues is zero, so
\[
G(a;p)=\sum_y\Bigl(\frac yp\Bigr)e(ay/p)
=\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr)\sum_y\Bigl(\frac{ay}p\Bigr)e(ay/p).
\]
Here multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol and $(\frac ap)^2=1$ give the second equality. Reindex by the bijection $y\mapsto ay$ to identify the remaining sum as $G(1;p)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:gaussmult}
For coprime positive integers $m,n$, $G(a;mn)=G(an;m)G(am;n)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The map $(y\bmod n,z\bmod m)\mapsto my+nz\bmod mn$ is bijective. Its kernel is zero by reducing modulo each factor, and its source and target have the same size. Expanding gives
$ a(my+nz)^2/(mn)=amy^2/n+anz^2/m+2ayz$.
The last term has exponential one, so summing over the two independent residues gives the product.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[quadratic reciprocity]\label{thm:qr}
For distinct odd primes $p,q$,
$\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)\bigl(\frac qp\bigr)=(-1)^{((p-1)/2)((q-1)/2)}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The two lemmas give
$G(1;pq)=(\frac qp)(\frac pq)G(1;p)G(1;q)$.
Substitute the known evaluations and cancel $\sqrt{pq}$. If either prime is one modulo four, $\varepsilon_{pq}=\varepsilon_p\varepsilon_q$, giving product one. If both are three modulo four, the left phase is one and the product of the two right phases is $i^2=-1$, giving symbol product minus one. These are precisely the two cases of the asserted exponent.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Counting square roots determines how $G(a;p)$ depends on $a$. The Chinese remainder theorem factors a sum at a product modulus. Analysis fixes its phase. Removing any one of those three ingredients would leave a gap in this reciprocity proof. Hecke's theta-function method and Weil's later representation-theoretic account extend these connections to number fields; Volume~13 develops their local and adelic setting.
\end{beauty}

\section{Arithmetic progressions and the game of SET}

A SET card has four attributes, each with three possible values. Label each value by $0,1,2$ to identify the $81$ cards with $\mathbb F_3^4$. Three attribute values are all equal or all different exactly when they sum to zero: if the first two differ, their negative sum is the remaining value, and if they agree, it equals them in characteristic three. Thus a set in the game is three distinct vectors summing to zero, equivalently a line $x,x+d,x+2d$ with $d\ne0$.

A subset of $\mathbb F_3^n$ with no such triple is a \emph{cap set}. The game grew from Marsha Falco's attribute cards in the 1970s and was marketed in 1991. The mathematical maximum in dimension four is $20$, proved by Pellegrino in 1971, so any $21$ distinct cards contain a set. In dimension two the maximum is four, as pictured below and proved in Exercise~\ref{ex:v8c12d}.

\caption{Left: the plane $\mathbb F_3^2$ and four of its twelve lines, which are its three-term progressions; lines wrap around, like
$\{(0,0),(1,2),(2,1)\}$. Right: a cap of four points, the largest possible in dimension $2$. Every fifth point would complete a line.}
\label{fig:cap}
\end{figure}

The binary cube $\{0,1\}^n$ is a cap: in each coordinate a zero-sum triple of binary values must be constant, so the three vectors must coincide. This gives $2^n$ points in every dimension. Products of cap sets are also caps by applying the same equal-triple criterion to each factor. An upper bound needs a way to detect progressions in a dense set.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:capcount}
For a subset $A$ of a finite abelian group of order $N$, the number of ordered triples in $A^3$ with $x+y+z=0$ is
$T(A)=N^2\sum_{\chi\in\widehat G}\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)^3$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Orthogonality gives $\mathbf1_{w=0}=N^{-1}\sum_\chi\chi(w)$. Insert $w=x+y+z$ and sum over $A^3$. The result is $N^{-1}\sum_\chi(\sum_{x\in A}\chi(x))^3$. Each inner sum is $N\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\bar\chi)$; conjugation permutes the dual, proving the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Meshulam, 1995]\label{thm:meshulam}
For $n\ge1$, every cap set $A\subset\mathbb F_3^n$ satisfies $|A|\le32\cdot3^n/n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The empty set satisfies the bound, so let $\alpha=|A|/3^n>0$. We show that density forces a denser cap in a lower-dimensional affine hyperplane, until the available dimension runs out.

\emph{Find a coefficient.} In a cap, a zero-sum triple has all three entries equal, so $T(A)=|A|$. With $N=3^n$, separating the trivial character gives
\[
\sum_{\chi\ne1}\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)^3=\alpha/N-\alpha^3.
\]
If $\alpha^2\ge2/N$, the right side has modulus at least $\alpha^3/2$. Triangle inequality and Parseval bound the left side by
$\max_{\chi\ne1}|\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)|\sum_\chi|\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)|^2
=\alpha\max_{\chi\ne1}|\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)|$.
Hence some nontrivial coefficient has modulus at least $\alpha^2/2$.

\emph{Raise the density.} Write its character as $\omega^{\xi\cdot x}$ with $\xi\ne0$ and $\omega=e(1/3)$. Let $\alpha_j$ be the density of $A$ in the hyperplane $\xi\cdot x=j$. These three equal-sized hyperplanes give
$\alpha=(\alpha_0+\alpha_1+\alpha_2)/3$ and
$\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)=\tfrac13\sum_j(\alpha_j-\alpha)\omega^{-j}$.
The three real differences sum to zero. Their absolute sum is twice the sum of the positive ones and is at most four times their largest value, since at most two are positive. Therefore some hyperplane has
$\alpha_j\ge\alpha+3\alpha^2/8\ge\alpha+\alpha^2/4$.
Translate it to its parallel linear subspace. Translation preserves zero-sum triples here because $3x=0$, and a linear identification gives a cap in dimension one less.

\emph{Count increments.} Repeat, with densities $\alpha_k$, while $\alpha_k^2\ge2\cdot3^{-(n-k)}$. All are at most one. At each increment,
\[
\frac1{\alpha_k}-\frac1{\alpha_{k+1}}
\ge\frac1{\alpha_k}-\frac1{\alpha_k+\alpha_k^2/4}
=\frac1{4+\alpha_k}\ge\frac15.
\]
Thus there are fewer than $5/\alpha$ increments. The condition fails by dimension zero at latest. At the stopping index $K$ it gives
$n-K<\log_3(2/\alpha_K^2)\le\log_3(2/\alpha^2)$, whence
$n<5/\alpha+\log_3(2/\alpha^2)$.
If $\alpha>32/n$, then $n>32$, $5/\alpha<5n/32$, and
$\log_3(2/\alpha^2)<\log_3(n^2/512)<2\log_2n\le n/2$.
The last elementary inequality holds for $n\ge16$: equality holds at $16$, and $n/2-2\log_2n$ has positive derivative there and thereafter. The resulting bound $n<21n/32$ is impossible. This proves the asserted constant.
\end{proof}

Meshulam's theorem has sharper constants than the one proved here. Density increments also treat three-term progressions in $\mathbb F_q^n$ for fixed odd $q$, with constants depending on $q$ and the progression equation $x+z=2y$ in place of the characteristic-three sum equation.
The 2016 polynomial method of Croot--Lev--Pach and Ellenberg--Gijswijt gives an exponential upper bound $2.756^n$ for caps. Exercise~\ref{ex:capset} proves its slice argument, checks the numerical-looking constant by an exact rational inequality, and removes a prefactor using products. If $M_n$ is the largest cap size, its capacity is $\sup_{n\ge1}M_n^{1/n}$, measuring exponential growth through Cartesian powers. Romera-Paredes and collaborators' FunSearch paper, online in 2023 and published in \emph{Nature} in 2024, gives a cap-set capacity lower bound $2.2202$ through explicit constructions. This quoted construction bound is not a matching upper bound or a claim about the current record. Over the integers, Kelley and Meka's 2023 progression bound improves on $N/(\log N)^A$ for every fixed $A$ at sufficiently large $N$. The finite-field argument above does not prove that integer theorem.

\section{Testing linearity}\label{sec:blr}

Suppose an oracle supplies $f:\mathbb F_2^n\to\mathbb F_2$. Query $x,y,x+y$ for uniform independent $x,y$ and check $f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)$. A linear map always passes. Blum, Luby and Rubinfeld's test asks whether frequent success forces closeness to a linear map.
Put $F=(-1)^f$. The linear maps become the characters $\chi_S(x)=(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}x_i}$. Agreement and disagreement give
\[
\operatorname{dist}(F,\chi_S)=\frac{1-\hat F(S)}2,
\qquad \sum_S\hat F(S)^2=1,
\]
where distance is the fraction of inputs at which the two values differ. The coefficients are real.

\begin{theorem}[Blum, Luby and Rubinfeld, 1990]\label{thm:blr}
For any $F:\mathbb F_2^n\to\{\pm1\}$, its acceptance probability is
\[
\Pr(F(x)F(y)=F(x+y))=\frac12+\frac12\sum_S\hat F(S)^3.
\]
If it is at least $1-\varepsilon$, then some character is within distance $\varepsilon$ of $F$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The acceptance indicator is $(1+F(x)F(y)F(x+y))/2$. Averaging the product gives $\langle F,F*F\rangle=\sum_S\hat F(S)^3$, by the convolution and Plancherel identities and $-y=y$. This proves the formula. Since
$\sum_S\hat F(S)^3\le(\max_S\hat F(S))\sum_S\hat F(S)^2=\max_S\hat F(S)$,
acceptance at least $1-\varepsilon$ gives a coefficient at least $1-2\varepsilon$. The distance formula gives the conclusion.
\end{proof}

Bellare, Coppersmith, H\aa stad, Kiwi and Sudan gave this Fourier analysis of the test after the original combinatorial proof. Related local tests enter the PCP theorem. Its setting is a decision problem in NP, where yes instances have polynomial-length certificates checkable in polynomial time. The theorem supplies polynomial-size encoded certificates whose verifier uses logarithmically many random bits and constantly many proof queries: a yes instance has a proof always accepted, while on a no instance every proposed proof is rejected with a fixed positive probability. This is a statement about that computational model, not about recognising every invalid mathematical proof from three symbols.
H\aa stad's later three-query results imply hardness of approximating the maximum satisfiable fraction of equations $x_i+x_j+x_k=c$ over $\mathbb F_2$ beyond the random-assignment factor $1/2$, by any fixed positive amount. These complexity results are quoted applications; the three-query Fourier calculation above proves only its stated linearity guarantee.

\begin{beauty}
For cap sets, expanding a pattern count produces a large Fourier coefficient and then a denser hyperplane. For linearity testing, the same kind of expansion produces a large coefficient and then a nearby character. The coefficient identifies a concrete piece of structure in each case. Parseval supplies the common estimate that forces one to be large.
\end{beauty}

\section{Averages without counting measure}

The Fourier minor turned sparse values into a support inequality, while subgroup averages selected annihilating frequencies. All these proofs used finite sums, and their normalisations were visible at every step. The Gauss-sum phase was the one analytic input in the reciprocity proof.

An infinite locally compact group has no finite uniform counting average. Chapter~\ref{ch:haar} replaces it by translation-invariant Haar measure and studies the corresponding characters. The $p$-adic field will give a self-dual example needed later for Tate's thesis in Volume~13.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:macwilliams}
\emph{MacWilliams identity.} For a linear code $C\le\mathbb F_2^n$, define $C^\perp=\{u:u\cdot c=0\text{ for all }c\in C\}$, weight $w(v)=|\{i:v_i=1\}|$ and $W_C(X,Y)=\sum_{c\in C}X^{n-w(c)}Y^{w(c)}$. Prove
$W_{C^\perp}(X,Y)=|C|^{-1}W_C(X+Y,X-Y)$.
Use the Hamming code's enumerator $X^7+7X^4Y^3+7X^3Y^4+Y^7$ to find the weight distribution of its dual.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:dsequality}
\emph{Support-product equality.} Prove that a nonzero function attains equality in Donoho--Stark exactly when it has form $c\psi\mathbf1_{a+H}$ for some nonzero scalar, character, point and subgroup.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:composite}
For $n=uv$ with $u,v\ge2$, construct a nonzero function on $\Z/n\Z$ whose two support sizes sum to $u+v$. Show this violates the prime-order sum bound with $p$ replaced by $n$. Find a singular $2\times2$ minor in $(i^{jk})_{0\le j,k\le3}$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:qrsums}
\emph{Two nonzero squares.} For odd prime $p$, let $Q$ be its nonzero quadratic residues and $g=G(1;p)$, $p^*=(\frac{-1}p)p$. Using the cyclic frequency identification, prove
$\widehat{\mathbf1_Q}(b)=((\frac{-b}p)g-1)/(2p)$ for $b\ne0$.
For $a\ne0$, prove that the number $N(a)$ of ordered pairs in $Q^2$ with sum $a$ is
\[
N(a)=\frac{p-2-(\frac{-1}p)}4-\frac12\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr).
\]
Deduce that every nonzero residue is a sum of two nonzero squares exactly for primes $p\ge7$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:circlefp}
Use Gauss sums to prove that $x^2+y^2=1$ over $\mathbb F_p$, with $p$ odd, has $p-(\frac{-1}p)$ solutions. When $p\equiv3\pmod4$, recover the count by lines through $(-1,0)$ and give the rational parametrisation.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:majority}
For majority vote on three signs, viewed as a function on $\mathbb F_2^3$, find every nonzero Fourier coefficient. Compute its BLR acceptance probability and distance to its nearest character. Compare the exact distance with the theorem's guarantee.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:capset}
\emph{Polynomial cap bound.} Let $A\subset\mathbb F_3^n$ be a cap. Let $M_d$ count monomials with each exponent at most two and total degree at most $d$.
(a) Show that $F(x,y,z)=\prod_{i=1}^n(1-(x_i+y_i+z_i)^2)$ indicates $x+y+z=0$, and restricts on $A^3$ to $\mathbf1_{x=y=z}$.
(b) Group its monomials to express it using at most $3M_{2n/3}$ terms of forms $u(x)v(y,z)$, $u(y)v(x,z)$ or $u(z)v(x,y)$.
(c) The diagonal slice lemma says a function with a nonzero diagonal and zero off the diagonal needs at least $|A|$ such terms; deduce $|A|\le3M_{2n/3}$. A proof of the lemma is included in the solution.
(d) Prove $M_{2n/3}\le(1+t+t^2)^n/t^{2n/3}$ for $0<t\le1$, and use the exact choice $t=3/5$ to get $|A|\le3(2.756)^n$. Finally use Cartesian powers to remove the factor three.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c12a}
In $G=\Z/12\Z$, let $H$ be generated by $3$ and $f=\mathbf1_{1+H}$. Find $H^\perp$, every Fourier coefficient of $f$, and $f*f$ with the chapter's normalised convolution. Check equality in the support-product bound.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c12b}
Let $p=5$ and $\zeta=e(1/5)$. Find, up to a scalar, the nonzero function supported on $\{0,1,2\}$ whose transform vanishes at frequencies $0,1$. Identify its exact frequency support and verify that both supports have size three. Prove uniqueness up to a scalar.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c12c}
On $\mathbb F_2^n$ with $n\ge1$ and $N=2^n$, set $F(0)=-1$ and $F(x)=1$ elsewhere. Compute all Fourier coefficients, its nearest-character distance and its BLR acceptance probability. Verify the probability by directly counting rejected pairs, and find the limiting ratio of rejection probability to distance as $n\to\infty$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c12d}
Prove that $\{0,1\}^2$ is a four-point cap in $\mathbb F_3^2$ and that no five-point cap exists. You may count the plane's twelve affine lines. For this four-point cap, compute the three densities on vertical lines and the modulus of its Fourier coefficient at $\chi(x_1,x_2)=e(x_1/3)$. Identify the density increment.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:macwilliams}
\idea Product weights have product transforms, and subgroup averaging switches the code with its annihilator.
\writeup Identify $u\in\mathbb F_2^n$ with $\chi_u(v)=(-1)^{u\cdot v}$. This is an injective character map, hence bijective by size, and carries orthogonal codes to annihilators. For the coordinate product $f$, direct multiplication gives
\[
\hat f(\chi_u)=2^{-n}(X+Y)^{n-w(u)}(X-Y)^{w(u)}.
\]
Poisson summation over $C^\perp$ gives
$|C^\perp|^{-1}W_{C^\perp}(X,Y)=2^{-n}W_C(X+Y,X-Y)$.
Since $|C||C^\perp|=2^n$, this is the required identity. It holds for all complex $X,Y$, hence as a polynomial identity.
Substituting the Hamming enumerator gives one sixteenth of
$(X+Y)^7+7(X+Y)^4(X-Y)^3+7(X+Y)^3(X-Y)^4+(X-Y)^7$.
For a coefficient check, the paired middle terms equal $14X(X^2-Y^2)^3$, while the first and last sum to $2(X^7+21X^5Y^2+35X^3Y^4+7XY^6)$. Adding yields $16X^7+112X^3Y^4$. Thus the dual has enumerator $X^7+7X^3Y^4$, one zero word and seven weight-four words. Distances between distinct nonzero words are also four, because their sum is another nonzero word, matching Volume~3, Example~B.15.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:dsequality}
\idea Equality in the norm estimates forces phase alignment, which identifies a subgroup rather than merely a set of frequencies.
\writeup The proposed functions attain equality by the subgroup-indicator calculation. Conversely translate a maximum point of an extremiser to zero. Equality throughout the proof forces equal modulus on its spatial support $A$, equal modulus on its frequency support $B$, and common argument of the coefficients in $B$. Rescale so that $\hat f=\mathbf1_B$. Then $f=\sum_{\chi\in B}\chi$ and $f(0)=|B|=\|f\|_\infty$.
Choose $\chi_0\in B$ and let $L$ be the subgroup of $\widehat G$ generated by $B\bar\chi_0$. The sum of unit complex numbers has modulus $|B|$ exactly when all its terms agree. Thus $|f(x)|=|B|$ exactly on $L^\perp$, interpreted in the double dual. Since the spatial modulus is constant at its maximum on $A$ and zero elsewhere, $A=L^\perp$. Equality of support product gives $|B|=|L|$, so the subset $B\bar\chi_0$ fills $L$ and $B=\chi_0L$. Character averaging gives
$f(x)=\chi_0(x)\sum_{\eta\in L}\eta(x)=|L|\chi_0(x)\mathbf1_{L^\perp}(x)$.
Undoing the translation and scalar yields precisely $c\psi\mathbf1_{a+H}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:composite}
\idea A proper subgroup gives small support on both sides of the transform.
\writeup Take $f=\mathbf1_H$ with $H=u\Z/n\Z$. The support size is $v$ and its annihilator support has size $u$. Their sum is smaller than $uv+1=n+1$, because $(u-1)(v-1)>0$. For $n=4$, choose rows and columns $0,2$ in the Fourier matrix. The minor is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&i^4\end{pmatrix}$, the all-ones matrix, with determinant zero. This exhibits both obstructions without using a numerical search.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:qrsums}
\idea Fourier multiplication computes the exact convolution count once the Gauss coefficients are known.
\writeup Every nonzero square has two square roots, so
$2p\widehat{\mathbf1_Q}(b)=G(-b;p)-1=(\frac{-b}p)g-1$ for $b\ne0$.
At zero the coefficient is $(p-1)/(2p)$. Convolution and inversion give
$N(a)=p\sum_b\widehat{\mathbf1_Q}(b)^2e(ab/p)$.
For $b\ne0$ the squared coefficient numerator is $p^*+1-2(\frac{-b}p)g$. The exponential sum over nonzero $b$ is $-1$, while
$\sum_{b\ne0}(\frac{-b}p)e(ab/p)=(\frac{-1}p)(\frac ap)g$.
Using $(\frac{-1}p)g^2=p$, substitution yields
\[
N(a)=\frac{(p-1)^2-p^*-1}{4p}-\frac12\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr)
=\frac{p-2-(\frac{-1}p)}4-\frac12\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr).
\]
For $p>5$ the right side is at least $(p-5)/4>0$. At $p=3$, the only nonzero square is one, so one itself is not a sum of two such squares. At $p=5$, the formula gives $N(1)=0$. This proves both directions of the stated prime criterion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:circlefp}
\idea Character orthogonality imposes the equation, and squaring the Gauss sum removes its phase ambiguity.
\writeup The solution count is
\[
\frac1p\sum_r e(-r/p)G(r;p)^2
=p+\frac{g^2}{p}\sum_{r\ne0}e(-r/p)
=p-\Bigl(\frac{-1}p\Bigr).
\]
The first term is the $r=0$ contribution, and for the others use $G(r;p)^2=g^2$ and $g^2=(\frac{-1}p)p$.
A line through $(-1,0)$ of slope $t$ has second intersection
$((1-t^2)/(1+t^2),,2t/(1+t^2))$ whenever its denominator is nonzero. If $p\equiv3\pmod4$, the denominator never vanishes. These $p$ slopes give distinct points, each different from $(-1,0)$; every other circle point has such a slope, since $x=-1$ forces $y=0$. The vertical line is tangent and supplies no further point. The total is therefore $p+1$, agreeing with the formula. When $-1$ is a square, the two exceptional slopes are absent, giving $p-2+1=p-1$ affine points instead.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:majority}
\idea A polynomial identity on sign inputs displays the Fourier coefficients directly.
\writeup For three signs, $F=(x_1+x_2+x_3-x_1x_2x_3)/2$: it gives one when all votes are positive or just one is negative, and oddness handles the other cases. Thus its singleton coefficients are $1/2$, its triple coefficient is $-1/2$, and all others vanish. The BLR formula gives acceptance $1/2+(3/8-1/8)/2=5/8$. The largest coefficient is $1/2$, so a singleton character is nearest, at distance $1/4$. Acceptance corresponds to rejection $3/8$, for which the theorem only promises distance at most $3/8$. The exact example therefore has a smaller distance than that general guarantee.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:capset}
\idea Group low-degree monomials by one variable block, then compare their matrix ranks after contracting the other blocks.
\writeup Over $\mathbb F_3$, $1-w^2$ is one at zero and zero at both nonzero values. Hence $F$ is the indicated sum-zero function, diagonal on $A^3$ because $A$ is a cap. Every expanded monomial has total degree at most $2n$ and each coordinate exponent at most two. At least one of its three variable blocks has degree at most $2n/3$. Assign each monomial to one eligible block, using a fixed order to break ties. Grouping by that block's monomial gives at most $M_{2n/3}$ slices of each type, hence at most $3M_{2n/3}$ in total.
Here is the diagonal lemma over the field being used. Suppose a diagonal function on a set of size $m$ has a decomposition with $r_1$ slices in $x$, $r_2$ in $y$ and $r_3$ in $z$. If $r_1\ge m$, the desired bound is immediate. Otherwise let $U$ be the vectors $h$ annihilating the $r_1$ first-block coefficient functions under $\sum_xh(x)u_i(x)$. Its dimension $d$ is at least $m-r_1$. A vector in $U$ can be chosen with at least $d$ nonzero coordinates: choose $d$ independent coordinate rows of a basis matrix for $U$ and prescribe all those coordinates to be one.
Contract the diagonal function in $x$ against this $h$. The resulting diagonal matrix in $y,z$ has at least $d$ nonzero entries, because the original diagonal entries were all nonzero. Its rank is therefore at least $m-r_1$. The $x$-slices vanish. Each remaining slice becomes an outer-product matrix of rank at most one, so the rank is at most $r_2+r_3$. Thus $m\le r_1+r_2+r_3$, proving the lemma without an extra assumption. Apply it to get $|A|\le3M_{2n/3}$.
For $0<t\le1$, each exponent vector counted by $M_d$ contributes at least one to $t^{-d}\sum_{e\in\{0,1,2\}^n}t^{\sum e_i}$. The sum factors, giving $M_d\le t^{-d}(1+t+t^2)^n$. At $t=3/5$, the base's cube is
\[
\left(\frac{1+t+t^2}{t^{2/3}}\right)^3
=\frac{117649}{5625}
<\frac{327082769}{15625000}=\left(\frac{689}{250}\right)^3.
\]
Cross multiplication makes the positive difference $1{,}574{,}950{,}625$, proving this strict inequality exactly. Since $689/250=2.756$, the claimed prefactored bound follows.
Finally $A^k$ is a cap in dimension $nk$. Apply the bound there to get $|A|^k\le3(2.756)^{nk}$, take $k$th roots, and let $k\to\infty$. This removes the factor three and proves $|A|\le(2.756)^n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c12a}
\idea Character averaging gives the Fourier support, while coset addition gives the convolution support.
\writeup A frequency $b$ annihilates the generator $3$ exactly when $3b/12$ is an integer. Thus $H^\perp=\{0,4,8\}$. Translation of the subgroup indicator gives
\[
\hat f(b)=\frac13e(-b/12)\mathbf1_{\{0,4,8\}}(b).
\]
The spatial support has size four and the frequency support three, giving equality $4\cdot3=12$. For each $x\in2+H$, every one of the four elements of $1+H$ has its partner $x-y$ in that coset, while for other $x$ none does. The normalised convolution is therefore $(f*f)(x)=\tfrac13\mathbf1_{2+H}(x)$. Its transform is the square of the displayed coefficients, also checking the normalisation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c12b}
\idea Place two prescribed roots in the polynomial whose coefficients are the three spatial values.
\writeup Set
$P(t)=(1-t)(1-\zeta t)=1-(1+\zeta)t+\zeta t^2$,
and use these coefficients for $f(0),f(1),f(2)$, with the other values zero. All three coefficients are nonzero. Since $5\hat f(b)=P(\zeta^{-b})$, the zeros are exactly $b=0,1$: a nonzero degree-two polynomial has only its two distinct roots. Thus its frequency support is $\{2,3,4\}$ and the two sizes sum to six. Every degree-at-most-two polynomial satisfying those two root conditions is a scalar multiple of $P$, by polynomial division or factoring its roots. This proves uniqueness up to a scalar and gives the small-prime example explicitly.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c12c}
\idea A single altered value spreads the same small Fourier contribution across every character.
\writeup The point indicator has every Fourier coefficient $1/N$. Hence the trivial coefficient of $F$ is $1-2/N$, and every other coefficient is $-2/N$. The largest is the trivial one, so the nearest character is the constant one and its distance is $1/N$.
The cube sum is $(1-2/N)^3+(N-1)(-2/N)^3=1-6/N+4/N^2$. Thus acceptance is $1-3/N+2/N^2$.
Directly, rejection occurs precisely in the cases $x=0$, $y=0$ or $x=y$. Outside their common pair $(0,0)$ these three sets are disjoint, with $N-1$ pairs each; that common pair is also rejected because it tests three negative values. The rejected count is $3N-2$, agreeing with the Fourier formula. Dividing rejection probability by distance gives $3-2/N\to3$. The theorem's distance upper bound is therefore not an equality for this example.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v8c12d}
\idea Count pair-lines and incidences to exclude five points, then calculate the three hyperplane averages exactly.
\writeup The binary square is a cap by the coordinate argument in the text. In the affine plane there are four directions, because the eight nonzero vectors come in pairs with the same direction; each direction has three parallel lines. Every point lies on four lines, and every two points determine a unique line.
If five points formed a cap, their ten pairs would lie on ten distinct lines, with exactly two chosen points on each. These lines already account for all $5\cdot4=20$ incidences with chosen points. The other two lines would therefore be empty. Two distinct lines have union of size six if parallel and five otherwise, but only four points of the plane lie outside the proposed cap. This contradiction proves the maximum is four.
The three vertical-line densities of the binary square are $2/3,2/3,0$, with mean $4/9$. For $\omega=e(1/3)$, the coefficient at $\chi(x)=\omega^{x_1}$ is
$\widehat{\mathbf1_A}(\chi)=2(1+\omega^2)/9=-2\omega/9$,
of modulus $2/9$. Either occupied line raises density by $2/3-4/9=2/9$, a direct instance of the density-increment step.
\end{solution}
