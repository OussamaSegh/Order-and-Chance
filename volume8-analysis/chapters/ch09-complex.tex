\chapter{Cauchy's Theorem, Growth and Mellin Inversion}\label{ch:complex}

\begin{center}\itshape
A contour remembers what it winds around. A growth bound lets us control an infinite strip. Mellin inversion turns a sum over scales into residues, including the tiny oscillations between the main terms.
\end{center}

Volume~5 proved Cauchy's theorem for star-shaped regions. Volume~7, Definition~6.5 and Proposition~6.6, gave continuous closed curves their winding numbers, and Theorem~6.10 separated the inside and outside of a Jordan curve. We can now combine those results: a holomorphic function integrates to zero around a cycle when the cycle has winding number zero at every point outside its domain. Dixon's proof does this using Liouville's theorem, without cutting the region into smaller pieces.

The complex analysis already available from Volume~5, Chapter~9, includes Cauchy's formula, Laurent series, residues and the maximum principle. Here we extend its reach to general cycles, unbounded strips and entire functions of finite order. Phragm\'en--Lindel\"of will give a bound for $\zeta(1/2+it)$; Hadamard will recover an entire function from its zeros up to a polynomial exponential. The Riemann mapping theorem belongs to Volume~9; its domains must be proper simply connected domains, since the whole plane cannot map conformally onto a disc.

We finish by changing from additive to multiplicative scale. The Mellin transform is Fourier analysis after $x=e^u$. It separates the shape of a function from the scales at which we add its copies, a useful tool in both number theory and the analysis of algorithms.

\section{Cauchy's theorem in its final form}

A \emph{cycle} $\gamma=\gamma_1+\cdots+\gamma_k$ is a formal sum of closed piecewise $C^1$ paths. Integrals and winding numbers add:
\[\int_\gamma f=\sum_j\int_{\gamma_j}f,\qquad
 n(\gamma,a)=\sum_jn(\gamma_j,a),\qquad\gamma^*=\bigcup_j\gamma_j^*.
\]
For example, an annulus has a boundary cycle with its outer circle counterclockwise and its inner circle clockwise. A cycle in an open set $\Omega$ is \emph{homologous to zero in $\Omega$} when $n(\gamma,a)=0$ for every $a\notin\Omega$. The condition tests the holes outside the domain, not every point inside the contour.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy's theorem, homology form]\label{thm:globalcauchy}
Suppose $\Omega$ is open, $f$ is holomorphic on $\Omega$, and $\gamma$ is a cycle in $\Omega$ homologous to zero there. Then
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] for every $z\in\Omega\setminus\gamma^*$,
\[f(z)n(\gamma,z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w;\]
\item[(b)] the integral $\int_\gamma f$ is zero.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Following John Dixon (1971), remove a divided difference's apparent singularity:
\[g(z,w)=\begin{cases}(f(w)-f(z))/(w-z),&w\ne z,\\f'(z),&w=z.\end{cases}\]
This is continuous on $\Omega\times\Omega$. Near a diagonal point choose a closed disc inside $\Omega$; for $z,w$ in that disc,
\[g(z,w)=\int_0^1 f'(z+t(w-z))\dd t,\]
which proves the required continuity there. With $w$ fixed, the removable-singularity theorem (Volume~5, Theorem~9.20) makes $g$ holomorphic in $z$. Integration along the finitely many parameter intervals of $\gamma$ therefore gives a holomorphic function
\[h(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma g(z,w)\dd w\quad(z\in\Omega),\]
by Volume~5, Theorem~9.15(b).

Let $\Omega_1=\{z\notin\gamma^*:n(\gamma,z)=0\}$. Local constancy of winding number makes this set open (Volume~7, Proposition~6.6(c)). The hypothesis says $\Omega\cup\Omega_1=\C$. On $\Omega_1$ define another holomorphic function,
\[h_1(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w.\]
On the overlap, $h=h_1-f(z)n(\gamma,z)=h_1$. They join to an entire function $H$. For $r=\max_{w\in\gamma^*}|w|$, the region $|z|>r$ lies in $\Omega_1$, and
\[|H(z)|\le\frac{L(\gamma)\max_{\gamma^*}|f|}{2\pi(|z|-r)}.\]
Thus $H$ tends to zero at infinity and is bounded everywhere. Liouville (Volume~5, Theorem~9.16) forces $H=0$. Unpacking $h=0$ gives (a).

For (b), an empty cycle is immediate. Otherwise choose $z\in\Omega\setminus\gamma^*$. Such a point exists because a finite union of rectifiable paths has area zero: divide each path by arc length into $N$ pieces, cover them by discs of radius $L/N$, and let $N$ grow. Apply (a) to $F(w)=(w-z)f(w)$. Since $F(z)=0$, its Cauchy integral is exactly $(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_\gamma f$, proving (b).
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The two formulas for $H$ do different jobs. The divided difference makes it holomorphic across the contour; the Cauchy kernel makes it small far away. Winding number tells us that their domains cover the plane. Liouville then finishes the argument.
\end{beauty}

\begin{corollary}[residue theorem, general form]\label{cor:globalresidue}
Let $\Omega$ be open and $S\subset\Omega$ finite. Suppose $f$ is holomorphic on $\Omega\setminus S$, and $\gamma$ is a cycle in $\Omega\setminus S$ homologous to zero in $\Omega$. Then its integral is
\[\int_\gamma f=2\pi i\sum_{c\in S}n(\gamma,c)\operatorname{Res}_cf.\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
At each $c\in S$, take the negative-power part $P_c(z)=\sum_{k\ge1}a_{-k}(z-c)^{-k}$ of the Laurent series (Volume~5, Theorem~9.22). This series is holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{c\}$ and converges uniformly on $\gamma^*$. Subtracting all the $P_c$ leaves removable singularities, hence a holomorphic function on $\Omega$, whose cycle integral vanishes by Theorem~\ref{thm:globalcauchy}(b). In each principal part, terms with $k\ge2$ have single-valued primitives. Only $a_{-1}(z-c)^{-1}$ contributes, giving $2\pi i\,a_{-1}n(\gamma,c)$.
\end{proof}

Call an open set \emph{homologically simply connected} if every cycle in it is homologous to zero there. Equivalently, no closed path in the set has nonzero winding number about a point outside it.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:hsc}
On a connected homologically simply connected open set $\Omega$, every holomorphic function has a primitive. Every nowhere-zero holomorphic function on $\Omega$ also has a holomorphic logarithm and a holomorphic $n$th root for each $n\ge1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Fix $z_0\in\Omega$. Polygonal paths join it to every other point (Volume~7, Proposition~3.14). By Theorem~\ref{thm:globalcauchy}(b), integrating $f$ along such a path gives a value $F(z)$ independent of the chosen path. Inside a small disc, $F(z+h)-F(z)=\int_{[z,z+h]}f$, so continuity of $f$ gives $F'=f$, as in Volume~5, Theorem~9.9.

For a nowhere-zero $f$, take a primitive $L_0$ of $f'/f$. Differentiation gives $(fe^{-L_0})'=0$. Connectedness makes this a nonzero constant, which is $e^c$ for some $c\in\C$. Therefore $L=c+L_0$ is a holomorphic logarithm of $f$, and $e^{L/n}$ is an $n$th root.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:hsccriterion}
Suppose every point of $\C\setminus\Omega$, with $\Omega$ open, belongs to a connected unbounded subset of that complement. Then $\Omega$ is homologically simply connected. In particular this holds for star-shaped open sets, the slit plane $\C\setminus\left]-\infty,0\right]$, and the interior of any Jordan curve.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Given a closed path $\gamma$ in $\Omega$ and $a\notin\Omega$, choose such a connected unbounded set $E$ through $a$. Winding number is continuous and integer valued off $\gamma^*$, hence constant on $E$. It is zero at points of sufficiently large modulus (Volume~7, Proposition~6.6(c)), so it vanishes at $a$. Additivity extends the conclusion to cycles.

If $\Omega$ is star-shaped about $c$ and $a\notin\Omega$, the ray $\{a+t(a-c):t\ge0\}$ stays outside: a point of that ray inside $\Omega$ would bring the segment through $a$ inside too. The slit plane's complement is already connected and unbounded. For a Jordan interior $U$ with exterior $V$, Volume~7, Theorem~6.10, gives $\C\setminus U=\overline V$. This closure remains connected and unbounded, as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
The annulus $1<|z|<2$ admits no primitive of $1/z$. Could a homologically simply connected open set have a non-empty bounded complement? Choose a circle enclosing that complement. The circle lies in the open set but winds once around every point of the complement, so the answer is no. Connectedness of a bounded complement alone does not remove the obstruction; the exterior of a closed disc is an example.
\end{think}

\section{Growth in a strip: Phragm\'en and Lindel\"of}

In 1889 Phragm\'en, working at \emph{Acta Mathematica}, spotted an error in Poincar\'e's prize memoir on the three-body problem. Correcting it led Poincar\'e to the complicated orbit geometry that helped begin chaos theory. Phragm\'en's 1908 work with Lindel\"of concerns another question about infinity: when does the maximum principle still control an unbounded region?

A boundary bound alone is insufficient. On the strip $|\Re s|\le\pi/2$, the function $\exp(\exp(-is))$ has modulus one on both vertical edges and grows doubly exponentially up the middle (Exercise~\ref{ex:plsharp}). A growth hypothesis excludes this escape. As in Volume~6, write $s=\sigma+it$.

\begin{theorem}[Phragm\'en--Lindel\"of in a half-strip]\label{thm:pl}
Let $a<b$, $t_0\in\R$, and $S=\{s:a\le\sigma\le b,\ t\ge t_0\}$. Suppose $f$ is continuous on $S$, holomorphic in its interior, satisfies $|f(s)|\le Ce^{Ct}$ on $S$ for some $C>0$, and has $|f|\le M$ on its boundary. Then $|f(s)|\le M$ everywhere in $S$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First suppose $M>0$. For $\varepsilon>0$ put $K=\max(a^2,b^2)$ and $G(s)=f(s)e^{\varepsilon s^2}$. Since $\Re s^2=\sigma^2-t^2$, we have
\[|G(s)|\le Ce^{Ct+\varepsilon K-\varepsilon t^2}.
\]
This is at most $Me^{\varepsilon K}$ for all sufficiently large $t$. On the original boundary we also have $|G|\le Me^{\varepsilon K}$. Apply the ordinary maximum principle (Volume~5, Theorem~9.19 and its following remark) to rectangles cut off at any sufficiently high horizontal line. Each point of $S$ lies in one, so
\[|f(\sigma+it)|\le M\exp\bigl(\varepsilon(K-\sigma^2+t^2)\bigr).
\]
Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ at the fixed point. If $M=0$, apply the result with any positive boundary bound $\eta$ and let $\eta\downarrow0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[convexity]\label{cor:convexity}
On $S=\{a\le\sigma\le b,\ t\ge1\}$, let $f$ be continuous, holomorphic inside, and bounded in modulus by $Ce^{Ct}$. Suppose $|f(a+it)|\le At^\alpha$ and $|f(b+it)|\le Bt^\beta$ for $t\ge1$. If $\ell$ is affine with $\ell(a)=\alpha$ and $\ell(b)=\beta$, then $|f(\sigma+it)|\le C't^{\ell(\sigma)}$ on $S$ for some constant $C'$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Extend $\ell$ by $\ell(s)=\alpha+\kappa(s-a)$, where $\kappa=(\beta-\alpha)/(b-a)$. On $S$, the point $-is=t-i\sigma$ lies in the right half-plane, so use its holomorphic logarithm. The multiplier satisfies
\[\left|e^{-\ell(s)\operatorname{Log}(-is)}\right|
 =|s|^{-\ell(\sigma)}e^{\kappa t\arg(-is)},\qquad\arg(-is)=-\arctan(\sigma/t).
\]
Set $c=\max(|a|,|b|)$ and $L=\max(|\alpha|,|\beta|)$. The estimates $|t\arg(-is)|\le c$ and $1\le|s|/t\le1+c$ place the multiplier between $k^{-1}t^{-\ell(\sigma)}$ and $kt^{-\ell(\sigma)}$, where $k=e^{|\kappa|c}(1+c)^L$.

Thus $F(s)=f(s)e^{-\ell(s)\operatorname{Log}(-is)}$ is bounded on both vertical edges and, by continuity, on the bottom edge. A fixed power of $t$ can be absorbed into an exponential, so $F$ satisfies the growth hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:pl}. The resulting bound $|F|\le M'$ gives $|f(s)|\le kM't^{\ell(\sigma)}$.
\end{proof}
The zeta application needs vertical-line estimates. Stirling's formula controls the Gamma factor, and the functional equation transfers bounds across the critical strip.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:verticalsizes}
For any compact interval $I$, the following estimates hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Uniformly for $\sigma\in I$ and $|t|\ge1$, the quantity $|\Gamma(\sigma+it)|$ is bounded above and below by positive constant multiples, depending on $I$, of $|t|^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi|t|/2}$. As $|t|\to\infty$, uniformly for $\sigma\in I$,
\[|\Gamma(\sigma+it)|\sim\sqrt{2\pi}\,|t|^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi|t|/2}.\]
\item[(b)] For $|t|\ge1$, we have $\zeta(\sigma+it)=O(|t|)$ uniformly on $\sigma\ge1/2$. On $\sigma\in I$, $\sigma\le1/2$, we have $\zeta(\sigma+it)=O(|t|^{3/2-\sigma})$ uniformly.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) Conjugation reduces the proof to $t\ge1$. For $t\ge\max(1,\max_I|\sigma|)$, the argument of $s$ stays within $3\pi/4$ in absolute value. Stirling's formula (Volume~6, Theorem~6.1(c)) gives
\[\log|\Gamma(s)|=\Re((s-\tfrac12)\log s)-\sigma+\tfrac12\log(2\pi)+O(t^{-1}).\]
Uniformly on $I$, $\log|s|=\log t+O(t^{-2})$ and $\arg s=\pi/2-\sigma/t+O(t^{-3})$. Substitution cancels the $\sigma$ term and leaves
\[\log|\Gamma(s)|=(\sigma-\tfrac12)\log t-\tfrac\pi2t+\tfrac12\log(2\pi)+O(t^{-1}).\]
Exponentiation proves the asymptotic. On the remaining compact range of $t$, the ratio to the model function is continuous and positive, since Gamma has no zeros or poles there. Its maximum and minimum give the two-sided bounds.

(b) Volume~5, Proposition~10.6 and its proof write $\zeta(s)-(s-1)^{-1}=\sum_n\phi_n(s)$, where $|\phi_n(s)|\le|s|n^{-\sigma-1}$. For $1/2\le\sigma\le2$ this gives $|\zeta(s)|\le1+(2+|t|)\zeta(3/2)$. For $\sigma\ge2$, the Dirichlet series gives $|\zeta(s)|\le\zeta(2)$.

For $\sigma\le1/2$, set $w=1-s$ and apply Volume~5, Corollary~10.9(d):
\[\zeta(s)=2(2\pi)^{-w}\cos(\pi w/2)\Gamma(w)\zeta(w).\]
The first factor is bounded for $\sigma\in I$. The cosine is at most $e^{\pi|t|/2}$ in modulus, while (a) bounds Gamma by a constant times $|t|^{1/2-\sigma}e^{-\pi|t|/2}$. The already proved bound on $\zeta(w)$ supplies one more factor $|t|$, establishing (b).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the convexity bound]\label{thm:convexzeta}
For each $\varepsilon>0$, the estimate
\[\zeta(\tfrac12+it)=O_\varepsilon(|t|^{1/4+\varepsilon})\]
holds for $|t|\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Conjugation lets us take $t\ge1$. Choose $0<\delta<1/2$ and consider $-\delta\le\sigma\le1+\delta$. There is no pole when $t\ge1$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:verticalsizes}(b) supplies polynomial growth throughout this half-strip. On the right edge the Dirichlet series bounds zeta by $\zeta(1+\delta)$. On the left edge the functional equation and Stirling give $|\zeta(-\delta+it)|=O_\delta(t^{1/2+\delta})$.

Corollary~\ref{cor:convexity} interpolates these exponents. The line $\sigma=1/2$ is midway between the edges, so its exponent is $1/4+\delta/2$. Taking $\delta/2\le\varepsilon$ proves the stated estimate for every $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The Lindel\"of hypothesis predicts $O_\varepsilon(|t|^\varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon>0$; the Riemann hypothesis would imply it. Hardy and Littlewood used Weyl's exponential-sum method to reach exponent $1/6$ around 1921. Bourgain's 2017 work reached $13/84$, about $0.155$. These are historical examples of subconvex bounds, quoted without proof. Improving the convexity exponent requires more information about zeta than the boundary-growth argument provides.
\end{remark}

\section{Entire functions of finite order}

A polynomial's roots determine it up to a constant. For entire functions the missing factor can be much larger. Weierstrass in 1876 constructed entire functions with any prescribed discrete zero sequence by correcting each linear factor with an exponential. Hadamard's 1893 theorem shows that finite growth limits the degree of those corrections and of the remaining exponential. Volume~6, Theorem~6.7, treated Riemann's $\xi$; we now prove the general form.

A nonzero entire function has \emph{finite order} if $|f(z)|\le\exp(|z|^\lambda)$ for some $\lambda>0$ and all sufficiently large $|z|$. Its \emph{order} $\rho$ is the infimum of these positive $\lambda$. Nonzero constants and polynomials have order zero. The functions $e^z$, $\sin z$ and $\xi$ have order one, $e^{z^2}$ has order two, and $\exp(e^z)$ has infinite order. With $M(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|$, a formula valid even for small constant functions is
\[\rho=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\max(e,M(r))}{\log r}.\]
For nonconstant entire functions the maximum with $e$ eventually makes no difference.

For an integer $p\ge0$, put
\[E_p(z)=(1-z)\exp\left(z+\frac{z^2}2+\cdots+\frac{z^p}p\right),\qquad E_0(z)=1-z.\]
The exponential cancels the first $p$ terms of $\log(1-z)$. This makes the \emph{elementary factor} approach one to order $p+1$ at the origin.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:products}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $|z|\le1$, then $|1-E_p(z)|\le|z|^{p+1}$.
\item[(b)] Suppose $u_n$ are holomorphic on an open set $\Omega$ and $\sum_n|u_n|$ converges uniformly on each compact subset. The partial products $\prod_{n\le N}(1+u_n)$ then converge uniformly on compact sets to a holomorphic $P$. At any point where no factor is locally identically zero, $P$ vanishes exactly when some factor does, and its zero multiplicity is the sum of their multiplicities. If a factor is identically zero on a component, so is $P$ there.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) Differentiation gives $E_p'(z)=-z^p\exp(z+\cdots+z^p/p)$. The power series of $-E_p'$ has non-negative coefficients. After integration, $1-E_p(z)=z^{p+1}\varphi(z)$, where $\varphi$ has non-negative coefficients and $\varphi(1)=1-E_p(1)=1$. Thus $|\varphi(z)|\le\varphi(|z|)\le1$ on the unit disc.

(b) On a compact set choose $N_0$ with $\sum_{n>N_0}|u_n|\le1/2$. The logarithm series obeys $|\log(1+u)|\le2|u|$ for $|u|\le1/2$. Consequently the sum $\Sigma=\sum_{n>N_0}\log(1+u_n)$ converges uniformly there, and the tail product converges uniformly to $e^\Sigma$, which never vanishes. Locally, therefore,
\[P=\left(\prod_{n\le N_0}(1+u_n)\right)e^\Sigma.\]
This proves convergence, holomorphy by Volume~5, Theorem~9.15(a), and the zero statement. Only the finitely many initial factors can vanish at a given point. Their multiplicities add unless one is locally identically zero, in which case the product itself is identically zero on that component.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:zerocount}
Let $f$ be a nonzero entire function of finite order $\rho$, and list its nonzero zeros $a_n$ with multiplicity. For every $\lambda>\rho$, the sum $\sum_n|a_n|^{-\lambda}$ converges.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Remove the zero at the origin by dividing by a power of $z$; this does not increase the order. Choose $\eta>0$ with $\rho+\eta<\lambda$. The zero-counting consequence of Jensen's formula (Volume~6, Corollary~6.4), applied at radii $r,2r$, gives
\[n(r)\le\frac{\log M(2r)-\log|f(0)|}{\log2}=O(r^{\rho+\eta}).\]
The zeros in $2^k\le|a_n|<2^{k+1}$ therefore contribute at most $O(2^{(k+1)(\rho+\eta)-k\lambda})$. These bounds form a convergent geometric series. There are only finitely many nonzero zeros with modulus below one.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Hadamard factorisation]\label{thm:hadamardgen}
Suppose $f$ is entire of finite order $\rho$, has a zero of order $m\ge0$ at the origin, and has remaining zeros $a_n$, listed with multiplicity. With $p=\lfloor\rho\rfloor$, it has a factorisation
\[f(z)=z^me^{Q(z)}\prod_nE_p(z/a_n),\]
where $Q$ is a polynomial of degree at most $p$ and the product converges uniformly on compact sets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $p+1>\rho$, Lemma~\ref{lem:zerocount} gives $\sum_n|a_n|^{-p-1}<\infty$. Lemma~\ref{lem:products}(a) bounds each large-index error on $|z|\le r$ by $r^{p+1}|a_n|^{-p-1}$. Part (b) therefore constructs an entire product $P$ with exactly the prescribed nonzero zeros and their multiplicities. The quotient $f/(z^mP)$ extends across those zeros and never vanishes. Corollary~\ref{cor:hsc}, applied on $\C$, supplies an entire logarithm $Q$, so $f=z^me^QP$.

Logarithmic differentiation gives, away from the zeros,
\[\frac{f'}f=\frac mz+Q'(z)+\sum_n\frac{z^p}{a_n^p(z-a_n)}.\]
Indeed,
\[\frac1{z-a}+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\frac{z^j}{a^{j+1}}=\frac{z^p}{a^p(z-a)},\]
with the sum empty when $p=0$. Uniform convergence of products and their derivatives on compact sets justifies this identity there (Volume~5, Theorem~9.15(a)).

For $R\ge1$, remove just the nearby linear factors:
\[g_R(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z^m\prod_{|a_n|\le2R}(1-z/a_n)}.\]
This is entire and has no zeros on $|z|\le2R$. Its value $c=g_R(0)\ne0$ is independent of $R$. On $|z|=4R$, each denominator factor has modulus at least one, so $|g_R|\le M(4R)$; the maximum principle extends the bound inside. Take a logarithm $L_R$ on $|z|<2R$. Then $h=L_R-L_R(0)$ satisfies $h(0)=0$ and
\[\Re h\le A_R=\log M(4R)-\log|c|.\]
Borel--Carath\'eodory (Volume~6, Lemma~6.5) bounds $|h|$ on $|z|\le3R/2$ by $6A_R$. Cauchy's derivative estimate on discs of radius $R/2$ now gives, for $|z|\le R$,
\[|L_R^{(p+1)}(z)|\le\frac{6A_R(p+1)!}{(R/2)^{p+1}}\longrightarrow0.\]
The limit follows because $A_R=O(R^{\rho+\eta})$ for any $\eta>0$ with $\rho+\eta<p+1$.

On the other hand, subtracting the nearby logarithmic derivatives in the preceding identity and differentiating $p$ times yields
\[L_R^{(p+1)}(z)=Q^{(p+1)}(z)+(-1)^pp!\sum_{|a_n|>2R}(z-a_n)^{-p-1}.
\]
The nearby correction polynomials have degree below $p$ and disappear. The distant series and its derivatives converge locally uniformly; on $|z|\le R$ its displayed tail is bounded by $2^{p+1}\sum_{|a_n|>2R}|a_n|^{-p-1}$, which tends to zero. Fixing $z$ and letting $R$ grow gives $Q^{(p+1)}(z)=0$. Thus $Q$ has degree at most $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Jensen limits how many zeros a slowly growing entire function can have. That makes its canonical product converge. Borel--Carath\'eodory then controls the real part of the logarithm left over, and Cauchy's estimate forces its high derivatives to vanish. Those are the two growth arguments behind Hadamard's polynomial.
\end{beauty}
\section{The Mellin transform and harmonic sums}\label{sec:mellin}

The multiplicative group $]0,\infty[$ has Haar measure $\dd x/x$, as translation becomes multiplication there (Chapter~\ref{ch:haar}). Its Fourier analysis is Mellin analysis after taking logarithms. Mellin developed the transform systematically in the 1890s. Riemann had already used the same integral in 1859 to connect theta and $\Gamma(s/2)\pi^{-s/2}\zeta(s)$ (Volume~5, Lemma~10.7). Flajolet later used it to study algorithms whose costs repeat at different scales.

For continuous $f:]0,\infty[\to\C$, define
\[\mathcal Mf(s)=\int_0^\infty f(x)x^{s-1}\dd x\]
where the integral is absolutely convergent. If absolute convergence holds for every real part in $]\sigma_1,\sigma_2[$, then the transform is holomorphic on that strip. On a closed smaller strip its integrand is dominated by
\[|f(x)|(x^{\sigma'-1}+x^{\sigma''-1}),\]
so Volume~5, Theorem~9.15(b), applies. In particular, $\mathcal M(e^{-x})(s)=\Gamma(s)$ for $\sigma>0$. For $-1<\sigma<0$, integration by parts has vanishing boundary terms and gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mellin1me}
\mathcal M(1-e^{-x})(s)=-\frac1s\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^s\dd x=-\Gamma(s).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Mellin inversion]\label{thm:mellininv}
Let $f:]0,\infty[\to\C$ be continuous. Suppose a real $c$ satisfies
\[\int_0^\infty|f(x)|x^{c-1}\dd x<\infty,\qquad
\int_\R|\mathcal Mf(c+i\tau)|\dd\tau<\infty.
\]
Then for every $x>0$,
\[f(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\mathcal Mf(s)x^{-s}\dd s.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Set $F(u)=f(e^u)e^{cu}$. The substitution $x=e^u$ makes $F$ continuous and integrable, and gives
\[\hat F(\xi)=\mathcal Mf(c-2\pi i\xi).\]
The second hypothesis makes this Fourier transform integrable too. Fourier inversion (Theorem~\ref{thm:ftinv}) therefore applies at every $u$. Substitute $u=\log x$ and $\tau=-2\pi\xi$ in its formula:
\[f(x)x^c=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R\mathcal Mf(c+i\tau)x^{-i\tau}\dd\tau.
\]
Writing $s=c+i\tau$ proves the stated contour integral.
\end{proof}

A \emph{harmonic sum} adds copies of one function at different scales:
\[G(x)=\sum_k\lambda_kg(\mu_kx),\qquad\mu_k>0.\]
If $\sum_k|\lambda_k|\mu_k^{-\sigma}<\infty$ and $\int_0^\infty|g(x)|x^{\sigma-1}\dd x<\infty$, absolute integrability permits substitution and Fubini, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:harmonicsum}
\mathcal MG(s)=\Lambda(s)\mathcal Mg(s),\qquad
\Lambda(s)=\sum_k\lambda_k\mu_k^{-s},\quad\Re s=\sigma.
\end{equation}
The Dirichlet series records the scales; the other factor records the shape. When meromorphic continuation and decay justify moving the inversion contour, poles yield asymptotic terms. Move left for $x\to0$ and right for $x\to\infty$. A simple pole at $s_0$ contributes a multiple of $x^{-s_0}$, with sign depending on the direction of the shift; repeated poles also produce powers of $\log x$.

\begin{example}[a theta sum]\label{ex:thetamellin}
For $G(x)=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-k^2x}$ and every $N>0$, we have
\[G(x)=\frac12\sqrt{\pi/x}-\frac12+O(x^N)\qquad(x\downarrow0).
\]
To prove it, use $g(x)=e^{-x}$ and $\mu_k=k^2$, obtaining $\mathcal MG(s)=\Gamma(s)\zeta(2s)$ for $\sigma>1/2$. The bounds
\[G(x)\le\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2x}\dd u=\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/x},\qquad
G(x)\le\frac{e^{-x}}{1-e^{-x}}
\]
justify the weighted integrability at $c=1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:verticalsizes} gives integrability of the transform there. Choose an integer $J>N$ and move the contour to $\sigma=-J-1/2$. Gamma's exponential decay dominates the polynomial zeta bound on the horizontal segments, whose integrals tend to zero.

At $s=1/2$ the residue is $\tfrac12\Gamma(1/2)x^{-1/2}=\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/x}$. At zero it is $\zeta(0)=-1/2$ (Volume~5, Corollary~10.9). Gamma's other poles are cancelled by the trivial zeros $\zeta(-2),\zeta(-4),\ldots$. The remaining vertical integral is $O(x^{J+1/2})$, proving the claim.
\end{example}

Theta's transformation (Volume~5, Corollary~8.10) gives the stronger remainder $\sqrt{\pi/x}\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-\pi^2k^2/x}$, which is exponentially small. Mellin inversion found that all further power terms vanish.

Consider a different application: counting distinct visitors without storing every identifier. Flajolet and Martin's 1985 method hashes each item to random bits and remembers the largest leading-zero count. A level $L$ has $\Pr(L\ge k)=2^{-k}$, so the maximum among $n$ independent levels is near $\log_2n$. This idea also underlies HyperLogLog (Flajolet, Fusy, Gandouet and Meunier, 2007), used for compact distinct-item estimates.

To compute the mean precisely, take a Poisson number $N$ of items with mean $x$, independently of their levels. Let $H$ be their maximum, with $H=0$ for no items. For $k\ge1$, summing the Poisson series gives
\[\Pr(H<k)=\sum_{n\ge0}e^{-x}\frac{x^n}{n!}(1-2^{-k})^n=e^{-x2^{-k}}.
\]
The tail-sum formula therefore yields $\mathbb E[H]=\sum_{k\ge1}(1-e^{-x2^{-k}})$.

\begin{theorem}[the maximum of geometric levels]\label{thm:maxgeom}
For every $d>0$, as $x\to\infty$,
\[\mathbb E[H]=\log_2x+\frac\gamma{\ln2}-\frac12+P(\log_2x)+O(x^{-d}),\]
where
\[P(u)=-\frac1{\ln2}\sum_{k\ne0}\Gamma\left(\frac{2\pi ik}{\ln2}\right)e^{-2\pi iku}\]
is continuous, has period one, and satisfies $|P(u)|<1.6\cdot10^{-6}$ everywhere.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $G(x)=\mathbb E[H]$, equations~\eqref{eq:mellin1me} and \eqref{eq:harmonicsum} give
\[\mathcal MG(s)=-\Gamma(s)\frac{2^s}{1-2^s}\qquad(-1<\sigma<0).
\]
The absolute convergence condition follows from $\sum_{k\ge1}2^{k\sigma}<\infty$. The sum defining $G$ converges uniformly on compact $x$ intervals, since $1-e^{-x2^{-k}}\le x2^{-k}$, so $G$ is continuous. On $c=-1/2$ the rational factor is bounded and Gamma decays exponentially. Mellin inversion applies.

Move the contour to $\sigma=d>0$, cutting the rectangles at $T_j=(2j+1)\pi/\ln2$. Along their horizontal edges $2^{\pm iT_j}=-1$, so $|1-2^s|=1+2^\sigma\ge1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:verticalsizes} makes those edges vanish in the limit. On the new vertical edge the rational factor is bounded by $2^d/(2^d-1)$, so the new integral is $O(x^{-d})$.

The poles crossed are $\chi_k=2\pi ik/\ln2$, simple except for the double pole at zero. The right-hand vertical integral minus the left-hand one equals the sum of residues; hence
\[G(x)=-\sum_{k\in\Z}\operatorname{Res}_{s=\chi_k}(\mathcal MG(s)x^{-s})
 +\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(d)}\mathcal MG(s)x^{-s}\dd s.
\]
For $k\ne0$, the residue is $\Gamma(\chi_k)x^{-\chi_k}/\ln2$, giving the displayed periodic series with a minus sign. At zero use
\[\Gamma(s)=s^{-1}-\gamma+O(s),\quad
\frac{2^s}{1-2^s}=-\frac1{s\ln2}-\frac12+O(s).
\]
Here $\Gamma'(1)=-\gamma$ is Volume~5, Exercise~5.7. Multiplying also by $x^{-s}=1-s\ln x+O(s^2)$ gives residue $1/2-\gamma/\ln2-\log_2x$. Its negative gives the three main terms.

For a rigorous bound on the oscillation, reflection (Volume~5, Theorem~5.13) gives $|\Gamma(iy)|^2=\pi/(y\sinh\pi y)$. Put $q=e^{-\pi^2/\ln2}$. For $k\ge1$ this yields
\[\left|\Gamma\left(\frac{2\pi ik}{\ln2}\right)\right|
 =\sqrt{\frac{\ln2}{k}}\frac{q^k}{\sqrt{1-q^{4k}}}.
\]
Thus the series converges uniformly, and
\[|P|\le\frac{2q}{\sqrt{\ln2}(1-q)\sqrt{1-q^4}}<1.6\cdot10^{-6}.
\]
Here the last strict bound can be certified without numerical sampling. The elementary series for arctangent and logarithm give $\pi>314159/100000$ and $69/100<\ln2<173287/250000$. They imply $\pi^2/\ln2>7117/500$; the positive Taylor sum $\sum_{j=0}^{69}(7117/500)^j/j!>50000000/33$ then gives $q<33/50000000$. Substituting this rational bound and $\sqrt{\ln2}>83/100$ in the preceding expression, and squaring positive quantities, proves the claimed inequality. For example, the constants for $\pi$ follow from $\pi=16\arctan(1/5)-4\arctan(1/239)$ with alternating remainders; those for $\ln2$ follow from $2\sum_{j\ge0}((2j+1)3^{2j+1})^{-1}$ with its geometric tail bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The correction repeats when $x$ doubles, because doubling shifts $\log_2x$ by one. Its frequencies are the poles spaced along the imaginary axis in $1/(1-2^s)$. The oscillation is small enough to hide in ordinary plots, but its origin is exact. Similar terms appear in tries, radix algorithms and other processes that repeatedly halve a scale; Flajolet, Gourdon and Dumas give many examples.
\end{beauty}

\section{Contours, growth and scale}

Winding number was enough to extend Cauchy's theorem to general cycles. Growth bounds then extended the maximum principle to half-strips and limited the exponential left over in an entire function's zero product. Mellin inversion brings these tools together: it converts scales to a vertical contour, and justified contour shifts turn poles into asymptotic terms.

The next chapter reverses another limiting process. It asks which boundary information about a power series or Dirichlet series determines the growth of its coefficients. Tauberian hypotheses make that reversal possible. Applied to zeta's nonvanishing on $\Re s=1$, Wiener--Ikehara will give another proof of the prime number theorem.

\section{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:annuluscauchy}
\emph{Recovering Laurent series.} Let $f$ be holomorphic on $r<|z|<R$, and choose $r<r_1<r_2<R$. Denote the counterclockwise circle of radius $\rho$ by $C(\rho)$. (a) Verify that $C(r_2)-C(r_1)$ is homologous to zero in the annulus and deduce, for $r_1<|z|<r_2$,
\[f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{C(r_2)}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w
 -\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{C(r_1)}\frac{f(w)}{w-z}\dd w.
\]
(b) Expand the two kernels to recover a Laurent series valid throughout the annulus.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:plsharp}
\emph{Escape up the middle.} For $f(s)=\exp(\exp(-is))$ on $|\sigma|\le\pi/2$, prove that both vertical edges have modulus one whereas $|f(it)|=\exp(e^t)$. Explain precisely which assumption of Theorem~\ref{thm:pl} this violates, also checking the bottom edge of a half-strip.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:sinprod}
\emph{From zeros to the sine product.} (a) Prove that $\sin\pi z$ has order one. (b) Use Hadamard's theorem to establish
\[\sin\pi z=\pi z\prod_{n\ge1}(1-z^2/n^2).\]
(c) Justify comparing the cubic coefficients and obtain $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:nonintorder}
(a) Prove that a nonzero entire function of finite order with only finitely many zeros has integer order. (b) Apply this to the entire function $\cos\sqrt z=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nz^n/(2n)!$: establish its order $1/2$, then infer infinitely many zeros without locating them.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:lambert}
\emph{A divisor sum at small scale.} Write $d(n)$ for the number of positive divisors of $n$. Prove, as $x\downarrow0$,
\[\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{e^{kx}-1}=\sum_{n\ge1}d(n)e^{-nx}
 =\frac{\log(1/x)+\gamma}{x}+\frac14-\frac{x}{144}+O(x^{3/2}).\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v8c14a}
\emph{A contour with two poles.} Let $0<r_1<r_2$, with neither radius equal to $|a|$ or $|b|$, where $a\ne b$. Evaluate
\[\int_{C(r_2)-C(r_1)}\frac{\dd z}{(z-a)(z-b)}\]
in terms of which poles lie between the circles. Explain why the answer is zero when both poles lie there, although each circle need not have zero integral.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c14b}
\emph{A product of order one half.} Define $F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}z^n/(2n+1)!$. Prove that $F$ is entire of order $1/2$, with zeros $-\pi^2n^2$, $n\ge1$, all simple. Deduce
\[F(z)=\prod_{n\ge1}(1+z/(\pi^2n^2)).\]
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c14c}
\emph{An exact scale equation.} Put $G(x)=\sum_{k\ge1}(1-e^{-x2^{-k}})$. Establish
\[G(2x)-G(x)=1-e^{-x}.\]
Let $R(x)=G(x)-\log_2x-\gamma/\ln2+1/2$. Prove that $R(2x)-R(x)=-e^{-x}$, and explain why Theorem~\ref{thm:maxgeom}'s periodic correction satisfies this relation asymptotically. Does this relation alone determine the periodic function?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v8c14d}
\emph{A Mellin pair from a Laplace integral.} For $f(x)=1/(1+x)$, prove
\[\mathcal Mf(s)=\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\frac\pi{\sin\pi s}\qquad(0<\Re s<1).
\]
Use Mellin inversion to represent $f(x)$ by a vertical integral on any line $0<c<1$. Justify the integrability needed for inversion.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}
\begin{solution}{ex:annuluscauchy}
\idea The cycle cancels winding around the hole, allowing the general Cauchy formula to separate positive and negative powers.
\writeup (a) For $|a|\le r$, the circles both have winding number one, and for $|a|\ge R$ both have winding number zero. Their difference is therefore homologous to zero. Between $r_1$ and $r_2$ its winding number is one, so Theorem~\ref{thm:globalcauchy}(a) gives the asserted formula.

(b) Uniformly on the relevant circles,
\[\frac1{w-z}=\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{z^j}{w^{j+1}}\quad(|w|=r_2),\qquad
-\frac1{w-z}=\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{w^j}{z^{j+1}}\quad(|w|=r_1).
\]
Termwise integration gives the positive coefficients $(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{C(r_2)}f(w)w^{-j-1}\dd w$ and negative coefficients $(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_{C(r_1)}f(w)w^{j-1}\dd w$, $j\ge1$. Volume~5, Lemma~9.21, makes them independent of the admissible radii. Varying those radii covers the whole annulus.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:plsharp}
\idea The second exponential turns a positive real part on the centre line into doubly exponential growth.
\writeup Since $e^{-is}=e^t(\cos\sigma-i\sin\sigma)$, we have $|f(s)|=\exp(e^t\cos\sigma)$. This is one at $\sigma=\pm\pi/2$ and $\exp(e^t)$ at $\sigma=0$. Continuity and holomorphy hold everywhere. The bottom segment $t=t_0$ is bounded by $\exp(e^{t_0})$, so the whole half-strip boundary has a finite bound. The missing hypothesis is $|f(s)|\le Ce^{Ct}$: on the centre line it would require $e^t\le\log C+Ct$ for all large $t$, which is impossible. A stronger strip theorem allows growth $\exp(Ce^{\kappa t})$ when $\kappa<1$ for this width; that sharper result is quoted here only to locate the example at its threshold.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:sinprod}
\idea Pairing opposite zeros cancels the exponential corrections; symmetry then removes the remaining linear exponential.
\writeup (a) The upper bound $|\sin\pi z|\le e^{\pi|z|}$ gives order at most one. Along $z=iy$ with $y\to\infty$, the modulus is $\sinh\pi y\ge e^{\pi y}/4$, giving order at least one.

(b) The simple zeros are exactly the integers. Hadamard gives
\[\sin\pi z=ze^{A+Bz}\prod_{n\ne0}(1-z/n)e^{z/n}.
\]
The locally absolute convergence permits pairing $n$ and $-n$. Each pair becomes $1-z^2/n^2$, yielding an even product $\Pi(z)$. Oddness of sine implies $e^{Bz}=e^{-Bz}$ near zero, and differentiation at zero gives $B=0$. Taking $z\to0$ after division by $z$ gives $e^A=\pi$.

(c) Uniform convergence on a small closed disc gives convergence of derivatives by Cauchy's formula. The quadratic coefficient of $\Pi$ is therefore $-\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}$. Compare the cubic coefficient of $\pi z\Pi(z)$ with $-\pi^3/6$ to obtain $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:nonintorder}
\idea With finitely many zeros, only a polynomial exponential can create positive order.
\writeup (a) Absorb the finite elementary factors from Hadamard into $f=Pe^Q$, where $P$ is a nonzero polynomial and $Q$ a polynomial. If $Q$ is constant, $f$ is a polynomial of order zero. Otherwise let $d=\deg Q\ge1$ and write its leading coefficient as $c$. Choose $\theta$ with $ce^{id\theta}=|c|$. Along this ray, $\Re Q(re^{i\theta})\ge|c|r^d/2$ for large $r$, while $\log|P(re^{i\theta})|=O(\log r)$. Hence the order is at least $d$. The global bound $|Pe^Q|\le\exp(Cr^d)$ for large $r$ gives the reverse inequality, so the order equals the integer $d$.

(b) The power series is entire and equals either branch expression $\cos\sqrt z$. Its modulus is at most $e^{\sqrt{|z|}}$, whereas at $z=-r$ it is $\cosh\sqrt r\ge e^{\sqrt r}/2$. Its order is consequently $1/2$. Part (a) forces infinitely many zeros, without their locations being needed.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:lambert}
\idea Counting factorizations gives the squared zeta factor. The three crossed poles give the three displayed terms.
\writeup Expand $(e^{kx}-1)^{-1}=\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-kmx}$. Non-negative summation groups each $n=km$ with multiplicity $d(n)$. The Dirichlet identity $\sum_nd(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s)^2$ for $\sigma>1$ is Volume~5, Proposition~10.2. Thus the Mellin transform is $\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)^2$.

The bound $d(n)\le n$ gives $G(x)=O(x^{-2})$ near zero and exponential decay at infinity, so inversion applies at $c=3$. Vertical-line estimates from Lemma~\ref{lem:verticalsizes} justify the shift to $-3/2$, as in Example~\ref{ex:thetamellin}; the remaining integral is $O(x^{3/2})$.

At $s=1$, use $\Gamma(s)=1-\gamma(s-1)+O((s-1)^2)$ (Volume~5, Exercise~5.7), $\zeta(s)^2=(s-1)^{-2}+2\gamma(s-1)^{-1}+O(1)$ (Volume~5, Proposition~10.6), and $x^{-s}=x^{-1}(1-(s-1)\log x+O((s-1)^2))$. The residue is $x^{-1}(\log(1/x)+\gamma)$. At zero, Gamma's residue one times $\zeta(0)^2$ gives $1/4$. At $-1$, Gamma's residue $-1$ times $\zeta(-1)^2=1/144$ (Volume~5, Theorem~10.10) gives $-x/144$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c14a}
\idea Winding numbers select the poles in the annular region, and their residues have opposite signs.
\writeup Partial fractions give $(z-a)^{-1}(z-b)^{-1}=((z-a)^{-1}-(z-b)^{-1})/(a-b)$. If $I_c$ equals one when $r_1<|c|<r_2$ and zero otherwise, the integral is $2\pi i(I_a-I_b)/(a-b)$. Thus it is zero if both poles lie between the circles, or if neither does. Cancellation of residues explains the first case; the individual circle integrals depend on which poles each encloses.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c14b}
\idea Squaring the variable halves the growth order of the hyperbolic sine expression.
\writeup The series has infinite radius of convergence and gives $F(z)=\sinh w/w$ when $w^2=z$, with the removable value one at zero. The integral identity $\sinh w/w=\int_0^1\cosh(tw)\dd t$ gives $|F(z)|\le e^{\sqrt{|z|}}$. For positive $r$ it grows at least $e^{\sqrt r}/(4\sqrt r)$ for large $r$. Thus its order is $1/2$. The nonzero zeros of $\sinh w$ are $w=i\pi n$, $n\ne0$, all simple. Since $w\mapsto w^2$ is locally invertible there, the zeros of $F$ are exactly $-\pi^2n^2$, $n\ge1$, each simple. Hadamard has $p=0$ and no zero at the origin, so its remaining exponential is constant. Evaluation at zero gives that constant as one, yielding the product.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c14c}
\idea Reindexing isolates the largest scale; any logarithmic periodic term is invisible to this difference.
\writeup Absolute convergence permits
\[G(2x)=\sum_{j\ge0}(1-e^{-x2^{-j}})=1-e^{-x}+G(x).
\]
Subtracting the change $\log_2(2x)-\log_2x=1$ gives $R(2x)-R(x)=-e^{-x}$. In Theorem~\ref{thm:maxgeom}, the term $P(\log_2x)$ has zero difference because $P$ has period one. Its remaining error can have difference $-e^{-x}$, which is smaller than every negative power of $x$. The relation alone cannot determine $P$: adding any period-one function of $\log_2x$ preserves the difference equation. The Mellin residues supply the missing information.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{ex:v8c14d}
\idea A Laplace integral separates the two Gamma factors, after which reflection gives the trigonometric form.
\writeup For $0<\sigma<1$, the absolute double integral after inserting the suggested formula is
\[\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\int_0^\infty e^{-xt}x^{\sigma-1}\dd x\dd t
 =\Gamma(\sigma)\Gamma(1-\sigma)<\infty.
\]
Fubini and $u=xt$ therefore give $\mathcal Mf(s)=\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)$. Reflection (Volume~5, Theorem~5.13) makes this $\pi/\sin\pi s$. On any line $0<c<1$, the original weighted integral is finite at both endpoints, and
\[|\sin\pi(c+i\tau)|^2=\sin^2\pi c+\sinh^2\pi\tau.
\]
The reciprocal is bounded near zero and decays exponentially at infinity. Mellin inversion is therefore applicable and gives
\[\frac1{1+x}=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\frac\pi{\sin\pi s}x^{-s}\dd s.
\]
\end{solution}
