\chapter{Galois Theory}

\begin{center}\itshape
Quadratic equations can be solved with square roots.\\
Cubic and quartic formulas also use higher roots. What breaks at degree $5$?
\end{center}

There is no shortage of numerical methods for approximating roots of a polynomial. The problem with the quintic is different: can every root be written using the coefficients, the four arithmetic operations, and a finite number of radicals?

The quadratic formula says yes for degree $2$. Formulas for cubics and quartics say yes for degrees $3$ and $4$. But the polynomial
\[
f(X)=X^5-4X+2
\]
will give us a specific obstruction in degree $5$. Eisenstein's criterion proves that $f$ is irreducible over $\Q$. It has exactly three real roots and two non-real roots. Complex conjugation therefore swaps just the two non-real roots. That is one permutation of its roots. We will find enough others to show that its full Galois group is $\mathfrak S_5$.

An automorphism of a field extension fixes the smaller field while permuting the larger field in a way that respects addition and multiplication. In a splitting field, such an automorphism permutes the roots, but not arbitrarily: every polynomial relation over the base field must survive.

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory pairs intermediate fields with subgroups of these automorphisms. Bigger groups have smaller fixed fields, and normal subgroups correspond to Galois intermediate extensions. Once that correspondence is understood, we can turn geometric construction questions into statements about field degrees, and radical formulas into statements about solvable groups.

The proofs here have more stages than the earlier ones. Keep the running example $\Q(\sqrt[4]2,i)$ in mind as you pass between a field, a subgroup and the corresponding fixed field.

In this chapter, unless explicitly stated otherwise, $K\subseteq L\subseteq\C$:
our extensions have \emph{characteristic zero}. The separability
proved in Chapter~8 is essential for the equivalence between a normal
splitting field and a Galois extension. In positive characteristic,
an inseparable splitting field need not have enough automorphisms.
Finite fields were treated separately in Chapter~9; their relevant
polynomials $X^{q^n}-X$ have distinct roots.

\section{The Galois group}

\begin{definition}\label{def:galois}
Let $L/K$ be a finite extension. The \emph{Galois group} $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is the group of field automorphisms of $L$ that fix every element of $K$. For a subgroup $H$ of $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$, the \emph{fixed field} is $L^H=\{x\in L:\sigma(x)=x\text{ for all }\sigma\in H\}$, an intermediate field. The extension is \emph{Galois} if $|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|=[L:K]$. The \emph{Galois group of a nonzero polynomial} $P\in K[X]$ is $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$, where $L$ is the splitting field of $P$ in $\C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:permroots}
Let $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and $P\in K[X]$. If $\alpha\in L$ is a root of $P$, so is $\sigma(\alpha)$. Consequently, if $L=K(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ where $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ are the distinct roots of a nonzero polynomial $P$, then $\sigma\mapsto(\sigma\text{ restricted to the roots})$ is an injective homomorphism $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\to\mathfrak S_n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $P(X)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_jX^j$, with $a_j\in K$.
Since $\sigma$ fixes every coefficient and respects sums and
products,
\[
P(\sigma(\alpha))
=\sum_j a_j\sigma(\alpha)^j
=\sigma\left(\sum_j a_j\alpha^j\right)
=\sigma(P(\alpha))=0.
\]
Thus $\sigma$ takes a root of $P$ to another root in $L$.

An automorphism is injective, so its action on the finite
set of roots is an injection and therefore a permutation.
The assignment from field automorphisms to root permutations
respects composition: $(\sigma\tau)(\alpha)=
\sigma(\tau(\alpha))$.

Finally suppose $L=K(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)$.
An automorphism fixing all the $\alpha_i$ and every
coefficient in $K$ fixes their sums, products and
inverses of non-zero elements. It therefore fixes
every element of their generated field, which is $L$.
Only the identity induces the identity permutation.
Hence the homomorphism into $\mathfrak S_n$ is injective.

This is an embedding, not usually an isomorphism:
a permutation of roots must also preserve every
polynomial relation among them over the base field.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:autbound}
For every finite extension, $|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|\le[L:K]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $K\subseteq L\subseteq\C$ has characteristic
zero and finite degree, the primitive element theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:primitiveelement}) gives
$L=K(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$.

Every automorphism $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$
is determined by $\sigma(\gamma)$: each element of
$L$ is a rational expression in $\gamma$ with
coefficients in $K$, and $\sigma$ fixes those
coefficients.

Let $m_\gamma$ be the minimal polynomial of
$\gamma$ over $K$. Applying $\sigma$ to
$m_\gamma(\gamma)=0$ gives
\[
m_\gamma(\sigma(\gamma))=0.
\]
Thus $\sigma(\gamma)$ must be one of the roots
of $m_\gamma$ lying in $L$. Distinct automorphisms
have distinct images of $\gamma$, while a polynomial
of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots in a field.
Therefore
\[
\boxed{|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|
\le\deg m_\gamma=[L:K]}.
\]
Equality is special: it need not hold for an
arbitrary finite extension.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
The roots of $X^3-2$ are $\sqrt[3]2$, $\omega\sqrt[3]2$, $\omega^2\sqrt[3]2$. Which permutations of them should extend to automorphisms of the splitting field? What about $X^3-3X+1$, whose roots are $2\cos\frac{2\pi}9$, $2\cos\frac{4\pi}9$, $2\cos\frac{8\pi}9$? (Hint: $(2\cos\theta)^2-2=2\cos2\theta$.)
\end{think}

Example~\ref{ex:cuberoot} answered the first question: all six permutations do, and the group is $\mathfrak S_3$. For the second, if $u=2\cos\frac{2\pi}9$, the other roots are $u^2-2$ and $(u^2-2)^2-2$, so an automorphism is determined by the image of $u$, and only the three cyclic permutations can occur: the relation ``$r_2=r_1^2-2$'' must be respected. Exercise~\ref{ex:discr} shows the group is $A_3$.

An automorphism of a splitting field permutes roots, but the set of all such permutations need not have the same size as the degree of an arbitrary field extension. The right equality holds for Galois extensions, and the distinction matters when we compare fields with groups.

\section{Galois extensions}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:galoischar}
For a finite extension $K\subseteq L\subseteq\C$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $L$ is the splitting field over $K$ of some polynomial of $K[X]$;
\item $L/K$ is Galois;
\item $L^{\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)}=K$;
\item ($L/K$ is \emph{normal}) every irreducible polynomial of $K[X]$ with a root in $L$ splits in $L$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
All extensions here lie in $\C$ and have characteristic
zero; in particular every irreducible polynomial is
separable (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}).

\emph{(i) implies (ii).} Suppose $L$ is the splitting
field of $P\in K[X]$. Replace $P$ by the product
$P_0$ of its distinct monic irreducible factors.
Both have the same roots and the same splitting field.
Because the irreducible factors are separable and
distinct factors share no roots, $P_0$ has no
repeated roots. The splitting-field automorphism
theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:splitunique}) now gives
exactly $[L:K]$ automorphisms fixing $K$.
So $L/K$ is Galois.

\emph{(ii) implies (iii).} Put
$G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and $F=L^G$.
Every element of $G$ fixes $F$, so
\[
G\subseteq\operatorname{Gal}(L/F).
\]
The automorphism bound and tower law give
\[
[L:K]=|G|\le[L:F]\le[L:K].
\]
Equality holds throughout. Hence $[F:K]=1$,
which forces $F=K$.

\emph{(iii) implies (iv).} Assume $L^G=K$.
Let $P\in K[X]$ be irreducible with a root
$\alpha\in L$. Take the distinct images of
$\alpha$ under $G$, say $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r$,
and form the orbit polynomial
\[
Q(X)=\prod_{j=1}^r(X-\alpha_j)\in L[X].
\]
Every $\tau\in G$ permutes these images.
Consequently every coefficient of $Q$ is
fixed by $G$, hence belongs to $L^G=K$.
The monic minimal polynomial of $\alpha$,
an associate of $P$, divides $Q$ because
$Q(\alpha)=0$. As $Q$ splits in $L$,
so does $P$.

\emph{(iv) implies (i).} Write
$L=K(\gamma)$ by the primitive element
theorem. The irreducible minimal polynomial
$m_\gamma$ has a root $\gamma\in L$, so
normality makes it split in $L$.
The splitting field of $m_\gamma$ over
$K$ is contained in $L$ yet contains
$\gamma$; it must therefore equal
$K(\gamma)=L$.

These implications prove the equivalence.
In characteristic $p>0$ one would need
a separability hypothesis in addition to
the splitting-field or normality condition.
\end{proof}

For the inseparable example from Chapter~8, let $K=\mathbb F_p(t)$ and
$L=K(u)$ with $u^p=t$. Its degree is $p$, but
$X^p-t=(X-u)^p$ splits in $L$. Every $K$-automorphism must fix its unique
root $u$, so its automorphism group is trivial. Thus a splitting field
need not be Galois without separability. The present chapter's equivalence
is proved under its stated characteristic-zero contract.

\begin{lemma}[Artin]\label{lem:artin}
Let $L/K$ be finite and $H$ a subgroup of $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$. Then $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $\operatorname{Gal}(L/L^H)=H$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $F=L^H$. We compare $[L:F]$ and $|H|$.

For $\alpha\in L$, let
\[
Q_\alpha(X)=\prod_{\beta\in H\cdot\alpha}(X-\beta),
\]
where the product runs over distinct elements
in the orbit under $H$. Each $h\in H$ permutes
that orbit, so $h$ fixes every coefficient.
Thus $Q_\alpha\in F[X]$, has $\alpha$ as a
root, and has degree at most $|H|$.

Furthermore $Q_\alpha$ has distinct roots.
The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $F$
divides it and therefore is separable.
Every element of $L$ is algebraic and
separable over $F$. In characteristic
zero we may apply the primitive element
theorem: $L=F(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$.
Since the orbit polynomial $Q_\gamma$
annihilates $\gamma$,
\[
[L:F]=\deg m_{\gamma,F}\le|H|.
\]

Conversely, by definition of the fixed field,
every $h\in H$ fixes $F$. Hence
\[
H\subseteq\operatorname{Gal}(L/F).
\]
The automorphism bound gives
\[
|H|\le|\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)|\le[L:F].
\]
Together these inequalities force
\[
\boxed{[L:L^H]=|H|,\qquad
\operatorname{Gal}(L/L^H)=H}.
\]
The final group equality holds because $H$
is a subgroup of the same finite order
as the entire automorphism group.
\end{proof}

A Galois extension has enough automorphisms to recover the base field as the elements fixed by every automorphism. If we fix a smaller subgroup instead, we obtain a larger intermediate field. This reversal is the central correspondence.

\section{The fundamental theorem}

\begin{theorem}[fundamental theorem of Galois theory]\label{thm:ftgt}
Let $L/K$ be a Galois extension with group $G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The maps $H\mapsto L^H$ and $M\mapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)$ are inverse bijections between the subgroups of $G$ and the intermediate fields $K\subseteq M\subseteq L$. They reverse inclusions, and
\[
[L:L^H]=|H|,\qquad[L^H:K]=[G:H].
\]
\item For $\sigma\in G$, $\sigma(L^H)=L^{\sigma H\sigma^{-1}}$.
\item $M/K$ is Galois iff $\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)$ is normal in $G$. Then restriction $\sigma\mapsto\sigma|_M$ induces an isomorphism $G/\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)\cong\operatorname{Gal}(M/K)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$. We will show first that subgroups and intermediate fields recover one another, then that conjugation of a subgroup corresponds exactly to moving its fixed field.

\emph{(a) The two assignments are inverse.}
For a subgroup $H\le G$, the fixed field is
\[
L^H=\{x\in L:\sigma(x)=x\ \text{for every }\sigma\in H\}.
\]
Artin's lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:artin}) tells us not only that $[L:L^H]=|H|$ but that its full fixing group is precisely $H$:
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(L/L^H)=H.
\]
Thus we can start with a subgroup, take the fixed field, and get the same subgroup back.

Now begin instead with an intermediate field $K\subseteq M\subseteq L$. Because $L/K$ is Galois, Theorem~\ref{thm:galoischar} expresses $L$ as the splitting field of a separable polynomial over $K$. That polynomial still has its coefficients in $M$ and still has the same splitting field over $M$. Therefore $L/M$ is also Galois. The fixed-field characterisation in Theorem~\ref{thm:galoischar}(iii) yields
\[
L^{\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)}=M.
\]
So starting with a field and going back through its group also recovers the original object.

If $H\subseteq H'$, then every element fixed by all of $H'$ is certainly fixed by all of $H$. Hence $L^{H'}\subseteq L^H$: the correspondence reverses inclusion.

Finally, Artin's lemma gives $[L:L^H]=|H|$. The tower law and the equality $[L:K]=|G|$ give
\[
[L^H:K]=\frac{[L:K]}{[L:L^H]}
=\frac{|G|}{|H|}=[G:H].
\]

\emph{(b) Conjugation moves fixed fields.}
Fix $\sigma\in G$. An element of $\sigma(L^H)$ has the form $y=\sigma(x)$ with $x$ fixed by $H$. For any $h\in H$,
\[
(\sigma h\sigma^{-1})(y)=\sigma(hx)=\sigma(x)=y.
\]
Thus $\sigma(L^H)\subseteq L^{\sigma H\sigma^{-1}}$. Apply the same argument to $\sigma^{-1}$ and the conjugate subgroup to obtain the reverse inclusion. Therefore
\[
\sigma(L^H)=L^{\sigma H\sigma^{-1}}.
\]

\emph{(c) Normality corresponds to Galois intermediate fields.}
Let $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/M)$, so that $M=L^H$ by (a).

Suppose first that $H$ is normal in $G$. Then $\sigma H\sigma^{-1}=H$ for every $\sigma\in G$. Part (b) gives
\[
\sigma(M)=\sigma(L^H)=L^H=M.
\]
Hence each $\sigma\in G$ restricts to an automorphism of $M$ fixing $K$, and restriction defines a homomorphism
\[
\mathrm{res}:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(M/K).
\]
Its kernel consists exactly of automorphisms fixing $M$, which is $H$. By the first isomorphism theorem, its image has size $|G/H|=[M:K]$, using (a). On the other hand, an extension has at most its degree many $K$-automorphisms (Proposition~\ref{prop:autbound}). Thus
\[
|\operatorname{Gal}(M/K)|=[M:K]
\]
and the image is the whole group. The equality of degree and number of automorphisms shows that $M/K$ is Galois, and
\[
G/H\cong\operatorname{Gal}(M/K).
\]

Conversely, suppose $M/K$ is Galois. Then $M$ is the splitting field over $K$ of a separable polynomial $f\in K[X]$. Every $\sigma\in G$ fixes the coefficients of $f$, so it permutes its roots. Those roots generate $M$, hence $\sigma(M)=M$. By part (b),
\[
L^{\sigma H\sigma^{-1}}=\sigma(M)=M=L^H.
\]
The subgroup--fixed-field correspondence of (a) is injective, so $\sigma H\sigma^{-1}=H$ for every $\sigma\in G$. This says $H\trianglelefteq G$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
The correspondence reverses inclusion. The more automorphisms we require an element to survive, the fewer elements remain fixed. Thus $H_1\subseteq H_2$ gives $L^{H_2}\subseteq L^{H_1}$. The equalities in the theorem say that, in a Galois extension, nothing is lost by passing from a subgroup to its fixed field and back. Normality has a meaning on both sides: a normal subgroup corresponds to an intermediate field preserved by every automorphism of the whole splitting field over the base. Restriction then identifies its Galois group with the quotient group. For $L=\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ the group has four elements; the three subgroups of order $2$ fix $\Q(\sqrt2)$, $\Q(\sqrt3)$, and $\Q(\sqrt6)$. Each fixed field has degree $2$ over $\Q$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:galv4}
$L=\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ is the splitting field of $(X^2-2)(X^2-3)$, of degree $4$ (Example~\ref{ex:sqrt2sqrt3}). Its automorphisms send $\sqrt2\mapsto\pm\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt3\mapsto\pm\sqrt3$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:permroots}); there are at most $4$ sign choices and exactly $4$ automorphisms, so all occur, and $G\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2$. This group has exactly three subgroups of order $2$, so $L$ has exactly three subfields of degree $2$, $\Q(\sqrt2)$, $\Q(\sqrt3)$, $\Q(\sqrt6)$ (fixed respectively by changing only the sign of $\sqrt3$, only that of $\sqrt2$, and both signs), as Chapter~8 announced.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:galx42}
Let $\alpha=\sqrt[4]2$ and $L=\Q(\alpha,i)$, the splitting field of $X^4-2$, of degree $8$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:splitdeg}). An automorphism sends $\alpha$ to one of $\pm\alpha,\pm i\alpha$ and $i$ to $\pm i$; that gives at most $8$ choices, and there are $8$ automorphisms, so each choice occurs. Let $r$ be $\alpha\mapsto i\alpha$, $i\mapsto i$, and $s$ complex conjugation ($\alpha\mapsto\alpha$, $i\mapsto-i$). Then $r$ has order $4$ ($r^k(\alpha)=i^k\alpha$), $s$ has order $2$, and $srs^{-1}=r^{-1}$: both fix $i$ and send $\alpha$ to $-i\alpha$, since $srs(\alpha)=sr(\alpha)=s(i\alpha)=-i\alpha$. So $G=\langle r,s\rangle$ is the dihedral group $D_4$, the symmetries of the square formed by the four roots. Figure~\ref{fig:galoislattice} shows its ten subgroups and the ten corresponding fields; Exercise~\ref{ex:x42check} checks them.
\end{example}

The correspondence becomes very concrete for roots of unity. Each automorphism sends a primitive $n$-th root to another primitive $n$-th root, so it is described by an invertible residue class modulo $n$. We can use the resulting group to decide which regular polygons can be constructed.

\section{Cyclotomic fields and regular polygons}

For $n\ge1$ let $\zeta_n=e^{2i\pi/n}$. The roots of $X^n-1$ are its powers, so $\Q(\zeta_n)$ is the splitting field of $X^n-1$: it is Galois over $\Q$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cyclo}
For every subfield $K\subseteq\C$, the extension $K(\zeta_n)/K$ is Galois, and $\sigma\mapsto a_\sigma$, where $\sigma(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^{a_\sigma}$, is an injective homomorphism $\operatorname{Gal}(K(\zeta_n)/K)\to(\Z/n\Z)^*$. In particular this Galois group is abelian. For $K=\Q$ and $n=p$ prime, it is an isomorphism, and $\operatorname{Gal}(\Q(\zeta_p)/\Q)$ is cyclic of order $p-1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The powers $\zeta_n^j$ are precisely the roots
of $X^n-1$. Hence $K(\zeta_n)$ is their
splitting field over $K$. Characteristic zero
makes those roots distinct, so the Galois
criterion (Theorem~\ref{thm:galoischar})
shows that this extension is Galois.

An automorphism $\sigma$ fixing $K$ sends
$\zeta_n$ to some power $\zeta_n^{a_\sigma}$.
It preserves multiplicative order.
Since $\zeta_n$ has exact order $n$,
the image $\zeta_n^{a_\sigma}$ must
also have order $n$, so
$\gcd(a_\sigma,n)=1$.
Thus $a_\sigma\in(\Z/n\Z)^*$.

For automorphisms $\sigma,\tau$,
\[
(\sigma\tau)(\zeta_n)
=\sigma(\zeta_n^{a_\tau})
=(\zeta_n^{a_\sigma})^{a_\tau}
=\zeta_n^{a_\sigma a_\tau}.
\]
Hence $\sigma\mapsto a_\sigma$ is a
homomorphism into $(\Z/n\Z)^*$.
If it maps $\sigma$ to $1$, then
$\sigma$ fixes $\zeta_n$ as well as
$K$, hence fixes the generated field:
the map is injective.
Its target is abelian, so the
Galois group is abelian too.

If $K=\Q$ and $n=p$ is prime,
the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p$
is irreducible (Volume~1, Chapter~6),
giving
\[
[\Q(\zeta_p):\Q]=p-1=|(\Z/p\Z)^*|.
\]
The extension is Galois, so its
group has order $p-1$.
Our injection between two groups of
this size is an isomorphism.
The group $(\Z/p\Z)^*$ is cyclic
by Volume~2's primitive-root theorem,
proving the final claim.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Working out every cyclotomic degree.}
For composite $n$, sending a primitive root to another primitive root still gives
an injection into the units modulo $n$. To prove it is onto, we must know that
all primitive roots share one minimal polynomial. The following worked problem
earns that fact before we use it for polygon classification or later exercises.
Try its three parts before reading the hint and full solution.

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:phin}
Let $n\ge1$ and $\Phi_n=\prod_{\gcd(a,n)=1}(X-\zeta_n^a)$, the $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\Phi_n\in\Z[X]$ (use $X^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d$ and induction).
\item Let $f$ be the minimal polynomial of $\zeta_n$, and $p$ a prime not dividing $n$. Show that $f(\zeta^p)=0$ for every root $\zeta$ of $f$.
\item Deduce that $\Phi_n$ is irreducible, and that $\operatorname{Gal}(\Q(\zeta_n)/\Q)\cong(\Z/n\Z)^*$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{hint}{ex:phin}
(b) If not, $\zeta^p$ is a root of another irreducible factor $g$ of $X^n-1$; then $f$ divides $g(X^p)$. Reduce modulo $p$: $\bar g(X^p)=\bar g(X)^p$. Why is that impossible?
\end{hint}

\begin{solution}{ex:phin}
\idea The polynomial $X^n-1$ has all the $n$-th roots of unity as its roots. First separate those roots by their exact order. Then show that if an irreducible factor contains one primitive root, it must contain every primitive root. The key is that reduction modulo a prime $p\nmid n$ preserves the distinctness of the roots of $X^n-1$.

\writeup \emph{(a) Integer coefficients.}
Every $n$-th root of unity has some exact order $d$ dividing $n$. The roots of exact order $d$ are precisely those of $\Phi_d$. They are all simple, since the derivative of $X^n-1$ in characteristic zero is $nX^{n-1}$ and has no root in common with $X^n-1$. Thus
\[
X^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(X).
\]
We prove $\Phi_n\in\mathbb Z[X]$ by induction. For $n=1$ we have $\Phi_1=X-1$. For larger $n$, the product
\[
D(X)=\prod_{\substack{d\mid n\\d<n}}\Phi_d(X)
\]
is monic with integer coefficients by induction. Divide $X^n-1$ by $D$ using long division. Because $D$ is monic, each subtraction has integer coefficients, so the quotient is in $\mathbb Z[X]$. The factorisation above says that quotient is exactly $\Phi_n$.

\emph{(b) Raising a root to a prime power.}
Let $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$ be the monic minimal polynomial of $\zeta_n$ over $\mathbb Q$. The integrality follows because $f$ is a monic factor of the monic integer polynomial $X^n-1$, by Gauss's lemma. Write
\[
X^n-1=f(X)h(X),\qquad f,h\in\mathbb Z[X]\text{ monic}.
\]
Take any complex root $\zeta$ of $f$ and a prime $p\nmid n$. Since $\zeta^n=1$, its $p$-th power is still an $n$-th root. Suppose, to get a contradiction, that $f(\zeta^p)\ne0$. It follows that
\[
h(\zeta^p)=0,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad f(X)\mid h(X^p)
\]
over $\mathbb Q[X]$, because $f$ is the minimal polynomial of $\zeta$. Gauss's lemma upgrades this divisibility to $\mathbb Z[X]$.

Reduce modulo $p$, and denote reduced polynomials by bars. In $\mathbb F_p[X]$ the Frobenius identity gives
\[
\overline{h(X^p)}=\bar h(X)^p.
\]
Thus $\bar f\mid\bar h^p$. Choose any monic irreducible factor $u$ of the non-constant polynomial $\bar f$. (Its degree is unchanged by reduction because $f$ is monic.) Then $u\mid\bar h^p$, so the irreducibility of $u$ implies $u\mid\bar h$. It also divides $\bar f$, and therefore
\[
u^2\mid\bar f\,\bar h=X^n-1\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_p[X].
\]
This is impossible. Since $p\nmid n$, the derivative $nX^{n-1}$ is not zero modulo $p$, and
\[
\gcd(X^n-1,nX^{n-1})=1.
\]
Hence $X^n-1$ is square-free over $\mathbb F_p$. The contradiction proves $f(\zeta^p)=0$.

\emph{(c) All primitive roots occur.}
The roots of $\Phi_n$ are $\zeta_n^a$ for $1\le a\le n$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Every prime dividing such an $a$ is prime to $n$. Starting with $\zeta_n$, apply part (b) successively for the prime factors of $a$. Each step starts from another root of $f$; hence every $\zeta_n^a$ is a root of $f$.

All primitive roots are simple, so $\Phi_n\mid f$. But $\zeta_n$ is a root of $\Phi_n\in\mathbb Q[X]$, while $f$ is its monic minimal polynomial; therefore $f\mid\Phi_n$. Both are monic, giving
\[
\boxed{f=\Phi_n,\qquad [\mathbb Q(\zeta_n):\mathbb Q]=\varphi(n).}
\]
Every automorphism must send $\zeta_n$ to another primitive root, and conversely every such choice defines a $\mathbb Q$-automorphism because all those roots have the same minimal polynomial. Composition multiplies the exponents, so
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q)
       \cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^*.}
\]
For $n=1$, both groups are trivial, as the same conclusion predicts.

\tools Gauss's lemma (Chapter~6), minimal polynomials (Chapter~8), Frobenius (Chapter~9), and the cyclotomic automorphisms of this chapter.

\leads The arithmetic group $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^*$ from Volume~2 now appears as a group of symmetries of a field. That is precisely the connection the Galois correspondence is meant to expose.
\end{solution}

We now have $[\Q(\zeta_n):\Q]=\varphi(n)$ and
$\operatorname{Gal}(\Q(\zeta_n)/\Q)\cong(\Z/n\Z)^*$ for every $n$.
For the construction argument in the next theorem, the earlier injection alone
will already suffice.

\begin{theorem}[Gauss, Wantzel]\label{thm:gw}
For an integer $n\ge3$, if $\varphi(n)$ is a power of $2$, the regular $n$-gon is constructible with ruler and compass. In particular the $p$-gon is constructible for every Fermat prime $p=2^{2^k}+1$: $3$, $5$, $17$, $257$, $65537$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $L=\Q(\zeta_n)$ and
$G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:cyclo}, this Galois
group injects into $(\Z/n\Z)^*$.
Lagrange's theorem therefore gives
$|G|\mid\varphi(n)$.
If $\varphi(n)=2^a$, then $|G|=2^m$
for some $0\le m\le a$.

A group of order $2^m$ has a subgroup
of index two, by Corollary~\ref{cor:pk}.
Apply the same assertion inside that
subgroup, and repeat to obtain
\[
G=G_0\supset G_1\supset\cdots
\supset G_m=\{e\},\qquad
[G_j:G_{j+1}]=2.
\]
When $m=0$, the chain has no steps
and $L=\Q$; the argument still applies.
Every index-two subgroup is normal,
so $G_{j+1}\trianglelefteq G_j$.

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory
turns this decreasing subgroup chain
into an increasing field chain,
\[
\Q=L^{G_0}=K_0\subset K_1
\subset\cdots\subset K_m=L,
\qquad K_j=L^{G_j}.
\]
By Artin's lemma,
$[L:K_j]=|G_j|$. The tower law then yields
\[
[K_{j+1}:K_j]
=\frac{[L:K_j]}{[L:K_{j+1}]}
=\frac{|G_j|}{|G_{j+1}|}=2.
\]
Thus $\zeta_n\in L=K_m$ lies in a tower
of quadratic extensions starting from $\Q$.
Wantzel's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:wantzel})
makes $\zeta_n$ constructible.

The points $1,\zeta_n,\zeta_n^2,\ldots$
are the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.
Constructibility of $\zeta_n$ and closure
under multiplication make all these
vertices constructible.

For a Fermat prime $p=2^{2^k}+1$,
$\varphi(p)=p-1=2^{2^k}$ is a power of two,
so the criterion applies.
\end{proof}

Conversely, if a regular $n$-gon is constructible, construct its center and scale one radius to $1$. Its vertices then include $\zeta_n$. By Wantzel's theorem, $\zeta_n$ lies in a quadratic tower of total degree $2^r$. The tower law makes $[\Q(\zeta_n):\Q]=\varphi(n)$ divide $2^r$, so it is a power of $2$. This proves both directions. We can now determine exactly which $n\ge3$ occur. By the Chinese remainder theorem for rings (Theorem~\ref{thm:crtring}), if $n=\prod p^{a}$ then $\Z/n\Z\cong\prod\Z/p^{a}\Z$, and the unit groups correspond, so $\varphi(n)=\prod\varphi(p^{a})$. The group $(\Z/p^{a}\Z)^*$ is the residues not divisible by $p$, and there are $\varphi(p^{a})=p^{a}-p^{a-1}=p^{a-1}(p-1)$ of them. For the prime $2$ and $k\ge1$, the odd residues give $\varphi(2^{k})=2^{k-1}$, a power of $2$. For an odd prime $p$, $\varphi(p^{a})$ is a power of $2$ only if $a=1$, since otherwise $p$ divides it, and only if $p-1$ is a power of $2$. A prime of the form $2^{m}+1$ has $m$ itself a power of $2$: if an odd integer $r$ divides $m$, then $2^{m/r}+1$ divides $2^{m}+1$, and both factors exceed $1$ when $r>1$ and $m/r>0$. Such primes are the Fermat primes. Conversely, if $n=2^{k}p_1\cdots p_s$ with distinct Fermat primes $p_i$, then each $p_i-1$ is a power of $2$, and so is $\varphi(n)$. Nobody knows whether there are Fermat primes beyond $65537$. Exercise~\ref{ex:gauss17} computes the first step of Gauss' tower for the $17$-gon.

\begin{remark}[Frobenius and an outlook on reduction modulo $p$]
Chapter~6 observed that $X^4+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$ but factors modulo
every prime. We can now explain the second assertion using only finite fields.
For odd $p$, the integer $p^2-1$ is divisible by $8$. The cyclic group
$\mathbb F_{p^2}^*$ therefore contains all eight roots of $X^8-1$, including
all four roots of $X^4+1$. Every irreducible factor of $X^4+1$ over
$\mathbb F_p$ consequently has degree at most $2$. For $p=2$,
$X^4+1=(X+1)^4$. No prime makes it irreducible.

More generally, let a monic $f\in\Z[X]$ have squarefree reduction modulo
$p$. In a finite splitting field, Frobenius permutes the roots. A root
$\beta$ of an irreducible factor of degree $d$ has Frobenius orbit of
length $d$: it generates $\mathbb F_p(\beta)$, whose Frobenius has order
$d$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:galfq}. The factor degrees are therefore exactly
the cycle lengths of this permutation.

The comparison with the complex roots is a further theorem, quoted here
as an outlook to Volume~13: Dedekind's theorem puts a permutation with
these same cycle lengths in the Galois group of $f$ over $\Q$.
For $X^4+1$, that group is $(\Z/8\Z)^*\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2$ and has no
$4$-cycle, in agreement with the finite-field proof above. Factoring a
polynomial modulo several primes can thus provide information about its
Galois group; proving that general comparison requires more arithmetic.
\end{remark}

Constructing a regular polygon is a question about towers of quadratic extensions. Solving an equation by radicals allows roots of higher orders as well. The question is whether the corresponding Galois group can be assembled from abelian steps.

\section{Solvability by radicals}\label{sec:radicals}

\begin{definition}
A nonzero polynomial $P\in K[X]$ is \emph{solvable by radicals} if its roots lie in a field $K_r$ obtained by a chain
\[
\begin{gathered}K=K_0\subseteq K_1\subseteq\dots\subseteq K_r,\qq K_j=K_{j-1}(a_j),\quad a_j^{n_j}\in K_{j-1},\end{gathered}
\]
for some $a_j\in\C$ and $n_j\ge1$: each step adjoins an $n_j$-th root of an element already obtained. Such a chain is a \emph{radical tower}.
\end{definition}

The quadratic formula gives a radical tower of degree at most two. Exercise~\ref{ex:cardano} will derive the cubic formula; the criterion proved below will also explain why every polynomial of degree at most four is solvable by radicals. First we describe the steps of a radical tower.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kummerstep}
Let $m\ge1$, let $F\subseteq\C$ contain $\zeta_m$, and let $b\in\C$ with $b^m=c\in F$. Then $F(b)/F$ is Galois with abelian Galois group.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $b^m=c\in F$. If $b=0$, then
$F(b)=F$, and the extension is
trivially Galois with trivial group.

Suppose $b\ne0$. Because $F$ contains
all powers of the primitive root
$\zeta_m$, the roots of $X^m-c$
are
\[
b,\ \zeta_m b,\ \ldots,\ \zeta_m^{m-1}b,
\]
and all lie in $F(b)$.
The polynomial splits there, and
that field is generated over $F$
by one of its roots. Thus $F(b)$
is the splitting field of $X^m-c$.
Characteristic zero makes the roots
distinct; the Galois criterion makes
$F(b)/F$ Galois.

For $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(F(b)/F)$,
the root-permutation lemma gives
$\sigma(b)=\zeta_m^{k_\sigma}b$
for a unique class $k_\sigma\bmod m$.
Each automorphism fixes $\zeta_m\in F$.
Consequently
\[
(\sigma\tau)(b)
=\sigma(\zeta_m^{k_\tau}b)
=\zeta_m^{k_\tau+k_\sigma}b.
\]
So $\sigma\mapsto k_\sigma$ is a
homomorphism to the additive cyclic
group $\Z/m\Z$. It is injective,
since $F(b)$ is generated by $b$
over the fixed field $F$.
Every subgroup of a cyclic group
is abelian, so the Galois group is abelian.

The $m$-th roots of unity are the key:
without them, adjoining one radical
need not produce a normal extension.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Galois]\label{thm:radsolv}
If a nonzero $P\in K[X]$ is solvable by radicals, its Galois group is solvable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let
\[
K=K_0\subseteq K_1\subseteq\cdots
\subseteq K_r
\]
be a radical tower containing every root of $P$.
We may write $K_j=K_{j-1}(a_j)$ with
$a_j^{n_j}\in K_{j-1}$. Each step is finite because $a_j$ satisfies
$X^{n_j}-a_j^{n_j}$ over the previous field. The tower law and
Corollary~\ref{cor:finitealg} make every $a_j$ algebraic over $K$.
Put $N=n_1\cdots n_r$, with the empty product $N=1$ when $r=0$.
The obstruction is that the intermediate
radical steps need not be normal, and so
their individual automorphism groups
need not form quotients of one another.
We repair this by adjoining roots of unity
and all conjugates of each radical.

\emph{Place the radical tower inside a splitting field.}
Let $E$ be the splitting field over $K$
of
\[
(X^N-1)\prod_{j=1}^r m_{a_j}(X),
\]
where $m_{a_j}$ denotes the minimal
polynomial of $a_j$ over $K$.
This field contains $K_r$ because it
contains every $a_j$, and it is Galois
over $K$ because it is a splitting
field in characteristic zero.
Put $G=\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)$.

Every root of $m_{a_j}$ is an image
$\sigma(a_j)$ for some $\sigma\in G$.
Indeed the root-extension lemma sends
$a_j$ to any such root, and the
splitting-field extension theorem
extends that isomorphism to an
automorphism of $E$.

Write $E_0=K(\zeta_N)$.
List the conjugates $\sigma(a_j)$
as $b_1,\ldots,b_s$, taking all
conjugates of $a_1$ first, then
those of $a_2$, and so on.
For each $i$ put $E_i=E_{i-1}(b_i)$.
We may repeat a conjugate in the
list without harm: a repeated element
gives a trivial extension.
All conjugates together generate
$E$ over $E_0$, so $E_s=E$.

Suppose $b_i=\sigma(a_j)$. Because
$a_j^{n_j}\in K_{j-1}
=K(a_1,\ldots,a_{j-1})$,
we obtain
\[
b_i^{n_j}
=\sigma(a_j^{n_j})
\in K(\sigma(a_1),\ldots,
      \sigma(a_{j-1}))
\subseteq E_{i-1}.
\]
The last inclusion follows from our
ordering: every conjugate of every
earlier radical has already been
adjoined. Furthermore
$\zeta_{n_j}=\zeta_N^{N/n_j}$
belongs to $E_0$ and hence to
$E_{i-1}$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:kummerstep} now says
that each $E_i/E_{i-1}$ is Galois
with an abelian Galois group.
The first extension $E_0/K$
is cyclotomic and likewise Galois
with an abelian group, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:cyclo}.

\emph{Turn the tower into a group series.}
Set $H_{-1}=G$ and
\[
H_i=\operatorname{Gal}(E/E_i)
\qquad(0\le i\le s).
\]
The last field is $E_s=E$, so
$H_s=\{e\}$.
Each extension $E_i/E_{i-1}$
is Galois. By the fundamental
theorem of Galois theory,
\[
H_i\trianglelefteq H_{i-1},
\qquad
H_{i-1}/H_i
\cong\operatorname{Gal}(E_i/E_{i-1})
\]
is abelian. For the first
step the same theorem gives
\[
G/H_0\cong\operatorname{Gal}(E_0/K),
\]
also abelian. Hence
\[
G=H_{-1}\trianglerighteq H_0
\trianglerighteq\cdots
\trianglerighteq H_s=\{e\}
\]
is a normal series with abelian
successive quotients. This is the
definition of a solvable group.

\emph{Pass to the polynomial.}
Let $L$ be the splitting field of
$P$ over $K$. Its roots lie in
$K_r\subseteq E$, so $L\subseteq E$.
Because $L/K$ is itself Galois,
restriction gives a surjective
homomorphism
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)\to
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)
\]
with kernel $\operatorname{Gal}(E/L)$,
by the Galois correspondence.
Thus $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is
a quotient of the solvable group
$G$, and is solvable by
Proposition~\ref{prop:solv}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Abel, Ruffini]\label{thm:abelruffini}
The Galois group of $X^5-4X+2$ over $\Q$ is $\mathfrak S_5$. Consequently its roots cannot be expressed by radicals, and there is no formula in radicals for the roots of the general quintic.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $f(X)=X^5-4X+2$. We will find two kinds of permutations in its Galois group: a $5$-cycle and a transposition. Together they will give every permutation of the five roots.

\emph{First, the roots.} All non-leading coefficients of $f$ are divisible by $2$, while its constant coefficient $2$ is not divisible by $4$. Eisenstein's criterion at $2$ therefore makes $f$ irreducible over $\Q$.

Check four values:
\[
f(-2)=-22,\qquad f(0)=2,\qquad f(1)=-1,\qquad f(2)=26.
\]
The signs alternate. By the intermediate-value theorem there is a root in each of the three disjoint intervals $(-2,0)$, $(0,1)$, and $(1,2)$.

There cannot be a fourth real root. Indeed, $f'(X)=5X^4-4$ has just two real zeros, $\pm\rho$ where $\rho=(4/5)^{1/4}$. Four distinct real roots of $f$ would, by Rolle's theorem, force at least three distinct zeros of $f'$. Thus $f$ has exactly three distinct real roots.

Irreducibility in characteristic zero already guarantees that all roots
are simple (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}). We can also check this directly without
restricting attention to real critical points. If a complex number $z$
were a common zero of $f$ and $f'$, then $z^4=4/5$, so
\[
f(z)=z\frac45-4z+2=2-\frac{16}{5}z.
\]
Its vanishing would force $z=5/8$, but $(5/8)^4\ne4/5$.
Thus there is no common zero, real or complex. All five roots are simple,
and the remaining two form a non-real conjugate pair.

\emph{Next, the permutations.} Let $L$ be the splitting field and $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:permroots}, $G$ acts faithfully on the five roots, so $G\le\mathfrak S_5$.

Choose a root $\alpha$. Irreducibility gives $[\Q(\alpha):\Q]=5$. The tower law and the Galois equality give
\[
5\mid[L:\Q]=|G|.
\]
By Cauchy's theorem, $G$ has an element of order $5$. Such an element of $\mathfrak S_5$ must be a $5$-cycle; call it $c$.

Complex conjugation preserves $L$, since it permutes the roots of the real polynomial $f$. Its restriction to $L$ fixes the three real roots and exchanges the two non-real roots. It therefore gives a transposition $\tau=(a\,b)\in G$.

A power $c^k$ takes $a$ to $b$ because a $5$-cycle visits every root. We may take $1\le k\le4$, so $c^k$ is still a $5$-cycle. Rename the roots in the order visited by $c^k$:
\[
c^k=(1\,2\,3\,4\,5),\qquad\tau=(1\,2).
\]
Conjugating $\tau$ by successive powers of $c^k$ gives $(2\,3)$, $(3\,4)$, and $(4\,5)$. These adjacent transpositions generate $\mathfrak S_5$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:gens}). Hence $G=\mathfrak S_5$.

The group $\mathfrak S_5$ is not solvable, because of the simple non-abelian group $A_5$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:solv}(d)). By Theorem~\ref{thm:radsolv}, the roots of $f$ cannot all be expressed by radicals over $\Q$.
In fact no individual root can be. If one root lay in a radical tower,
the preceding theorem's construction, applied to that tower, would put it
in a Galois extension $E/\Q$ with solvable group. Normality would put every
root of its irreducible polynomial $f$ in $E$. Restriction to the splitting
field would then make $\mathfrak S_5$ a quotient of a solvable group,
a contradiction.

Finally, a radical formula for \emph{every} quintic would also solve this particular quintic. Since it does not, there is no general quintic formula in radicals.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Two small observations force a very large group. Eisenstein at $2$ makes the quintic irreducible, so its Galois group acts transitively on five roots and has order divisible by $5$. Cauchy's theorem gives a $5$-cycle. The polynomial has three real roots and one non-real conjugate pair, so complex conjugation supplies a transposition. Because $5$ is prime, a power of the $5$-cycle connects the two points of that transposition. Conjugation then gives adjacent swaps, which generate every permutation. We have reached all of $\mathfrak S_5$. Since that group is not solvable, the earlier theorem rules out a formula by radicals. Notice what has \emph{not} been ruled out: numerical approximations, special functions, or radical formulas for some other particular quintics.

The converse of Theorem~\ref{thm:radsolv} is also true, and completes Galois' criterion. It rests on a converse of Lemma~\ref{lem:kummerstep}, due to Lagrange and Kummer.

\begin{lemma}[Dedekind]\label{lem:dedekind}
Distinct automorphisms $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$ of a field $L$ are linearly independent over $L$: if $c_1\sigma_1(x)+\dots+c_n\sigma_n(x)=0$ for all $x\in L$, with $c_i\in L$, then all $c_i=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose there were a non-trivial
linear relation between distinct
automorphisms $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n$:
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\sigma_i(x)=0
\qquad\text{for every }x\in L.
\]
Choose such a relation with the
fewest non-zero coefficients.
After relabelling, write it as
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r c_i\sigma_i(x)=0,
\qquad c_i\ne0.
\]
There must be at least two summands:
a single non-zero coefficient times
an automorphism cannot vanish at $x=1$.

Distinctness of $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$
means there is some $y\in L$ with
$\sigma_1(y)\ne\sigma_2(y)$.
Substitute $yx$ into the chosen relation.
Because each $\sigma_i$ respects products,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r
c_i\sigma_i(y)\sigma_i(x)=0.
\]
Multiply the original relation by
$\sigma_1(y)$ and subtract. The term
$i=1$ disappears and we obtain
\[
\sum_{i=2}^r
c_i\bigl(\sigma_i(y)-\sigma_1(y)\bigr)
\sigma_i(x)=0
\qquad\text{for every }x.
\]
Its coefficient at $i=2$ is non-zero,
since $c_2\ne0$ and
$\sigma_2(y)\ne\sigma_1(y)$.
So it is a non-trivial relation
with at most $r-1$ terms.
That contradicts the minimality of $r$.
Hence no non-trivial relation exists:
the automorphisms are linearly independent
as functions $L\to L$ over $L$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Kummer]\label{prop:kummer}
Let $m\ge1$, let $F\subseteq\C$ contain $\zeta_m$, and let $E/F$ be Galois with cyclic group of order $m$. Then $E=F(\theta)$ for some $\theta$ with $\theta^m\in F$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\sigma$ generate the cyclic group
$\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)$ of order $m$,
and let $\zeta_m\in F$ be primitive.
For a variable $x\in E$, consider
the Lagrange resolvent
\[
R(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}
\zeta_m^{-j}\sigma^j(x).
\]
This is an $F$-linear map $E\to E$.
It cannot vanish identically:
if it did, the distinct field
automorphisms
$\mathrm{id},\sigma,\ldots,\sigma^{m-1}$
would satisfy a non-trivial
linear relation with coefficients
$\zeta_m^{-j}$, contradicting
Lemma~\ref{lem:dedekind}.

Choose $x$ for which $\theta=R(x)\ne0$.
Because $\sigma$ fixes $\zeta_m$,
reindex the sum modulo $m$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\theta)
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}
\zeta_m^{-j}\sigma^{j+1}(x)\\
&=\zeta_m
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}
\zeta_m^{-k}\sigma^k(x)
=\zeta_m\theta.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore $\sigma(\theta^m)=\theta^m$,
so every power of $\sigma$ fixes
$\theta^m$. The fixed field of the
whole Galois group is $F$,
by the Galois correspondence.
Hence $\theta^m\in F$.

Moreover
$\sigma^j(\theta)=\zeta_m^j\theta$.
Since $\theta\ne0$ and $\zeta_m$
has exact order $m$, no power
$\sigma^j$ with $0<j<m$ fixes $\theta$.
Thus the subgroup fixing $F(\theta)$
is trivial. By the fundamental theorem
of Galois theory,
\[
[E:F(\theta)]=
|\operatorname{Gal}(E/F(\theta))|=1.
\]
So $E=F(\theta)$, as required.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Galois' criterion]\label{thm:galoiscrit}
For $K\subseteq\C$ and a nonzero $P\in K[X]$, $P$ is solvable by radicals iff its Galois group is solvable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Radicals imply solvability.}
This is precisely Theorem~\ref{thm:radsolv};
we have already proved that direction.

\emph{Solvability implies a radical tower.}
Let $L$ be the splitting field of $P$ over
$K$, and assume its Galois group
$G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is solvable.
Write $N=|G|$.

First adjoin all $N$-th roots of unity:
\[
K'=K(\zeta_N),\qquad
L'=L(\zeta_N).
\]
The extension $K'/K$ is a radical step
because $\zeta_N^N=1\in K$.
The field $L'$ is the splitting field of
$P$ over $K'$ and is therefore Galois
over $K'$. Put
$G'=\operatorname{Gal}(L'/K')$.

Restriction to $L$ embeds $G'$ into
$G$. To see this carefully, $L/K$ is
Galois and therefore any $K'$-automorphism
of $L'$ maps the roots of $P$ to other
roots of $P$, so preserves $L$.
If it restricts to the identity on $L$,
it also fixes $\zeta_N\in K'$ and hence
every element of $L'=L(\zeta_N)$.
Thus restriction is injective.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:solv},
subgroups of solvable groups are solvable.
Therefore $G'$ is solvable. Lagrange's
theorem also gives $|G'|\mid N$.

Choose a series
\[
\{e\}=G'_0\trianglelefteq G'_1
\trianglelefteq\cdots
\trianglelefteq G'_r=G'
\]
whose quotients are abelian. Discard any repeated subgroups, so every
remaining inclusion is strict (when $G'$ is trivial, use the empty series).
We can refine it so that each quotient
has prime order. Indeed, an abelian
quotient of composite order has a
proper non-trivial subgroup (choose
an element of prime order by Cauchy's
theorem). Its full preimage gives a
strictly intermediate subgroup.
Because the quotient is abelian,
the intermediate subgroup is normal
in the upper group, and the two new
quotients remain abelian. Repeating
this procedure eventually
makes every successive quotient
cyclic of prime order $q_i$. It terminates because each insertion adds
a strict inclusion, while the product of all indices remains $|G'|$;
a strict chain has at most $\log_2|G'|$ steps.
All these primes divide $|G'|$
and therefore divide $N$.

Use the Galois correspondence to define
\[
F_i=(L')^{G'_i}.
\]
The group series turns into a field tower
in reverse order:
\[
K'=F_r\subset F_{r-1}\subset\cdots
\subset F_0=L'.
\]
For each $i$, the normal inclusion
$G'_{i-1}\trianglelefteq G'_i$
and the fundamental theorem yield
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(F_{i-1}/F_i)
\cong G'_i/G'_{i-1},
\]
a cyclic group of prime order $q_i$.

Because $q_i\mid N$, the base field
$K'$ contains a primitive $q_i$-th
root of unity $\zeta_{q_i}$;
so every $F_i$ does as well.
The cyclic Kummer proposition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:kummer})
therefore gives an element $\theta_i$
with
\[
F_{i-1}=F_i(\theta_i),
\qquad \theta_i^{q_i}\in F_i.
\]
These are radical steps.
Join them to the initial radical step
$K\subseteq K(\zeta_N)$.
We have obtained a tower of radical
extensions ending at $L'$, which
contains every root of $P$.
Thus $P$ is solvable by radicals.

The root-of-unity enlargement was
essential: a cyclic extension need
not be a single radical extension
before the relevant roots of unity
have been adjoined.

Together the two directions prove
the claimed equivalence. They also settle degrees at most four without
requiring a quartic formula: the faithful root action embeds the Galois
group in $\mathfrak S_4$ (add fixed letters if there are fewer roots).
Chapter~5 gave the abelian-quotient series
$\{e\}\trianglelefteq V\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq\mathfrak S_4$.
Its subgroups are solvable by Proposition~\ref{prop:solv}, so the criterion
puts all roots of any nonzero polynomial of degree at most four in a
radical tower. A nonzero constant has no roots and uses the empty tower.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
A field degree counts how many independent coordinates a root requires;
its Galois group records how those coordinates can move while preserving
all polynomial relations. The correspondence makes these two descriptions
agree exactly for a splitting field in characteristic zero. Quadratic
towers correspond to successive index-two subgroups. After adjoining the
needed roots of unity, radical towers correspond to cyclic steps, and
solvability measures whether those steps can reach the whole group.

For the quintic $X^5-4X+2$, the obstruction is now concrete: the derived
structure of $\mathfrak S_5$ cannot be built from abelian quotients.
Our next question returns to groups themselves. A field automorphism acts
linearly over its fixed field. What can we learn about an arbitrary finite
group by making it act through matrices, and how much of that action can
be recovered from traces? This leads to representations and characters. For a further direction, the
inverse Galois problem asks which finite groups occur as Galois groups of
polynomials over $\Q$; Völklein's book below takes up that question.
\end{beauty}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:q235}
Show that $\operatorname{Gal}(\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5)/\Q)\cong(\Z/2\Z)^3$, and count the intermediate fields of degree $2$ and of degree $4$ over $\Q$. List the quadratic ones.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:x42check}
In Example~\ref{ex:galx42}, check that each field of Figure~\ref{fig:galoislattice} is fixed by the subgroup drawn at the same place. Which of these fields are Galois over $\Q$? Show directly that $\Q(\sqrt[4]2)/\Q$ is not normal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:zeta7}
Let $\zeta=\zeta_7$. Show that $\Q(\zeta)$ has exactly two subfields other than $\Q$ and $\Q(\zeta)$: $\Q(\cos\frac{2\pi}7)$, of degree $3$, and a quadratic field. Show that $\eta=\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^4$ satisfies $\eta^2+\eta+2=0$, and identify the quadratic field.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:gauss17}
Let $\zeta=\zeta_{17}$; $3$ generates $(\Z/17\Z)^*$. Put $\eta_0=\sum_{k\text{ even}}\zeta^{3^k}$ and $\eta_1=\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\zeta^{3^k}$ ($0\le k\le15$). Show that $\eta_0+\eta_1=-1$ and $\eta_0\eta_1=-4$, so that $\eta_{0,1}=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{17}}2$: the first floor of Gauss' tower.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:sp}
Let $p$ be prime and $f\in\Q[X]$ irreducible of degree $p$, with exactly two non-real roots. Show that its Galois group is $\mathfrak S_p$. Deduce that $X^5-6X+3$ is not solvable by radicals.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:discr}
Let $f\in K[X]$ be a cubic with distinct roots $r_1,r_2,r_3$ and $\delta=(r_1-r_2)(r_1-r_3)(r_2-r_3)$. Show that $\delta^2\in K$, and that the Galois group, viewed in $\mathfrak S_3$, is contained in $A_3$ iff $\delta\in K$. For $f=X^3+pX+q$, $\delta^2=-4p^3-27q^2$ (you may use this identity or prove it by expansion). Deduce the Galois groups of $X^3-2$ and of $X^3-3X+1$ over $\Q$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:cardano}
Let $X^3+pX+q$ have roots $r_1,r_2,r_3$ (so $r_1+r_2+r_3=0$), let $\omega=\zeta_3$, and $u=r_1+\omega r_2+\omega^2r_3$, $v=r_1+\omega^2r_2+\omega r_3$ (Lagrange resolvents). Show that $uv=-3p$ and $u^3+v^3=-27q$. Deduce Cardano's formula
\[
\begin{aligned}r_1&=\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2+\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}}\eqbr+\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2-\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}},\end{aligned}
\]
where the two cube roots are chosen with product $-p/3$.
\end{exercise}


\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:kronecker}
\emph{Kronecker's theorem.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Let $P\in\Z[X]$ be monic with $P(0)\ne0$, and suppose that all its complex roots have modulus at most $1$. Show
that they are all roots of unity, and that if $P$ is irreducible, $P$ is a cyclotomic polynomial.
\item Let $P\in\Z[X]$ be monic, with all its roots real and in $[-2,2]$. Show that its roots are of the form $2\cos(2\pi r)$
with $r\in\Q$.
\item The number $z=\frac{3+4i}5$ has modulus $1$, and so does its only conjugate $\bar z$. Show that it is not a root of
unity. Which hypothesis of (a) fails?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:rattan}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Let $\zeta_n=e^{2i\pi/n}$. Show that the roots of unity in $\Q(\zeta_n)$ are the $n$-th roots of unity if $n$ is
even, and the $2n$-th roots of unity if $n$ is odd.
\item Let $\theta$ be a rational multiple of $\pi$ with $\tan\theta\in\Q$. Show that $\tan\theta\in\{-1,0,1\}$.
\item Show that the only regular polygon whose vertices all lie in $\Z^2$ is the square.
\item (Niven) Let $\theta$ be a rational multiple of $\pi$ with $\cos\theta\in\Q$. Show that
$\cos\theta\in\{0,\pm\frac12,\pm1\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:ramacube}
\emph{Ramanujan's cube roots.} With real cube roots, show that
\[
\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{2\pi}7}+\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{4\pi}7}+\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{8\pi}7}=\sqrt[3]{\frac{5-3\sqrt[3]7}2}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:q235}
\idea Each square root of a different prime should contribute an independent choice of sign. Prove that $\sqrt5$ is not already in $\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$, then count subgroups of the resulting eight-element Galois group as vector subspaces over $\mathbb F_2$.

\writeup Put $M=\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$. Its degree over $\Q$ is $4$, and its Galois group is $(\Z/2\Z)^2$, acting by independent sign changes. The three non-trivial proper subgroups have fixed fields
\[
\Q(\sqrt2),\quad\Q(\sqrt3),\quad\Q(\sqrt6).
\]
If $\sqrt5\in M$, then $\Q(\sqrt5)$, a quadratic extension of $\Q$, would have to equal one of these three fields. But if $\sqrt5=a+b\sqrt d$ with $a,b\in\Q$ and $d\in\{2,3,6\}$, squaring and comparing the rational and $\sqrt d$ coefficients gives $2ab=0$. The case $b=0$ would make $5$ a rational square, so $a=0$ and $b^2=5/d$. Thus equality of the quadratic fields would make $5/d$ a rational square. None of $5/2$, $5/3$, $5/6$ is a square in $\Q$, as is seen by comparing prime exponents in numerator and denominator. Thus $\sqrt5\notin M$.

It follows that $L=M(\sqrt5)$ has degree
\[
[L:\Q]=[L:M][M:\Q]=2\cdot4=8.
\]
The field $L$ is the splitting field of
\[
(X^2-2)(X^2-3)(X^2-5).
\]
This polynomial is separable over $\Q$, so $L/\Q$ is Galois and $|\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)|=8$.

Every automorphism must send each $\sqrt d$ to another root of $X^2-d$, namely $\pm\sqrt d$. Therefore the Galois group embeds in the eight sign triples. Since it has eight elements, every sign triple occurs, giving
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)\cong(\Z/2\Z)^3}.
\]

Identify this group with the three-dimensional vector space $\mathbb F_2^3$. By the Galois correspondence, quadratic intermediate fields are fixed fields of subgroups of order $4$, namely planes of dimension $2$. Every such plane is the kernel of one non-zero linear functional $\mathbb F_2^3\to\mathbb F_2$. There are $2^3-1=7$ non-zero functionals; over $\mathbb F_2$ no two of them differ by a nontrivial non-zero scalar. Hence there are exactly seven quadratic intermediate fields:
\[
\boxed{\Q(\sqrt2),\;\Q(\sqrt3),\;\Q(\sqrt5),\;
\Q(\sqrt6),\;\Q(\sqrt{10}),\;\Q(\sqrt{15}),\;\Q(\sqrt{30})}.
\]
Their radicands have different square-free prime factorizations, so the fields are distinct.

A degree-four intermediate field corresponds to a subgroup of order $2$, or a one-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb F_2^3$. Each non-zero vector spans exactly one such subspace, giving again $2^3-1=7$ choices. Thus $L$ has precisely seven intermediate fields of degree $4$, as well as seven of degree $2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:x42check}
\idea The field $L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,i)$, with $\alpha=\sqrt[4]{2}$, is the splitting field of $X^4-2$. To identify a fixed field, check what the group does to a generator and then compare degrees. The degree check matters: knowing that a generator is fixed gives only one inclusion.

\writeup Recall the automorphisms
\[
r(\alpha)=i\alpha,\quad r(i)=i,\qquad
s(\alpha)=\alpha,\quad s(i)=-i.
\]
They satisfy $r^4=s^2=e$, $srs=r^{-1}$ and generate the eight-element Galois group $G\cong D_4$.

\emph{Step 1: the five subgroups of order two.}
There are five such subgroups:
\[
\langle r^2\rangle,\quad\langle s\rangle,\quad
\langle r^2s\rangle,\quad\langle rs\rangle,\quad
\langle r^3s\rangle.
\]
We check the indicated fixed generators:
\begin{itemize}
\item $r^2$ sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$ and fixes $i$, so it fixes $\alpha^2=\sqrt2$ and $i$. Its fixed field contains $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$.
\item $s$ fixes $\alpha$, hence fixes $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$.
\item $r^2s$ sends $(\alpha,i)$ to $(-\alpha,-i)$ and therefore fixes $i\alpha$.
\item $rs$ sends $(\alpha,i)$ to $(i\alpha,-i)$ and thus fixes $(1+i)\alpha$, because $(1-i)i\alpha=(1+i)\alpha$.
\item $r^3s$ sends $(\alpha,i)$ to $(-i\alpha,-i)$ and fixes $(1-i)\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
The Galois correspondence tells us that a subgroup of order $2$ has fixed field of degree $8/2=4$ over $\mathbb Q$. Each field just listed has exactly that degree. For $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$, this follows because $\sqrt2$ has degree two and $i\notin\mathbb R$ cannot lie in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. For $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ and $\mathbb Q(i\alpha)$, use the irreducibility of $X^4-2$ by Eisenstein at $2$.

For the remaining two fields, put $\beta_\pm=(1\pm i)\alpha$. Both satisfy
\[
\beta_\pm^4=(1\pm i)^4\alpha^4=-4\cdot2=-8.
\]
To show $X^4+8$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, Gauss's lemma lets us rule out monic integer factors. There is no rational root. If it factored into two monic quadratics, we could write
\[
X^4+8=(X^2+aX+b)(X^2-aX+c),
\qquad a,b,c\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Comparing coefficients gives $a(c-b)=0$, $b+c=a^2$, $bc=8$. If $a=0$, then $c=-b$ and $-b^2=8$, impossible. If $a\ne0$, then $c=b$ and $b^2=8$, also impossible. Hence both $\beta_\pm$ have degree four, so the five inclusions above are equalities.

\emph{Step 2: the three subgroups of order four.}
The subgroups are
\[
\langle r\rangle,\qquad
\langle r^2,s\rangle,\qquad
\langle r^2,rs\rangle.
\]
They fix $i$, $\sqrt2$, and $i\sqrt2$, respectively. For the last claim, $r^2$ fixes $i\alpha^2$, while
\[
rs(i\alpha^2)=(-i)(i\alpha)^2=i\alpha^2.
\]
Each generator has degree two over $\mathbb Q$ (for $i\sqrt2$ the polynomial is $X^2+2$). The fixed field of each order-four subgroup also has degree $8/4=2$. Therefore their fixed fields are, exactly,
\[
\mathbb Q(i),\qquad\mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\qquad\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2}).
\]
Finally the identity subgroup fixes all of $L$, and $G$ fixes $\mathbb Q$. This accounts for every field in the lattice.

\emph{Step 3: which intermediate fields are Galois?}
The Galois correspondence says that an intermediate field $K$ is Galois over $\mathbb Q$ precisely when its corresponding subgroup is normal in $G$. Here $\{e\}$, $G$, $\langle r^2\rangle$, and all three index-two subgroups are normal. The four reflection subgroups are not. Hence the Galois subextensions are exactly
\[
\mathbb Q,\quad\mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\quad\mathbb Q(i),\quad
\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2}),\quad\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i),\quad L.
\]

There is also a direct test for the field $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. The irreducible polynomial $X^4-2$ has the root $\alpha$ in this field, but its root $i\alpha$ is not in $\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subseteq\mathbb R$. The polynomial does not split there. Thus $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]{2})/\mathbb Q$ is not normal, exactly as its non-normal reflection subgroup predicts.

\tools The Galois correspondence, Eisenstein's criterion, Gauss's lemma, and the explicit automorphisms from Example~\ref{ex:galx42}.

\leads The diagram of fields is not a picture of inclusions chosen by hand. Each inclusion is the reverse of an inclusion between subgroups, and normality tells us precisely where a smaller field is itself a splitting field over $\mathbb Q$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:zeta7}
\idea The automorphisms of a prime cyclotomic field are indexed by non-zero residue classes. For $7$, the group has order $6$ and is cyclic, so it has exactly two non-trivial proper subgroups. Find an element fixed by each one, and use its degree to identify the fixed field.

\writeup Let $L=\Q(\zeta)$ with $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:cyclo},
\[
G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)\cong(\Z/7\Z)^*,
\]
which is cyclic of order $6$. Here $\sigma_a(\zeta)=\zeta^a$ and $3$ generates the residue group. A cyclic group of order $6$ has exactly one subgroup of order $2$ and one of order $3$. The fundamental theorem of Galois theory therefore gives exactly two fields strictly between $\Q$ and $L$, of degrees $3$ and $2$ over $\Q$.

\emph{The cubic field.} The subgroup of order $2$ is $\{1,\sigma_6\}$, and $\sigma_6$ is complex conjugation. It fixes
\[
u=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2\cos\frac{2\pi}{7}.
\]
Exercise~\ref{ex:polygons} proved that $u$ has degree $3$ over $\Q$, so it already generates the degree-$3$ fixed field:
\[
L^{\langle\sigma_6\rangle}=\Q\left(\cos\frac{2\pi}{7}\right).
\]

\emph{The quadratic field.} The subgroup of order $3$ is $\{1,\sigma_2,\sigma_4\}$. It cyclically permutes the powers with exponents $1,2,4$, so it fixes
\[
\eta=\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^4.
\]
The other three non-zero exponents are $3,5,6$. Put $\eta'=\zeta^3+\zeta^5+\zeta^6$. Because $1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^6=0$,
\[
\eta+\eta'=-1.
\]
To compute their product, record the nine exponent sums modulo $7$:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
+&3&5&6\\\hline
1&4&6&0\\
2&5&0&1\\
4&0&2&3
\end{array}.
\]
Thus each exponent $1,\ldots,6$ occurs once, while exponent $0$ occurs three times. Therefore
\[
\eta\eta'=(\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^6)+3=-1+3=2.
\]
So $\eta$ and $\eta'$ are the roots of
\[
T^2-(\eta+\eta')T+\eta\eta'=T^2+T+2.
\]
Its discriminant is $1-8=-7$, which is not a square in $\Q$. Hence
\[
\eta=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{-7}}{2},\qquad
\Q(\eta)=\Q(\sqrt{-7}).
\]
Since $\eta$ is fixed by the order-$3$ subgroup and has degree $2$, it generates the whole quadratic fixed field. These two fields exhaust all proper intermediate fields.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:gauss17}
\idea The sixteen non-zero residues modulo $17$ split into quadratic residues and non-residues, each set having eight elements. Add the corresponding roots of unity to obtain two \emph{periods}. Their sum is immediate; their product reduces to counting residue sums.

\writeup Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/17}$. The element $3$ generates $(\Z/17\Z)^*$, so the even powers of $3$ form the subgroup $S$ of quadratic residues and the odd powers form its other coset $T$. Both sets have eight elements. Define
\[
\eta_0=\sum_{a\in S}\zeta^a,\qquad
\eta_1=\sum_{b\in T}\zeta^b.
\]
Together $S$ and $T$ comprise the residues $1,\ldots,16$. Since $\zeta\ne1$ and $\zeta^{17}=1$,
\[
\eta_0+\eta_1=\sum_{c=1}^{16}\zeta^c=-1.
\]

To find their product, expand it without simplification:
\[
\eta_0\eta_1=\sum_{a\in S,\ b\in T}\zeta^{a+b}.
\]
There are $8\cdot8=64$ ordered pairs. None has $a+b\equiv0\pmod{17}$: $-1=3^8$ lies in $S$, so $-a\in S$ for every $a\in S$, whereas $b$ belongs to the disjoint coset $T$.

For each non-zero residue $c$, let $N_c$ be the number of pairs $(a,b)\in S\times T$ with $a+b=c$. Multiplication by any $s\in S$ permutes each of $S$ and $T$, and it maps pairs summing to $c$ bijectively onto pairs summing to $sc$. Thus $N_c$ is constant on $S$, say $N_S$, and constant on $T$, say $N_T$.

There is also a bijection between these two kinds of sums: the map
\[
(a,b)\longmapsto(3b,3a)
\]
still takes $S\times T$ to itself, since $3T=S$ and $3S=T$, but sends a sum $c$ to $3c$, switching $S$ and $T$. Hence $N_S=N_T$.

All $64$ pairs have a non-zero sum, so
\[
8N_S+8N_T=64,
\qquad N_S=N_T=4.
\]
Therefore
\[
\eta_0\eta_1
=\sum_{c=1}^{16}4\zeta^c
=4(-1)=-4.
\]
The periods have sum $-1$ and product $-4$. Thus they are roots of
\[
Z^2-(\eta_0+\eta_1)Z+\eta_0\eta_1
=Z^2+Z-4.
\]
The quadratic formula gives
\[
\boxed{\{\eta_0,\eta_1\}
=\left\{\frac{-1+\sqrt{17}}2,\frac{-1-\sqrt{17}}2\right\}}.
\]
This is the first quadratic step in the construction of the regular $17$-gon.
Why do smaller subgroup sums continue to generate the required fixed fields?
For $H\le(\mathbb Z/17\mathbb Z)^*$, put $\eta_H=\sum_{h\in H}\zeta^h$.
If $\sigma_a(\eta_H)=\eta_H$, the polynomial
$\sum_{h\in H}X^{ah\bmod17}-\sum_{h\in H}X^h$ vanishes at $\zeta$.
Its degree is at most $16$ and its constant coefficient is zero. It must be
zero: a nonzero multiple of the degree-$16$ minimal polynomial
$\Phi_{17}=1+X+\cdots+X^{16}$ cannot have that constant coefficient.
Therefore $aH=H$, or $a\in H$. The stabilizer of $\eta_H$ is exactly $H$,
so the Galois correspondence gives $\mathbb Q(\eta_H)=L^H$.
Successively halving $H$ now gives precisely the quadratic tower; for
$H=\{1,-1\}$, its period is $2\cos(2\pi/17)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sp}
\idea Irreducibility of prime degree forces a $p$-cycle into the Galois group. Exactly one non-real conjugate pair makes complex conjugation a transposition. A prime cycle together with an arbitrary transposition generates the full symmetric group; the word \emph{prime} matters.

\writeup Let $f\in\Q[X]$ be irreducible of prime degree $p$, and assume it has exactly two non-real roots. Since $\Q$ has characteristic $0$, irreducible polynomials are separable: all $p$ complex roots of $f$ are distinct.

Let $L$ be the splitting field of $f$ and $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\Q)$. Every element of $G$ permutes the $p$ roots, and the action is faithful because the roots generate $L$. So $G$ embeds in $\mathfrak S_p$.

For any root $\alpha$, irreducibility gives $[\Q(\alpha):\Q]=p$. The tower law shows
\[
p\mid[L:\Q]=|G|.
\]
Cauchy's theorem supplies an element $c\in G$ of order $p$. An element of $\mathfrak S_p$ of order $p$ must be a $p$-cycle, so $c$ visits every root.

The polynomial has rational, hence real, coefficients. Complex conjugation therefore preserves its root set and the field $L$, fixes every real root, and exchanges the two non-real roots. Its restriction $\tau\in G$ is precisely one transposition $(a\,b)$.

Since $c$ visits every root, some power $c^k$ sends $a$ to $b$. Choose $1\le k<p$; primality ensures that $c^k$ is still a $p$-cycle. Label the points along its orbit as $1,2,\ldots,p$, starting at $a$. Then
\[
c^k=(1\,2\,\dots\,p),\qquad \tau=(1\,2).
\]
Conjugating $(1\,2)$ by the powers of this cycle yields every adjacent transposition $(j\,j+1)$ for $1\le j<p$. These generate $\mathfrak S_p$. Consequently
\[
\boxed{G=\mathfrak S_p}.
\]

For the specific polynomial $g(X)=X^5-6X+3$, Eisenstein's criterion at $3$ proves irreducibility: each non-leading coefficient is divisible by $3$, while the constant term $3$ is not divisible by $9$. Direct evaluation gives
\[
g(-2)=-17,\quad g(0)=3,\quad
g(1)=-2,\quad g(2)=23.
\]
The three changes of sign produce roots in $(-2,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,2)$. Its derivative $g'(X)=5X^4-6$ has exactly two real zeros, so Rolle's theorem rules out a fourth distinct real root. Since $g$ is irreducible in characteristic zero, all five roots are simple; the remaining two therefore form exactly one non-real conjugate pair.

The first part now gives $\operatorname{Gal}(g/\Q)=\mathfrak S_5$. This group is not solvable, because it contains the non-abelian simple normal subgroup $A_5$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:radsolv}, $g$ is not solvable by radicals.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:discr}
\idea The product of differences of the roots remembers the \emph{parity} of a root permutation. Its square is fixed by every Galois automorphism, hence belongs to the coefficient field by the fixed-field theorem. This links the square class of the discriminant to the Galois group.

\writeup Let $f\in K[X]$ be a separable cubic with roots $r_1,r_2,r_3$ in its splitting field $L$, and put
\[
\delta=(r_1-r_2)(r_1-r_3)(r_2-r_3).
\]
A transposition of two roots changes the sign of $\delta$. Since every permutation is a product of transpositions, for $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\le\mathfrak S_3$ we have
\[
\sigma(\delta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\delta.
\]
Hence $\sigma(\delta^2)=\delta^2$ for every $\sigma$, so $\delta^2$ lies in the fixed field $L^G=K$. Because the roots are distinct, $\delta\ne0$. Thus
\[
\boxed{G\subseteq A_3\iff\sigma(\delta)=\delta\ \forall\sigma\in G
\iff\delta\in K}.
\]
Here the last equivalence uses the fixed-field theorem. Our characteristic-zero assumption ensures that $2\ne0$, so an odd permutation cannot fix the nonzero $\delta$.

For the depressed cubic $X^3+pX+q$ the discriminant identity gives
\[
\delta^2=-4p^3-27q^2.
\]
Consider $f_1=X^3-2$. It is irreducible over $\Q$ by Eisenstein at $2$, and
\[
\delta^2=-27(-2)^2=-108<0.
\]
A rational square cannot be negative, so $\delta\notin\Q$ and $G$ contains an odd permutation. Irreducibility makes the action on the three roots transitive; the group order is a multiple of $3$. The only transitive subgroup of $\mathfrak S_3$ containing an odd permutation is all of $\mathfrak S_3$, therefore
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(X^3-2/\Q)\cong\mathfrak S_3}.
\]

Now take $f_2=X^3-3X+1$. The rational-root test rules out roots $\pm1$, so the cubic is irreducible over $\Q$. Its discriminant is
\[
-4(-3)^3-27(1)^2=108-27=81=9^2.
\]
Thus $\delta=\pm9\in\Q$ and the Galois group lies in $A_3$. Again irreducibility makes its order divisible by $3$, so it must be $A_3$ itself:
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(X^3-3X+1/\Q)\cong A_3\cong\Z/3\Z}.
\]
The difference between the two cubics is not their degree or irreducibility; it is the square class of their discriminants.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:cardano}
\idea Lagrange's resolvents replace three roots by two expressions whose cubes are easier to understand. Multiply the resolvents, and then use the factorisation of a sum of cubes. The two cubes satisfy a quadratic equation; taking cube roots finally recovers the original root.

\writeup Let $r_1,r_2,r_3$ be the roots of $X^3+pX+q$ in a splitting field containing a primitive cube root of unity $\omega$, where
\[
1+\omega+\omega^2=0,\qquad \omega^3=1.
\]
Viète's formulas give
\[
r_1+r_2+r_3=0,\quad
r_1r_2+r_1r_3+r_2r_3=p,\quad
r_1r_2r_3=-q.
\]
Define
\[
u=r_1+\omega r_2+\omega^2r_3,\qquad
v=r_1+\omega^2r_2+\omega r_3.
\]
Expanding $uv$ and collecting the diagonal and mixed terms yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
uv
&=r_1^2+r_2^2+r_3^2
  +(\omega+\omega^2)(r_1r_2+r_1r_3+r_2r_3)\\
&=(r_1+r_2+r_3)^2-3p=-3p.
\end{aligned}
\]

Next use the algebraic identity
\[
u^3+v^3=(u+v)(u+\omega v)(u+\omega^2v).
\]
The root-sum relation turns these factors into
\[
u+v=3r_1,\qquad
u+\omega v=3\omega^2r_3,\qquad
u+\omega^2v=3\omega r_2.
\]
Thus
\[
u^3+v^3=27\omega^3r_1r_2r_3=-27q.
\]

Set $U=u^3$ and $V=v^3$. We have $U+V=-27q$ and
\[
UV=(uv)^3=(-3p)^3=-27p^3.
\]
Therefore $U,V$ are the two roots of
\[
Z^2+27qZ-27p^3=0.
\]
The quadratic formula gives
\[
\frac{U}{27},\ \frac{V}{27}
=-\frac q2\pm\sqrt{\frac{q^2}{4}+\frac{p^3}{27}}.
\]
Finally $u+v=3r_1$, so
\[
r_1=\frac u3+\frac v3.
\]
The two terms $u/3$ and $v/3$ are cube roots of $U/27$ and $V/27$. Their product is fixed:
\[
\frac{uv}{9}=-\frac p3.
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{
r_1=
\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2+\sqrt{\frac{q^2}{4}+\frac{p^3}{27}}}
+\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2-\sqrt{\frac{q^2}{4}+\frac{p^3}{27}}}
}
\]
\emph{provided} the cube-root branches are chosen so their product is $-p/3$. Picking two unrelated cube roots may give the wrong result. This branch condition is part of Cardano's formula, not a cosmetic detail.
\end{solution}


\begin{solution}{pb:kronecker}
\idea The coefficients of a monic polynomial are symmetric sums of its roots. If all roots stay inside the unit disc, those coefficients cannot become large. Apply this observation not just to the roots but to all their powers.

\writeup \emph{(a) Kronecker's theorem.}
Let $P\in\mathbb Z[X]$ be monic of degree $d$, with $P(0)\ne0$, and list its complex roots with multiplicity as $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d$. They are all non-zero and satisfy $|\alpha_i|\le1$.

For each positive integer $r$, form
\[
P_r(X)=\prod_{i=1}^d(X-\alpha_i^r).
\]
If $d=0$, then $P=1$ and there are no roots to consider; assume $d\ge1$.
First we need to know that $P_r$ has integer coefficients, not merely algebraic ones. Let $C$ be the integer companion matrix of $P$. Its eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity, are exactly the $\alpha_i$. Chapter~7's Jordan form makes $C$ upper triangular after a change of basis, with those diagonal entries. Its $r$th power is upper triangular with diagonal entries $\alpha_i^r$. Thus the eigenvalues of $C^r$ are those powers, again with multiplicity, even when $P$ has repeated roots. The characteristic polynomial of $C^r$ is thus $P_r$, and it is monic with integer coefficients because $C^r$ is an integer matrix.

Now consider the coefficient of $X^{d-j}$ in $P_r$. By Viète's formulas it is, up to sign, the sum of $\binom dj$ products of $j$ roots $\alpha_i^r$. Every such product has modulus at most $1$, so the triangle inequality gives
\[
\left|[X^{d-j}]P_r\right|\le\binom dj.
\]
These coefficients are integers. Therefore, for each $j$, there are only finitely many possible values, independent of $r$. As $d$ is fixed, there are only finitely many distinct polynomials $P_r$.

Every power $\alpha_i^r$ is a root of one of these finitely many degree-$d$ polynomials. Their combined root sets are finite. Thus for each fixed $i$, the infinite list $\alpha_i,\alpha_i^2,\alpha_i^3,\ldots$ contains a repetition: $\alpha_i^u=\alpha_i^v$ for some $1\le u<v$. Since $\alpha_i\ne0$,
\[
\alpha_i^{v-u}=1.
\]
Every root of $P$ is a root of unity.

If $P$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, choose one root $\alpha$ of exact order $m$. Its monic minimal polynomial is $\Phi_m$, proved irreducible in the worked Exercise~\ref{ex:phin} earlier in this chapter. Since $P$ is also its monic minimal polynomial, $P=\Phi_m$.

\emph{(b) A polynomial whose roots lie in $[-2,2]$.}
Let $P$ be monic with integer coefficients and all roots real in this interval. Put $d=\deg P$ and define
\[
Q(Z)=Z^dP(Z+Z^{-1}).
\]
Although this expression initially looks like a Laurent polynomial, multiplying by $Z^d$ clears every negative power. The result belongs to $\mathbb Z[Z]$ and has leading coefficient $1$: the term $(Z+Z^{-1})^d$ contributes $Z^{2d}$, while the lower powers contribute strictly lower degrees. Its constant term is also $1$.

For each root $x$ of $P$, the two solutions of
\[
Z+Z^{-1}=x,\quad\text{or}\quad Z^2-xZ+1=0,
\]
lie on the unit circle: writing $x=2\cos\theta$ with $0\le\theta\le\pi$, they are $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$. Thus all roots of $Q$ have modulus $1$, and $Q(0)=1\ne0$. By part (a) every root of $Q$ is a root of unity. Hence every root of $P$ has the form
\[
x=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2\cos(2\pi r)
\]
for some rational $r$.

\emph{(c) Why the monic hypothesis matters.}
Let $z=(3+4i)/5$. It has $|z|=1$, and its complex conjugate $\bar z=(3-4i)/5$ does too. But
\[
z+\bar z=\frac65.
\]
Suppose $z^N=1$ for some $N\ge1$. The primitive polynomial
$g=5X^2-6X+5$ vanishes at $z$. Division of $X^N-1$ by $g$
over $\mathbb Q$ leaves a remainder of degree at most one. It vanishes
at the nonreal $z$, so its rational coefficients must both vanish.
Thus $g$ divides $X^N-1$ in $\mathbb Q[X]$. By the divisibility form
of Gauss's lemma (Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussirr}), it divides it in
$\mathbb Z[X]$. Comparing leading coefficients would give $5c=1$
for an integer $c$, impossible. Hence $z$ is not a root of unity.

Indeed the polynomial of $z$ over $\mathbb Q$ is
\[
X^2-\frac65X+1,
\]
or $5X^2-6X+5$ after clearing denominators. It is not monic with integer coefficients. That is exactly the missing assumption in (a).

\tools Integer matrices and their characteristic polynomials, Viète's formulas, Gauss's divisibility lemma, and cyclotomic irreducibility (Exercise~\ref{ex:phin}).

\leads Roots staying in a bounded region are usually not enough to force periodicity. Integrality makes the set of possible power polynomials finite, and that changes the conclusion completely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:rattan}
\idea Rational angles lead to roots of unity. The key is to ask which roots of unity can live in a given cyclotomic field. Once we know that, rational slopes and lattice polygons become questions about the small field $\mathbb Q(i)$.

\writeup \emph{(a) Roots of unity in a cyclotomic field.}
Set $K=\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$ and suppose that $K$ contains a primitive $m$-th root $\zeta_m$. Let $L=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ and $g=\gcd(m,n)$. Choose integers $u,v$ with $um+vn=g$. Then
\[
\zeta_n^u\zeta_m^v
=e^{2\pi i(um+vn)/(mn)}
=e^{2\pi i/L}=\zeta_L.
\]
Thus $\mathbb Q(\zeta_L)\subseteq K$, and the reverse inclusion also holds because $n\mid L$. Therefore these two fields are equal:
\[
\varphi(L)=\varphi(n).
\]
As $L=nt$ for some positive integer $t$, the product formula for $\varphi$ gives
\[
\frac{\varphi(nt)}{\varphi(n)}
=t\prod_{\substack{\ell\mid t\\\ell\text{ prime},\,\ell\nmid n}}
\left(1-\frac1\ell\right).
\]
This is at least $\varphi(t)$, because every prime factor omitted from the product would only make the product larger. Since the ratio equals $1$, we must have $\varphi(t)\le1$, forcing $t=1$ or $t=2$: for $t\ge3$, the distinct units $1$ and $t-1$ already give $\varphi(t)\ge2$.

If $t=2$, the displayed ratio is $1$ only when $2\nmid n$: then the extra prime factor $2$ contributes $2(1-\frac12)=1$. If $2\mid n$, it contributes the factor $2$ without cancellation. Hence $L=n$ for even $n$, and $L\mid2n$ for odd $n$. Since $m\mid L$, any root of unity in $K$ has order dividing $n$ (if $n$ is even) or $2n$ (if $n$ is odd).

Conversely, $K$ contains every $n$-th root of unity. For odd $n$ it also contains the primitive $2n$-th root $-\zeta_n^{(n+1)/2}$ (whose square is $\zeta_n$), and therefore every $2n$-th root. This proves the exact description.

\emph{(b) Rational tangents.}
Suppose $\theta/\pi\in\mathbb Q$ and $t=\tan\theta\in\mathbb Q$. The tangent is finite, so $\cos\theta\ne0$. The double-angle identity gives
\[
e^{2i\theta}=\frac{1+it}{1-it}\in\mathbb Q(i).
\]
Because $\theta$ is a rational multiple of $\pi$, this number is a root of unity. Apply (a) with $n=4$: the only roots of unity in $\mathbb Q(i)$ are $\pm1,\pm i$. Hence $2\theta$ is a multiple of $\pi/2$, or $\theta$ a multiple of $\pi/4$. Its finite tangent is consequently $0$, $1$, or $-1$.

\emph{(c) Regular polygons in the integer lattice.}
Identify the plane with $\mathbb C$. Let consecutive vertices $A,B,C$ of a regular $n$-gon ($n\ge3$) lie in $\mathbb Z[i]$. The directed edges $B-A$ and $C-B$ differ by a rotation through $\pm2\pi/n$. Their quotient is therefore
\[
\frac{C-B}{B-A}=e^{\pm2\pi i/n}.
\]
The left side lies in $\mathbb Q(i)$, while the right side is a primitive $n$-th root of unity. Part (a) forces $n\mid4$. Since $n\ge3$, it follows that $n=4$. The vertices $0,1,1+i,i$ show that a lattice square really exists.

\emph{(d) Rational cosine values.}
Let $\theta/\pi\in\mathbb Q$ and suppose $\cos\theta\in\mathbb Q$.
Define integer polynomials by
\[
S_0=2,\qquad S_1=X,\qquad S_{j+1}=XS_j-S_{j-1}.
\]
Induction shows that $S_j$ is monic of degree $j$ for $j\ge1$.
The cosine addition identity gives
\[
S_j(2\cos\theta)=2\cos(j\theta)
\]
by the same recurrence, starting with $j=0,1$.
Choose an integer $m\ge1$ with $m\theta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$.
Then the rational number $t=2\cos\theta$ is a root of the monic
integer polynomial $S_m(X)-2$. Write $t=a/b$ in lowest terms, $b>0$.
Multiplying its equation by $b^m$ makes $b$ divide $a^m$, so coprimality
forces $b=1$. Thus $t$ is an integer. Since $-2\le t\le2$,
\[
\boxed{\cos\theta\in\{0,\pm\tfrac12,\pm1\}.}
\]

\tools Cyclotomic irreducibility and degrees, Euler's totient function, the cosine recurrence, and the rational-root argument.

\leads Regular polygons and rational trigonometric values seem geometric, but here their restrictions come from the arithmetic of roots of unity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ramacube}
\idea Taking cube roots usually destroys the simple polynomial relations between three numbers. Here their original values are related by a cubic equation. If we call the cube roots $a,b,c$, symmetric identities for $a,b,c$ let us recover their sum without finding the three cube roots separately.

\writeup Put
\[
x=\cos\frac{2\pi}7,\quad
y=\cos\frac{4\pi}7,\quad
z=\cos\frac{8\pi}7.
\]
To find their polynomial, let $\zeta=\zeta_7$ and set $u=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$. Divide $1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^6=0$ by $\zeta^3$ and use
\[
\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2}=u^2-2,\qquad
\zeta^3+\zeta^{-3}=u^3-3u.
\]
This gives $u^3+u^2-2u-1=0$. Replacing $\zeta$ by $\zeta^2$ and $\zeta^4$ gives the other two distinct values $2y,2z$, while $u=2x$. Hence $2x,2y,2z$ are exactly the three roots of this monic cubic. Equivalently, $x,y,z$ are the roots of
\[
8X^3+4X^2-4X-1.
\]
Viète's formulas therefore give
\[
x+y+z=-\frac12,\qquad
xy+yz+zx=-\frac12,\qquad xyz=\frac18.
\]

Let $a=\sqrt[3]x$, $b=\sqrt[3]y$, $c=\sqrt[3]z$, using the \emph{real} cube root each time, and write
\[
s=a+b+c,\qquad t=ab+bc+ca.
\]
Because $abc$ is the real cube root of $xyz=1/8$, we have $abc=1/2$.

The basic symmetric identity
\[
(u+v+w)^3=u^3+v^3+w^3
 +3(u+v+w)(uv+vw+wu)-3uvw
\]
gives, when applied to $a,b,c$,
\[
s^3=(x+y+z)+3st-3abc
=-\frac12+3st-\frac32=3st-2.
\]
Apply the same identity to $ab,bc,ca$. Their sum is $t$, their pairwise products have sum $abc(a+b+c)=s/2$, and their total product is $(abc)^2=1/4$. So
\[
t^3=(xy+yz+zx)+3t\frac{s}{2}-\frac34
=\frac32st-\frac54.
\]

The two equations involve only $s$, $t$, and their product. Set $m=st$. Multiply them:
\[
m^3=s^3t^3=(3m-2)\left(\frac32m-\frac54\right).
\]
Expand and clear the denominator:
\[
4m^3-18m^2+27m-10=0.
\]
The square term is inconvenient, so put $m=u+\frac32$. Direct expansion produces
\[
4u^3+\frac72=0,\qquad u^3=-\frac78.
\]
Since $m$ is real, $u$ is the \emph{real} cube root $-\sqrt[3]7/2$. Therefore
\[
m=\frac{3-\sqrt[3]7}{2}.
\]
Return to the earlier relation $s^3=3m-2$:
\[
s^3=\frac{9-3\sqrt[3]7}{2}-2
=\frac{5-3\sqrt[3]7}{2}.
\]
Finally $s$ is real, and a real number has exactly one real cube root. Hence
\[
\boxed{\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{2\pi}7}
+\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{4\pi}7}
+\sqrt[3]{\cos\tfrac{8\pi}7}
=\sqrt[3]{\frac{5-3\sqrt[3]7}{2}}.}
\]

\tools Viète's formulas, the finite geometric sum for seventh roots of unity, and the symmetric cube identity expanded above.

\leads The useful move was to introduce the two symmetric sums $s,t$ rather than attack three cube roots one by one. Their product satisfied a cubic whose shift became a pure cube.
\end{solution}
