\chapter{Representations and Characters}

\begin{center}\itshape
The group $\mathfrak S_4$ has $24$ elements, and $24=1+1+4+9+9$.\\
The group $A_5$ has $60$ elements, and $60=1+9+9+16+25$.\\
Why should the order of a group be a sum of squares?
\end{center}

The symmetric group on three letters has six elements. Yet
\[
6=1^2+1^2+2^2.
\]
Those squares are not chosen to make the arithmetic work. They are the dimensions of three irreducible complex representations of $\mathfrak S_3$. For the cube's rotation group $\mathfrak S_4$, the same rule becomes
\[
24=1^2+1^2+2^2+3^2+3^2.
\]

A \emph{representation} lets a group act by invertible matrices. Matrices are easier to compute with than an abstract multiplication law: we can add them, take traces, and use linear algebra. A \emph{character} records the trace of each matrix. At first, throwing away almost every entry seems like a terrible loss of information. The surprise is that characters retain enough information to classify irreducible complex representations of a finite group.

The reason is an averaging trick. Average a linear map over a finite group and it begins to respect the group action. That small move leads to Maschke's theorem, Schur's lemma, and the orthogonality relations for characters.

In the abelian case the picture becomes especially simple. Every irreducible complex representation is one-dimensional, so a character is just a homomorphism into the unit circle. The corresponding change of basis is the finite Fourier transform. You have already seen its roots of unity in earlier volumes; now they appear as the coordinates naturally chosen by symmetry.

We will compute tables for $\mathfrak S_4$ and $A_5$, then see how the abelian case reconnects with modular arithmetic and, eventually, the analysis of random walks.

Throughout this chapter, $G$ is finite and representations are finite-dimensional
over $\C$, unless another field is explicitly specified.

\section{Representations}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{representation} of $G$ is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, where $V$ is a complex vector space of dimension $d\ge0$, the \emph{dimension} (or degree) of $\rho$; we also say that $V$ is a representation, and write $gv$ for $\rho(g)v$. A subspace $W$ is \emph{stable} if $gW\subseteq W$ for all $g$; then $gW=W$, since $g^{-1}$ also acts, and $W$ is a \emph{subrepresentation}. The zero space is allowed as a representation and has character zero. We call $V$ \emph{irreducible} when $V\ne0$ and its only stable subspaces are $0$ and $V$. A linear map $f:V\to V'$ between representations is an \emph{intertwiner} if $f(gv)=gf(v)$ for all $g,v$; an invertible one is an \emph{isomorphism}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:reps}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item The \emph{trivial} representation: $V=\C$, $\rho(g)=1$. The \emph{sign} representation of $\mathfrak S_n$: $\rho(\sigma)=\varepsilon(\sigma)\in\{\pm1\}=GL_1(\C)\cap\R$.
\item Every one-dimensional representation is a homomorphism $\chi:G\to\C^*$, and is irreducible. For $G=\Z/n\Z$, $\chi_j(k)=e^{2i\pi jk/n}$, $0\le j<n$.
\item For $n\ge1$, the \emph{permutation representation} of $\mathfrak S_n$ on $\C^n$: $\sigma e_i=e_{\sigma(i)}$. The line $\C(e_1+\dots+e_n)$ and the hyperplane $\{\sum x_i=0\}$ are stable.
\item More generally, if $G$ acts on a finite set $X$ (Chapter~3), $G$ acts on the space $\C^X$ with basis $(e_x)_{x\in X}$ by $ge_x=e_{gx}$. With $X=G$ acting on itself by left multiplication, this is the \emph{regular representation} $\C[G]$, of dimension $|G|$.
\item The symmetries of an equilateral triangle centred at $0$ give a homomorphism $\mathfrak S_3\cong D_3\to GL_2(\R)\subseteq GL_2(\C)$. It is irreducible: a stable line would be a common eigenline of the rotation by $120^\circ$, which has no real eigenvector, and of a reflection; over $\C$ the rotation's eigenlines are spanned by $(1,\mp i)$, and the reflection in the $x$-axis swaps them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{think}
$\mathfrak S_3$ has order $6$, and the trivial and sign representations have dimension $1$. If $6$ is a sum of the squares of the dimensions of all irreducible representations, what is missing? Which representation above has that dimension?
\end{think}

\paragraph{Complex lengths and perpendiculars.}
Volume~1 used real inner products. Over $\C$ we use a \emph{Hermitian inner
product}: it is linear in the first variable, conjugate-linear in the second,
obeys $\langle v,w\rangle=\overline{\langle w,v\rangle}$, and has
$\langle v,v\rangle>0$ for $v\ne0$. In any basis,
$\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_i v_i\overline{w_i}$ is an example. Put
$\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$ and say $v\perp w$ when their product is zero.

We need the complex version of orthogonal projection, so check it directly.
Given an independent family $v_1,\ldots,v_d$, set successively
\[
w_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle e_j,
\qquad e_k=w_k/\|w_k\|.
\]
Induction gives $\langle w_k,e_j\rangle=0$ for $j<k$.
The vector $w_k$ is nonzero because $v_k$ is not in the span of its
predecessors, so its positive norm can be divided out. This proves the
orthonormal-basis construction over $\C$. Start with a basis of a subspace
$W$ and extend it to a basis of $V$. The construction makes the remaining
orthonormal vectors a basis of
$W^\perp=\{v:\langle v,w\rangle=0\text{ for all }w\in W\}$.
Thus $V=W\oplus W^\perp$. Equivalently the projection is
$P_Wv=\sum_{j\le\dim W}\langle v,e_j\rangle e_j$; the remaining coordinates
are exactly the perpendicular part. Empty bases cover $W=0$ or $V=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Maschke]\label{thm:maschke}
For finite $G$, every complex representation $V$ has a $G$-invariant Hermitian inner product: $\langle gv,gw\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle$. Consequently every stable subspace $W$ has a stable complement $W^\perp$, and $V$ is a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Start with an arbitrary Hermitian inner product $(v,w)$ on the complex vector space $V$. It may not be preserved by the representation. We make an invariant one by averaging over the group:
\[
\langle v,w\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{h\in G}(hv,hw).
\]
This is again Hermitian. It is positive definite because, for $v\ne0$, every term $(hv,hv)$ is positive: $h$ acts invertibly, so $hv\ne0$. Thus it is an inner product.

For any $g\in G$, replace $h$ by $hg$ in the sum. Right multiplication permutes the elements of $G$, so
\[
\langle gv,gw\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{h\in G}(hgv,hgw)
=\langle v,w\rangle.
\]
The new inner product is invariant under $G$.

Let $W\subseteq V$ be an invariant subspace. We claim its orthogonal complement is invariant too. Take $v\in W^\perp$ and $w\in W$. Using invariance of the inner product,
\[
\langle gv,w\rangle=\langle v,g^{-1}w\rangle.
\]
Because $W$ is invariant, $g^{-1}w\in W$, so the right side is $0$. This holds for every $w\in W$, hence $gv\in W^\perp$.

We have obtained a direct sum $V=W\oplus W^\perp$ of invariant subspaces. Discard zero summands. Any nonzero reducible summand splits into two nonzero proper subspaces; repeat inside them. The positive integer dimensions decrease, so the process terminates in irreducibles. For $V=0$, the direct sum is empty.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
The obstacle in decomposing a representation is that an arbitrary linear complement to an invariant subspace need not be invariant. Start with any projection onto the invariant subspace and average its conjugates by the finite group. Every conjugate has image in the same invariant subspace and acts as the identity there, so the average does too and its square equals itself. It is therefore still a projection onto that subspace, and now it commutes with every group element. Its kernel is consequently invariant. The division by $|G|$ is the essential hypothesis: it works over $\C$ but can fail in a field whose characteristic divides $|G|$.
For example, over a field of characteristic $p$, the map
$T(a,b)=(a+b,b)$ satisfies $T^p=I$ and gives a representation of the cyclic
group of order $p$. The line spanned by $(1,0)$ is stable. Any complementary
line has a generator $(a,1)$, whose image is $(a+1,1)$; stability would force
this to equal $(a,1)$, impossible. Thus a stable complement need not exist
in that characteristic.

A map preserving this Hermitian product is called \emph{unitary}.
Each $\rho(g)$ is unitary and satisfies $\rho(g)^n=I$ for $n=|G|$.
Its minimal polynomial divides $X^n-1$, which splits into distinct linear
factors over $\C$, so Chapter~7's diagonalization criterion gives an eigenbasis.
Every eigenvalue is an $n$-th root of unity. Moreover, eigenvectors $v,w$
with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda,\mu$ satisfy
\[
\langle v,w\rangle=\langle\rho(g)v,\rho(g)w\rangle
=\lambda\overline\mu\langle v,w\rangle.
\]
Since $|\lambda|=|\mu|=1$ and $\lambda\ne\mu$, the coefficient
$\lambda\overline\mu$ is not $1$, forcing $\langle v,w\rangle=0$.
Orthonormalize within each eigenspace by the construction above: together
these bases give an orthonormal eigenbasis. This earns the precise complex
version needed here.

\begin{lemma}[Schur]\label{lem:schur}
Let $V,V'$ be irreducible and $f:V\to V'$ an intertwiner. Then $f=0$ or $f$ is an isomorphism. If $V=V'$, then $f=\lambda\,\mathrm{id}$ for some $\lambda\in\C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
An \emph{intertwiner} is a linear map
$f:V\to V'$ satisfying
$f(\rho(g)v)=\rho'(g)f(v)$
for every $g\in G$ and $v\in V$.

Its kernel is $G$-stable: if $f(v)=0$,
then $f(\rho(g)v)=\rho'(g)f(v)=0$.
Its image is also $G$-stable:
if $w=f(v)$ then
$\rho'(g)w=f(\rho(g)v)$ is still
in the image.

Irreducibility leaves just two
stable subspaces, $0$ and the whole
representation. Thus $\ker f$
is $0$ or $V$, and $\operatorname{im}f$
is $0$ or $V'$. If $f\ne0$, its
image is non-zero and its kernel
cannot equal $V$.
Consequently $\ker f=0$ and
$\operatorname{im}f=V'$, making $f$
an isomorphism.

Now suppose $V'=V$.
A complex linear operator on a
non-zero finite-dimensional complex
space has an eigenvalue $\lambda$.
The operator $f-\lambda I$ is still
an intertwiner, since both $f$ and
$I$ commute with every $\rho(g)$.
It has a non-zero eigenvector in its
kernel and hence is not injective.
The first part forces
$f-\lambda I=0$, that is,
\[
\boxed{f=\lambda I}.
\]
Working over $\C$ matters here:
over other fields a linear operator
need not have an eigenvalue.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:abelianirr}
If $G$ is abelian, every irreducible representation has dimension $1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ be an
irreducible complex representation
of an abelian group $G$.
Fix any $h\in G$. Since $hg=gh$
for every $g\in G$,
\[
\rho(h)\rho(g)=\rho(g)\rho(h).
\]
Thus $\rho(h):V\to V$ is an
intertwiner from the representation
to itself. Schur's lemma
(Lemma~\ref{lem:schur}) makes it
a scalar: $\rho(h)=\lambda_hI$.

Every one-dimensional subspace
of $V$ is preserved by every
scalar operator, hence by every
element of $G$. If $\dim V>1$,
a line would be a non-zero proper
$G$-stable subspace, contradicting
irreducibility. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\dim V=1}.
\]
The converse is automatic: every
one-dimensional representation is
irreducible.
\end{proof}

A representation stores a matrix for each group element. Taking traces reduces each matrix to one number, and those numbers stay constant on conjugacy classes. Orthogonality will show why this surprisingly short record can distinguish irreducible representations.

\section{Characters and orthogonality}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{character} of a representation $\rho$ is $\chi_\rho(g)=\operatorname{tr}\rho(g)$. A \emph{class function} is a function $G\to\C$ constant on conjugacy classes; they form a vector space of dimension the number of classes, with the inner product
\[
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\varphi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:charbasic}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\chi(e)=\dim V$; $\chi$ is a class function; isomorphic representations have the same character.
\item $\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}$, and $|\chi(g)|\le\chi(e)$.
\item $\chi_{V\oplus W}=\chi_V+\chi_W$.
\item For a permutation representation $\C^X$, $\chi(g)$ is the number of fixed points of $g$ on $X$. In particular the character of $\C[G]$ is $|G|$ at $e$ and $0$ elsewhere.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ and
$\chi(g)=\operatorname{tr}\rho(g)$.

\emph{(a) Degree and conjugacy.}
Since $\rho(e)=I$, we have
$\chi(e)=\operatorname{tr}I=\dim V$.
For $g,h\in G$,
\[
\rho(hgh^{-1})=
\rho(h)\rho(g)\rho(h)^{-1}.
\]
Trace is invariant under similarity,
so $\chi(hgh^{-1})=\chi(g)$:
characters are class functions.
If two representations are isomorphic,
the intertwining isomorphism conjugates
their matrices, so their traces agree.

\emph{(b) Inverses and bounds.}
Every $\rho(g)$ has finite order,
since $G$ is finite. Its eigenvalues
are therefore roots of unity, of
absolute value one, and its minimal
polynomial divides the separable
polynomial $X^{o(g)}-1$.
It is diagonalisable over $\C$.
If its eigenvalues are
$\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$,
those of $\rho(g^{-1})$ are
$\lambda_j^{-1}=\overline{\lambda_j}$.
Thus
\[
\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)},
\qquad
|\chi(g)|=\left|\sum_j\lambda_j\right|
\le\sum_j|\lambda_j|=d=\chi(e).
\]

\emph{(c) Direct sums.}
In a basis adapted to $V\oplus W$,
the representation matrix is block
diagonal. Its trace is the sum of
the traces of the blocks, so
$\chi_{V\oplus W}=\chi_V+\chi_W$.

\emph{(d) Permutation characters.}
In the basis $(e_x)_{x\in X}$,
the matrix of $g$ sends $e_x$
to $e_{g\cdot x}$. Its diagonal
entry at $x$ equals $1$ precisely
when $g\cdot x=x$; otherwise it is
$0$. The trace is therefore the
number of fixed points on $X$.
For the left-regular action on $G$,
the identity fixes all $|G|$ points,
whereas $g\ne e$ fixes none, because
$gx=x$ forces $g=e$.
This gives the stated regular
character.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[orthogonality of characters]\label{thm:orth}
Let $\chi,\chi'$ be the characters of irreducible representations $V,V'$. Then $\langle\chi,\chi'\rangle=1$ if $V\cong V'$, and $0$ otherwise.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For any linear map $u:V\to V'$, average it
over the group:
\[
\widetilde u=\frac1{|G|}
\sum_{g\in G}\rho'(g)u\rho(g)^{-1}.
\]
For every $h\in G$, the substitution
$g\mapsto hg$ in the sum gives
\[
\rho'(h)\widetilde u\rho(h)^{-1}=\widetilde u.
\]
Thus $\widetilde u$ is an intertwiner.
Schur's lemma tells us exactly what
it can be.

Choose bases of $V,V'$.
Let $E_{jk}:V\to V'$ send the $k$-th
basis vector of $V$ to the $j$-th
basis vector of $V'$, and all other
basis vectors to zero.
The $(i,\ell)$ matrix entry of its
average is
\[
(\widetilde E_{jk})_{i\ell}
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}
\rho'(g)_{ij}\rho(g^{-1})_{k\ell}.
\]

If $V$ and $V'$ are not isomorphic,
Schur's lemma gives
$\widetilde E_{jk}=0$.
Set $i=j$ and $k=\ell$, then sum
over both indices. This yields
\[
\frac1{|G|}\sum_g
\chi'(g)\chi(g^{-1})=0,
\]
which is $\langle\chi',\chi\rangle=0$
because $\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}$.

If $V\cong V'$, identify them through
an intertwining isomorphism, so we
may take $\rho'=\rho$ in one basis.
Schur's lemma makes
$\widetilde u=\lambda I$. Taking traces,
\[
d\lambda=\operatorname{tr}\widetilde u
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g
\operatorname{tr}(\rho(g)u\rho(g)^{-1})
=\operatorname{tr}u,
\]
where $d=\dim V$.
So $\widetilde u=(\operatorname{tr}u/d)I$.
For $u=E_{jk}$ the matrix entries give
\[
\frac1{|G|}\sum_g
\rho(g)_{ij}\rho(g^{-1})_{k\ell}
=\frac{\delta_{jk}\delta_{i\ell}}d.
\]
Again set $i=j$ and $k=\ell$ and sum:
\[
\langle\chi,\chi\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g|\chi(g)|^2
=\frac1d\sum_{j,k}\delta_{jk}=1.
\]
Combining the two cases proves
the orthonormality of irreducible
characters. Averaging converted
an arbitrary linear map into an
intertwiner, and Schur's lemma
converted that into a scalar.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Trace is invariant under a change of basis, but that alone does not explain the strong orthogonality formula. The averaging operation takes an arbitrary linear map $V\to V'$ and turns it into one that respects the group action. Schur's lemma then says that the average is zero between different irreducible representations, and a scalar multiple of the identity when they are the same. Summing the diagonal matrix coefficients converts that statement into an inner product of character values. This is why a short list of traces can recognise a whole irreducible representation.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mult}
Let $V_1,\dots,V_r$ be pairwise non-isomorphic irreducible representations with characters $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_r$, and $V$ any representation.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item In any decomposition of $V$ into irreducibles, the number of summands isomorphic to $V_i$ is $\langle\chi_V,\chi_i\rangle$. So two representations with the same character are isomorphic.
\item $V$ is irreducible iff $\langle\chi_V,\chi_V\rangle=1$.
\item (Regular representation) Every irreducible $V_i$ occurs in $\C[G]$, $\dim V_i$ times. Hence there are finitely many irreducible representations up to isomorphism, and if $V_1,\dots,V_r$ is the full list, $\sum_i(\dim V_i)^2=|G|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Distinct irreducible representations have orthogonal characters by
Theorem~\ref{thm:orth}, so their characters are linearly independent in
the finite-dimensional space of class functions. There can therefore be
only finitely many irreducibles up to isomorphism. Extend any initial
partial list $V_1,\ldots,V_r$ to a full list for this proof; we do not yet
assume that their characters span every class function.

Maschke's theorem decomposes a
representation $V$ into irreducibles.
Group isomorphic summands together:
\[
V\cong\bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus m_i},
\qquad m_i\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
\]
By additivity of characters,
$\chi_V=\sum_i m_i\chi_i$.
Take its inner product with $\chi_j$.
Character orthogonality gives
\[
\langle\chi_V,\chi_j\rangle
=\sum_i m_i\langle\chi_i,\chi_j\rangle
=m_j.
\]
Thus the character recovers every
multiplicity. Two representations
with the same character have the
same irreducible summands and
are isomorphic, proving (a).

For (b), compute
\[
\langle\chi_V,\chi_V\rangle
=\left\langle\sum_i m_i\chi_i,
                  \sum_j m_j\chi_j\right\rangle
=\sum_i m_i^2.
\]
This equals $1$ exactly when a
single $m_i=1$ and all others vanish,
which says $V$ is irreducible.

For (c), consider the regular
representation $\C[G]$. Its character
is $|G|$ at the identity and $0$
elsewhere. The multiplicity formula
therefore gives
\[
m_i=\langle\chi_{\C[G]},\chi_i\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}|G|\,\overline{\chi_i(e)}
=\dim V_i.
\]
In particular every irreducible
appears in $\C[G]$, so only finitely
many isomorphism classes can exist.
Comparing dimensions in
\[
\C[G]\cong\bigoplus_i
V_i^{\oplus\dim V_i}
\]
gives the degree-sum identity
\[
\boxed{|G|=\sum_i(\dim V_i)^2}.
\]
This identity provides a useful
check for any proposed character table.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:classes}
The irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis of the space of class functions. In particular, the number of irreducible representations of $G$ is the number of its conjugacy classes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Irreducible characters are orthonormal by
Theorem~\ref{thm:orth}, so they are
linearly independent. We show that
they span the space of class functions.

Let $f:G\to\C$ be a class function
orthogonal to every irreducible
character. For a representation $\rho$
define
\[
T_\rho=\sum_{g\in G}
\overline{f(g)}\,\rho(g).
\]
Since $f$ is constant on conjugacy
classes, conjugating $T_\rho$
by any $\rho(h)$ just permutes
the summands. So $T_\rho$
commutes with the representation.

If $\rho_i$ is irreducible, Schur's
lemma makes $T_{\rho_i}=\lambda I$.
Taking traces gives
\[
\lambda\dim V_i
=\sum_g\overline{f(g)}\chi_i(g)
=|G|\,\overline{\langle f,\chi_i\rangle}=0.
\]
Thus $T_{\rho_i}=0$ on every
irreducible representation.
Maschke's theorem decomposes
every representation into
irreducibles, and $T_\rho$
respects that decomposition.
Hence $T_\rho=0$ for every
representation.

Apply this to the regular
representation $\C[G]$, whose
basis vectors are $e_x$, $x\in G$.
At the identity basis vector,
\[
0=T_{\C[G]}e_e
=\sum_{g\in G}\overline{f(g)}\,e_g.
\]
The vectors $e_g$ are linearly
independent, so $f(g)=0$
for every $g$. Thus the orthogonal
complement of the span of
irreducible characters is zero.
Their span is therefore the
whole space of class functions.

That space has one independent
coordinate for each conjugacy
class. Its dimension equals the
number of conjugacy classes.
An orthonormal basis has
exactly that number of elements,
so the two numbers coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[column orthogonality]\label{cor:columns}
Let $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_r$ be the irreducible characters, and $g,h\in G$. Then $\sum_i\chi_i(g)\overline{\chi_i(h)}$ is $|C_G(g)|$ if $g$ and $h$ are conjugate, and $0$ otherwise.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $C_1,\ldots,C_r$ be the conjugacy
classes. Theorem~\ref{thm:classes}
shows that the number of irreducible
characters is also $r$.

Construct the square matrix
\[
M_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{|C_j|}{|G|}}\,
\chi_i(C_j).
\]
Row orthogonality says that
\[
(M\overline M^{\,T})_{ik}
=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{|C_j|}{|G|}
\chi_i(C_j)\overline{\chi_k(C_j)}
=\delta_{ik}.
\]
Thus $M\overline M^{\,T}=I$.
A square matrix with a right inverse
is invertible, so its right inverse
is also a left inverse:
$\overline M^{\,T}M=I$.

The $(j,k)$ entry gives
\[
\frac{\sqrt{|C_j||C_k|}}{|G|}
\sum_{i=1}^r
\overline{\chi_i(C_j)}\chi_i(C_k)
=\delta_{jk}.
\]
For different classes $C_j,C_k$,
the sum is therefore zero.
For $j=k$, it equals
\[
\sum_i|\chi_i(C_j)|^2
=\frac{|G|}{|C_j|}
=|C_G(g)|\qquad(g\in C_j),
\]
by orbit--stabiliser for conjugation.

Interchanging $g$ and $h$
and taking complex conjugates
gives the desired formula for
$\sum_i\chi_i(g)\overline{\chi_i(h)}$.
The proof shows why the
column relations are available:
we already know that the
character table is square.
\end{proof}

The irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis for class functions. Putting their values on conjugacy classes into a table makes the orthogonality relations visible and lets us determine unknown entries from a few constraints.

\section{Character tables}

The \emph{character table} of $G$ lists the values of the irreducible characters on the conjugacy classes; it is square by Theorem~\ref{thm:classes}. Three tools fill it in: one-dimensional representations, permutation representations (whose characters count fixed points), and the orthogonality relations. Two more constructions help. If $\chi$ is a character and $\lambda$ a one-dimensional character, $\lambda\chi$ is a character (of $\rho'(g)=\lambda(g)\rho(g)$ on the same space), irreducible with $\chi$ since $\langle\lambda\chi,\lambda\chi\rangle=\langle\chi,\chi\rangle$ ($|\lambda(g)|=1$). And if $N\trianglelefteq G$, every representation of $G/N$ gives one of $G$ by composing with $G\to G/N$; it is irreducible iff the original is, since the two have the same matrices.

\begin{example}[$\mathfrak S_3$]\label{ex:s3}
Three classes (identity, $3$ transpositions, $2$ three-cycles), so three irreducibles, with $\sum d_i^2=6$: dimensions $1,1,2$. The permutation character of $\C^3$ is $(3,1,0)$ (fixed points); minus the trivial character it gives $\chi_2=(2,0,-1)$, and $\langle\chi_2,\chi_2\rangle=\frac{4+0+2\cdot1}6=1$, so it is irreducible: it is the triangle representation of Example~\ref{ex:reps}(5).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[$\mathfrak S_4$]\label{ex:s4}
Five classes, of sizes $1,6,3,8,6$ for $e$, $(12)$, $(12)(34)$, $(123)$, $(1234)$ (Chapter~4). The permutation character of $\C^4$ is $(4,2,0,1,0)$; minus the trivial one, $\chi_3=(3,1,-1,0,-1)$, of norm $\frac{9+6+3+0+6}{24}=1$. Then $\chi_3'=\varepsilon\chi_3=(3,-1,-1,0,1)$ is another irreducible character. The quotient $\mathfrak S_4/V_4\cong\mathfrak S_3$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:v4}) turns $\chi_2$ of $\mathfrak S_3$ into $(2,0,2,-1,0)$: the double transpositions lie in $V_4$, and the image of a $4$-cycle in $\mathfrak S_3$ is a transposition, since its square lies in $V_4$ but it does not. With the trivial and sign characters, these are five distinct irreducible characters, and $1+1+4+9+9=24$: the table is complete (Figure~\ref{fig:tables}). The two $3$-dimensional ones are the rotations of the cube ($\chi_3'$, through the isomorphism of Chapter~1) and the symmetries of the regular tetrahedron with vertices $e_1,\dots,e_4$ ($\chi_3$).
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Characters prove theorems about groups that have nothing to do with linear algebra. Burnside (1904) showed with them that a group of order $p^aq^b$ ($p,q$ primes) is solvable, and Frobenius that in a group acting transitively on a set where no non-identity element fixes two points, the identity together with the elements fixing no point form a normal subgroup. No proof of the latter avoiding characters is known. The dimensions of the irreducible representations always divide $|G|$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:divides}).
\end{remark}

For an abelian group, every conjugacy class has one element and every irreducible complex representation has dimension one. Its character table is therefore the Fourier matrix. The abstract theory becomes the familiar arithmetic of roots of unity.

\section{Abelian groups: Fourier analysis}\label{sec:fourierab}

Let $A$ be a finite abelian group. By Corollary~\ref{cor:abelianirr} and Theorem~\ref{thm:classes} (each element is its own class), $A$ has exactly $|A|$ irreducible characters, all one-dimensional: the homomorphisms $\chi:A\to\C^*$. They form a group $\widehat A$ under pointwise multiplication (a product of homomorphisms to the abelian group $\C^*$ is one), the \emph{dual group}, and $|\widehat A|=|A|$. For $A=\Z/n\Z$, a homomorphism is determined by $\chi(1)$, an $n$-th root of unity, so $\widehat A=\{\chi_j:k\mapsto e^{2i\pi jk/n}\}\cong\Z/n\Z$.

\begin{theorem}[Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group]\label{thm:fourier}
For $f:A\to\C$ define $\hat f(\chi)=\langle f,\chi\rangle=\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a}f(a)\overline{\chi(a)}$. Then
\[
\begin{gathered}f=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}\hat f(\chi)\,\chi,\qq\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}|f(a)|^2=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}|\hat f(\chi)|^2,\end{gathered}
\]
and for $a\in A$,
\[
\frac1{|A|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}\chi(a)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a=0,\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The group $A$ is abelian, so every
conjugacy class is a singleton.
Every function on $A$ is therefore
a class function.
The irreducible characters are all
one-dimensional by
Corollary~\ref{cor:abelianirr},
and form an orthonormal basis
of $\C^A$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:classes}.

For any $f:A\to\C$, the coordinate
along a character $\chi$ is
\[
\langle f,\chi\rangle
=\frac1{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}
f(a)\overline{\chi(a)}
=\widehat f(\chi).
\]
Expanding $f$ in this basis proves
Fourier inversion:
\[
f=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}
\widehat f(\chi)\chi.
\]
Pythagoras for an orthonormal basis
gives Parseval's identity:
\[
\frac1{|A|}\sum_a|f(a)|^2
=\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}
|\widehat f(\chi)|^2.
\]

Finally apply column orthogonality
(Corollary~\ref{cor:columns})
with $h=0$, the identity in additive
notation. Every character has
$\chi(0)=1$. The centraliser of
every element is $A$, and the
element $a$ is conjugate to $0$
only when $a=0$. Thus
\[
\boxed{\frac1{|A|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat A}
\chi(a)=\begin{cases}
1&a=0,\\0&a\ne0.
\end{cases}}
\]
These three identities are the
ordinary coordinate formulas
for an orthonormal basis,
applied to the character basis.
\end{proof}

For $A=\Z/n\Z$, $\hat f(\chi_j)=\frac1n\sum_kf(k)e^{-2i\pi jk/n}$ is the \emph{discrete Fourier transform}. Exercise~\ref{ex:circulant} will show exactly how it turns convolution into multiplication, with our normalization. When $n=2^k$, separate a Fourier sum into its even and odd indices. Each part is a transform of length $n/2$, and combining the two requires $O(n)$ additions and multiplications. Repeating the split gives $T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(n)$; the $k$ levels each cost $O(n)$, for $O(n\log n)$ operations. This is the radix-two fast Fourier transform, associated with Cooley and Tukey.

\subsection*{Dirichlet characters}

Let $q\ge1$. A \emph{Dirichlet character} modulo $q$ is a function $\chi:\Z\to\C$ obtained from a character $\chi$ of $(\Z/q\Z)^*$ by $\chi(n)=\chi(n\bmod q)$ if $\gcd(n,q)=1$, and $\chi(n)=0$ otherwise. It is periodic of period $q$ and \emph{completely multiplicative}: $\chi(mn)=\chi(m)\chi(n)$ for all $m,n$ (if $m$ or $n$ is not prime to $q$, both sides are $0$). There are $\varphi(q)$ of them; the \emph{principal} character $\chi_0$ is $1$ on integers prime to $q$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dirichletorth}
Let $a$ be prime to $q$. For every integer $n$,
\[
\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}\overline{\chi(a)}\,\chi(n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }n\equiv a\ (q),\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $A=(\Z/q\Z)^*$.
Its order is $\varphi(q)$.
Dirichlet characters modulo $q$
are multiplicative characters
of $A$, extended by zero to
residues that are not units.

If $\gcd(n,q)>1$, every character
vanishes at $n$, so the sum in
the statement is zero. As
$\gcd(a,q)=1$, such an $n$
cannot be congruent to $a$
modulo $q$, as required.

Otherwise $a$ and $n$ are
both units modulo $q$.
Every character value on a
unit is a root of unity, so
\[
\overline{\chi(a)}
=\chi(a)^{-1}=\chi(a^{-1}).
\]
Thus the summand becomes
$\chi(a^{-1}n)$. By the
character-sum identity in
Theorem~\ref{thm:fourier},
now written multiplicatively,
\[
\frac1{\varphi(q)}
\sum_{\chi\bmod q}\chi(a^{-1}n)
=\begin{cases}
1&a^{-1}n=1\text{ in }A,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The first condition is exactly
$n\equiv a\pmod q$.
This proves both cases and
explains why character averaging
detects a prescribed residue class.
\end{proof}

This identity is a \emph{filter}. For any real bound $X$, apply it separately
to the finitely many primes $p\le X$ and sum:
\[
\#\{p\le X:p\text{ prime},\ p\equiv a\pmod q\}
=\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}\overline{\chi(a)}
\sum_{\substack{p\le X\\p\text{ prime}}}\chi(p).
\]
Every sum here is finite. Volume~6 will introduce the analytic series and
Euler products that make this filter useful for Dirichlet's theorem. For $q=p$ an odd prime, $(\Z/p\Z)^*$ is cyclic of even order, so it has exactly one character of order $2$, which must be the Legendre symbol $\bigl(\frac np\bigr)$ of Volume~2: Euler's criterion $\bigl(\frac np\bigr)\equiv n^{(p-1)/2}$ shows that it is multiplicative.

\begin{example}\label{ex:dir5}
Modulo $5$, $2$ generates $(\Z/5\Z)^*=\{1,2,4,3\}=\{2^0,2^1,2^2,2^3\}$, and the four characters are determined by $\chi(2)\in\{1,i,-1,-i\}$:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$n\bmod5$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$\\\hline
$\chi_0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$\chi_1$ & $1$ & $i$ & $-i$ & $-1$\\
$\chi_2$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $-1$ & $1$\\
$\chi_3$ & $1$ & $-i$ & $i$ & $-1$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$\chi_2$ is the Legendre symbol $\bigl(\frac n5\bigr)$: the squares modulo $5$ are $1$ and $4$.
\end{example}

\begin{beauty}
The same few lines of linear algebra describe the vibrations of a symmetric molecule (chemists read the character table of its symmetry group), the frequencies of a sampled signal (the Fourier transform on $\Z/n\Z$), the mixing time of card shuffles (Diaconis and Shahshahani used the characters of $\mathfrak S_n$ to show that about $\frac12n\log n$ random transpositions mix a deck of $n$ cards, Volume~4), and the distribution of primes in progressions (Dirichlet characters, Volume~6). Fourier analysis on $\R$ and on the circle, met in Volume~5, is the same theory for infinite abelian groups; for non-abelian Lie groups such as $SO(3)$, the next chapter's rotations, it becomes the theory of spherical harmonics and of spin in quantum mechanics.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:pairs}
$\mathfrak S_4$ acts on the $6$ two-element subsets of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Decompose this permutation representation into irreducibles.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:onedim}
Show that the one-dimensional representations of $G$ correspond to the homomorphisms $G/G'\to\C^*$, where $G'$ is the derived subgroup (Exercise~\ref{ex:derived}), so that there are $[G:G']$ of them. How many do $\mathfrak S_n$ ($n\ge2$), $A_4$ and $A_5$ have?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:a4}
Find the character table of $A_4$. (Its classes have sizes $1,3,4,4$; use $A_4/V_4\cong\Z/3\Z$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:centralchar}
Let $\rho$ be irreducible of dimension $d$. Show that $\rho(z)$ is a scalar for every $z$ in the centre $Z(G)$, and deduce that $d^2\le[G:Z(G)]$. What does this give for the quaternion group $Q_8$, and what is its character table?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:circulant}
Let $n\ge1$ be an integer. For $f,g:\Z/n\Z\to\C$, let $(f*g)(k)=\sum_jf(j)g(k-j)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\widehat{f*g}(\chi)=n\,\hat f(\chi)\hat g(\chi)$.
\item A \emph{circulant} matrix has entries $C_{kj}=c(k-j)$ for some $c:\Z/n\Z\to\C$. Show that each character $\chi_m$, as a vector $(\chi_m(k))_k$, is an eigenvector of $C$, and find the eigenvalues.
\item A walker on $\Z/n\Z$ ($n$ odd) moves $\pm1$ with probability $\frac12$ each. For integer $t\ge0$, find the Fourier coefficients of its distribution after $t$ steps, starting from $0$, and show that it converges to the uniform distribution.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:dirichlet8}
Write the table of Dirichlet characters modulo $8$ and modulo $12$. Check Proposition~\ref{prop:dirichletorth} for $q=8$, $a=3$, and show that all characters modulo $8$ are real.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:a5}
Find the character table of $A_5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:tables}), using its permutation representations on $\{1,\dots,5\}$ and on the $10$ two-element subsets, and the orthogonality relations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:divides}
A complex number is an \emph{algebraic integer} if it is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $x\in\C$ is an algebraic integer iff $xM\subseteq M$ for some non-zero finitely generated subgroup $M$ of $(\C,+)$. Deduce that sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers, and that a rational algebraic integer is an integer.
\item Let $\chi$ be irreducible of dimension $d$, $C$ a conjugacy class and $g\in C$. Show that $\sum_{x\in C}\rho(x)$ is the scalar $\omega_C=|C|\chi(g)/d$, and that $\omega_C$ is an algebraic integer.
\item Deduce that $d$ divides $|G|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:glnz}
(Finite groups of integer matrices.)
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\cos\frac{2\pi}m$ is rational only for $m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$, and that every element of finite order of
$GL_2(\Z)$ has order $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ or $6$.
\item Let $p\ge3$ be a prime and $A\in GL_n(\Z)$ with $A\equiv I_n\pmod p$ and $A\ne I_n$. Show that $A$ has infinite order.
Deduce that every finite subgroup of $GL_n(\Z)$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb F_p)$, and that for $n=2$ its
order divides $48$.
\item Show that every finite subgroup of $GL_2(\Z)$ is cyclic or dihedral, of order at most $12$, and that $12$ is attained.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:commutators}
Let $G$ be a finite group and $N(g)$ the number of pairs $(x,y)\in G^2$ with $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}=g$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Frobenius, 1896.) Show that $N(g)=|G|\sum_\chi\frac{\chi(g)}{\chi(1)}$, the sum over the irreducible characters.
\item Deduce from the character table of $A_5$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:a5}) that every element of $A_5$ is a commutator,
and compute $N(g)$ on each class.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:frobschur}
Let $G$ be finite, $r(g)$ the number of $x\in G$ with $x^2=g$, and for an irreducible character $\chi$ let
$\nu(\chi)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{x\in G}\chi(x^2)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\nu(\chi)\in\{-1,0,1\}$, and that $\nu(\chi)\ne0$ if and only if $\chi$ is real-valued.
\item (Frobenius and Schur, 1906.) Show that $r=\sum_\chi\nu(\chi)\chi$.
\item Show that $\nu=-1$ for the irreducible character of degree $2$ of the quaternion group $Q_8$.
\item Show that $\nu(\chi)=1$ when the representation can be realised by real matrices. Every irreducible representation of
$\mathfrak S_n$ can be (Young, 1900). Deduce that the sum of the degrees of the irreducible characters of $\mathfrak S_n$ is
the number of $\sigma$ with $\sigma^2=e$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:pairs}
\idea A permutation representation has a particularly easy character: $\chi(g)$ is the number of basis vectors left fixed by $g$. Here the basis vectors are the six unordered pairs. Count fixed pairs in each conjugacy class, then take the inner products with the irreducible characters.

\writeup Let $V$ be the six-dimensional complex vector space with basis $e_{\{i,j\}}$ for $1\le i<j\le4$. The action of $\mathfrak S_4$ sends $e_{\{i,j\}}$ to $e_{\{g(i),g(j)\}}$. The trace counts the pairs left unchanged as \emph{sets}.

On the five conjugacy types $1,(12),(12)(34),(123),(1234)$, with class sizes $1,6,3,8,6$, the counts are:
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{type}&1&(12)&(12)(34)&(123)&(1234)\\\hline
\text{fixed pairs}&6&2&2&0&0
\end{array}.
\]
For either involution, the two fixed pairs are $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$. A $3$-cycle leaves no two-element subset unchanged; neither does a $4$-cycle. Thus
\[
\chi=(6,2,2,0,0).
\]

Use the irreducible character rows $\mathbf1$, $\varepsilon$, $\chi_2$, $\chi_3$, and $\chi'_3$ of the table in this chapter. Their multiplicities in $V$ are the inner products with $\chi$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle\chi,\mathbf1\rangle&=\frac{6+6(2)+3(2)}{24}=1,\\
\langle\chi,\varepsilon\rangle&=\frac{6-6(2)+3(2)}{24}=0,\\
\langle\chi,\chi_2\rangle&=\frac{6(2)+3(2)(2)}{24}=1,\\
\langle\chi,\chi_3\rangle&=\frac{6(3)+6(2)(1)+3(2)(-1)}{24}=1,\\
\langle\chi,\chi_3'\rangle&=\frac{6(3)+6(2)(-1)+3(2)(-1)}{24}=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Therefore
\[
V\cong\mathbf1\oplus V_2\oplus V_3.
\]
The dimensions provide a final check: $1+2+3=6$. The single trivial summand is the constant vector $\sum_{i<j}e_{\{i,j\}}$, which is visibly fixed by every permutation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:onedim}
\idea A one-dimensional representation is a multiplicative complex-valued homomorphism. Complex multiplication is commutative, so every commutator must act trivially. Therefore a one-dimensional representation only sees the abelianisation $G/G'$.

\writeup Let $\lambda:G\to GL_1(\C)=\C^*$ be a one-dimensional representation. For any $x,y\in G$,
\[
\lambda(xyx^{-1}y^{-1})
=\lambda(x)\lambda(y)\lambda(x)^{-1}\lambda(y)^{-1}=1.
\]
Thus every commutator is in $\ker\lambda$, and so is the subgroup $G'$ that they generate. The first isomorphism/correspondence properties of quotients show that $\lambda$ factors uniquely as
\[
G\longrightarrow G/G'\overset{\bar\lambda}{\longrightarrow}\C^*.
\]
Conversely, composing any homomorphism $\bar\lambda:G/G'\to\C^*$ with the quotient map gives a one-dimensional representation of $G$. These assignments are inverse.

The finite group $G/G'$ is abelian. By the structure theorem for finite abelian groups, it is a product of cyclic groups, and a cyclic factor of order $d$ has exactly $d$ complex characters, one for each $d$-th root of unity. Taking products shows that a finite abelian group $A$ has exactly $|A|$ one-dimensional characters. Hence the number sought for $G$ is $|G/G'|=[G:G']$.

\emph{Symmetric groups.} For $n\ge3$, the sign homomorphism shows $G'\subseteq A_n$. On the other hand, commutators of transpositions with a common point yield three-cycles. In fact, for distinct $a,b,c$,
\[
[(a\,b),(a\,c)]
=((a\,b)(a\,c))^2,
\]
which is a three-cycle or its inverse. Every three-cycle is obtained by choosing the labels appropriately. Because three-cycles generate $A_n$, we have $G'=A_n$. Thus $G/G'\cong\Z/2\Z$, so $\mathfrak S_n$ has exactly two one-dimensional representations: the trivial one and sign. For $n=2$ the group itself is cyclic of order $2$, giving the same answer.

\emph{Alternating group $A_4$.} Its normal subgroup
\[
V_4=\{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}
\]
has quotient $A_4/V_4\cong\Z/3\Z$, which is abelian; hence $A_4'\subseteq V_4$. The group $A_4$ is non-abelian, so its derived subgroup is not trivial. A non-trivial normal subgroup inside $V_4$ must contain one double transposition. All three double transpositions are conjugate in $A_4$, so normality forces it to contain all three. Thus $A_4'=V_4$, and there are $|A_4/V_4|=3$ one-dimensional representations.

\emph{Alternating group $A_5$.} Its derived subgroup $A_5'$ is normal. It is non-trivial because $A_5$ is not abelian. Since $A_5$ is simple, $A_5'=A_5$. The abelianisation is trivial, so the only one-dimensional representation is the trivial one.

The answers are therefore
\[
\boxed{\mathfrak S_n\ (n\ge2):2,\qquad A_4:3,\qquad A_5:1.}
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:a4}
\idea The normal Klein subgroup $V_4$ makes the three linear characters easy: they come from the quotient $A_4/V_4\cong\Z/3\Z$. The last character comes from the permutation action of $A_4$ on four vertices after removing the constant direction.

\writeup The group $A_4$ has order $12$. Its four conjugacy classes have sizes $1,3,4,4$: the identity; the three double transpositions forming $V_4\setminus\{e\}$; and two distinct classes of four $3$-cycles each.

Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, so $\omega^3=1$, $\omega\ne1$, and $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$. The quotient
\[
A_4/V_4\cong\Z/3\Z
\]
has exactly three linear characters, assigning to its generator the values $1,\omega,\omega^2$. Lift them along the quotient map. If we denote the two classes of $3$-cycles by representatives $(1\,2\,3)$ and $(1\,3\,2)$, the resulting characters are
\[
\lambda_k=(1,1,\omega^k,\omega^{2k}),
\qquad k=0,1,2.
\]
The first is the trivial character.

The number of irreducible characters equals the number of conjugacy classes, so there is exactly one more irreducible character, say $\chi$. The sum of squares of irreducible degrees equals $|A_4|$, hence if its degree is $d$,
\[
1^2+1^2+1^2+d^2=12
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d=3.
\]

We can construct this representation concretely. Let $A_4$ permute the standard basis vectors of $\C^4$. Its permutation character counts fixed letters. On the four classes above, the fixed-letter counts are respectively $4,0,1,1$. The line spanned by $(1,1,1,1)$ is invariant and affords the trivial character. Its three-dimensional complementary subspace
\[
W=\{(z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4):z_1+z_2+z_3+z_4=0\}
\]
therefore has character
\[
\chi=(4,0,1,1)-(1,1,1,1)=(3,-1,0,0).
\]
Its norm is
\[
\langle\chi,\chi\rangle
=\frac{1\cdot9+3\cdot1+4\cdot0+4\cdot0}{12}=1,
\]
so it is irreducible.

Putting the four rows together gives the complete character table:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
 & $e$ & $(12)(34)$ & $(123)$ & $(132)$\\
class size & $1$ & $3$ & $4$ & $4$\\\hline
$\lambda_0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$\lambda_1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $\omega$ & $\omega^2$\\
$\lambda_2$ & $1$ & $1$ & $\omega^2$ & $\omega$\\
$\chi$ & $3$ & $-1$ & $0$ & $0$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The dimensions $1,1,1,3$ satisfy $1^2+1^2+1^2+3^2=12$, a final check. Geometrically, $W$ is the complexification of the familiar three-dimensional rotation representation of the regular tetrahedron.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:centralchar}
\idea The centre of a group is the part that every group element leaves alone under conjugation. In an irreducible complex representation, Schur's lemma says that any operator commuting with the whole representation is a scalar. Orthogonality then bounds the dimension, and for $Q_8$ it nearly fills in the character table by itself.

\writeup \emph{A bound for every finite group.}
Let $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ be an irreducible complex representation, with $\dim V=d$ and character $\chi$. For $z\in Z(G)$ and $g\in G$,
\[
\rho(z)\rho(g)=\rho(zg)=\rho(gz)=\rho(g)\rho(z).
\]
Schur's lemma therefore gives $\rho(z)=\lambda_z I$. As $z$ has finite order, $\rho(z)$ does too, so $\lambda_z$ is a root of unity. In particular $|\lambda_z|=1$ and
\[
|\chi(z)|=|\operatorname{tr}(\lambda_z I)|=d.
\]
The character orthogonality relation from this chapter says
\[
1=\langle\chi,\chi\rangle
 =\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\chi(g)|^2.
\]
Each summand is non-negative. Keeping only the elements in $Z(G)$ gives
\[
1\ge\frac{|Z(G)|}{|G|}d^2,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\boxed{d^2\le [G:Z(G)]}.
\]
For example, when $G$ is abelian, $Z(G)=G$ and this forces $d=1$, as we already know.

\emph{The quaternion group.}
Write $Q_8=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$ with $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$. Its centre is $\{\pm1\}$, so every irreducible representation has dimension at most $2$.

The five conjugacy classes are
\[
\{1\},\quad\{-1\},\quad\{\pm i\},\quad\{\pm j\},\quad\{\pm k\}.
\]
Indeed $i$ and $-i$ are conjugate (conjugate $i$ by $j$), and similarly for $j$ and $k$; the two central elements are each alone. Thus there are five irreducible characters. The quotient
\[
Q_8/\{\pm1\}\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2
\]
gives four distinct one-dimensional characters, all equal to $1$ on $-1$. The degree-sum identity $\sum_\chi\chi(1)^2=8$ then forces the fifth degree to be $2$.

We can compute its character $\psi$ without guessing any matrices. By column orthogonality, the column for the identity is orthogonal to the column for $-1$:
\[
0=4\cdot(1\cdot1)+2\overline{\psi(-1)}.
\]
Hence $\psi(-1)=-2$. For $\{\pm i\}$, the four linear characters have values $1,1,-1,-1$, whose sum is zero. Orthogonality of this column with the identity column therefore gives $\psi(i)=0$. The same argument works for $j$ and $k$.

The complete table (columns are conjugacy classes) is
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
 &\{1\}&\{-1\}&\{\pm i\}&\{\pm j\}&\{\pm k\}\\ \hline
\chi_0&1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_i&1&1&1&-1&-1\\
\chi_j&1&1&-1&1&-1\\
\chi_k&1&1&-1&-1&1\\
\psi&2&-2&0&0&0
\end{array}
\]
As a check, the sum of squared degrees is $4+4=8$, and the weighted squared norm of $\psi$ is $(4+4)/8=1$.

\tools Schur's lemma, character orthogonality, the degree-sum identity, and conjugacy classes.

\leads The dihedral group $D_4$ has the same character table as $Q_8$, although the two groups are not isomorphic. A character table remembers a great deal about symmetry, but not everything.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:circulant}
\idea Convolution is the algebraic rule for adding independent positions on a cyclic group. Fourier characters turn convolution into scalar multiplication, which makes repeated steps easy to compute. For the convergence rate, compare \emph{absolute values} of the Fourier eigenvalues: the most negative one can decay more slowly than the first positive one.

\writeup Write $\chi_m(k)=e^{2\pi i mk/n}$ for $m,k\in\Z/n\Z$, and use the normalised Fourier transform
\[
\hat f(\chi)=\frac1n\sum_{k\in\Z/n\Z}f(k)\overline{\chi(k)}.
\]

\emph{(a) Transform of a convolution.} Starting from the definitions and substituting $\ell=k-j$, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
n\,\widehat{f*g}(\chi)
&=\sum_k\sum_j f(j)g(k-j)\overline{\chi(k)}\\
&=\sum_j\sum_\ell f(j)g(\ell)\overline{\chi(j+\ell)}\\
&=\left(\sum_j f(j)\overline{\chi(j)}\right)
  \left(\sum_\ell g(\ell)\overline{\chi(\ell)}\right)\\
&=n^2\hat f(\chi)\hat g(\chi).
\end{aligned}
\]
We used the character law $\chi(j+\ell)=\chi(j)\chi(\ell)$ in the third line. Dividing by $n$ proves
\[
\boxed{\widehat{f*g}(\chi)=n\,\hat f(\chi)\hat g(\chi)}.
\]

\emph{(b) Circulant eigenvectors.} Let $C_{kj}=c(k-j)$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
(C\chi_m)(k)
&=\sum_jc(k-j)\chi_m(j)\\
&=\sum_\ell c(\ell)\chi_m(k-\ell)\\
&=\chi_m(k)\sum_\ell c(\ell)\overline{\chi_m(\ell)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $\chi_m$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\[
\boxed{\lambda_m=n\hat c(\chi_m)
=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}c(\ell)e^{-2\pi i m\ell/n}}.
\]
The $n$ characters form an orthogonal basis, so this lists all the eigenvalues, with multiplicity.

\emph{(c) The random walk.} A single step has law
\[
s=\tfrac12(\delta_1+\delta_{-1}).
\]
Starting from $0$, after $t$ independent steps the position law is the $t$-fold convolution $s^{*t}$. The transform of one step is
\[
n\hat s(\chi_m)=\tfrac12(e^{-2\pi im/n}+e^{2\pi im/n})
=\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}.
\]
Applying (a) repeatedly, including $t=0$, gives
\[
\boxed{\widehat{s^{*t}}(\chi_m)
=\frac1n\left(\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}\right)^t}.
\]
Fourier inversion consequently yields the exact probability
\[
s^{*t}(k)=\frac1n\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}
\left(\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}\right)^t
e^{2\pi i mk/n}.
\]

The mode $m=0$ equals $1/n$, the uniform distribution. For odd $n\ge3$, none of the other angles $2\pi m/n$ is a multiple of $\pi$, hence
\[
\left|\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}\right|<1
\quad (m\ne0).
\]
Every non-constant mode therefore tends to zero, proving $s^{*t}(k)\to1/n$ for every $k$.

\emph{Which mode is slowest?} The negative eigenvalues matter as much as the positive ones. For odd $n$,
\[
\max_{1\le m<n}\left|\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}\right|
=\cos\frac{\pi}{n}.
\]
The maximum occurs at $m=(n-1)/2$ and $m=(n+1)/2$, whose eigenvalues are $-\cos(\pi/n)$. Thus the slowest non-constant contribution is of magnitude
\[
\cos^t\frac{\pi}{n}
=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi^2t}{2n^2}
+O\!\left(\frac{t}{n^4}\right)\right)
\]
for large odd $n$. The negative sign makes that mode alternate with $t$, but its magnitude decays. This is why the natural relaxation scale is of order $n^2$.

For even $n$, the character $m=n/2$ has eigenvalue $\cos\pi=-1$. That component never decays: the walk alternates between the two parity classes, so it does not converge to the uniform law without an aperiodic modification such as a holding probability.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:dirichlet8}
\idea A Dirichlet character modulo $q$ is a character of the unit group $(\Z/q\Z)^*$, extended by $0$ on non-units. For $q=8$ and $12$, every non-identity unit has order $2$. The character is therefore determined by assigning signs to two independent generators.

\writeup Modulo $8$, the units are
\[
(\Z/8\Z)^*=\{1,3,5,7\}.
\]
They satisfy $3^2=5^2=7^2=1$ and $3\cdot5=7$ modulo $8$. Thus the group is $(\Z/2\Z)^2$, with independent generators $3$ and $5$. For a character $\chi$, multiplicativity forces
\[
\chi(3)^2=\chi(5)^2=1,
\quad\text{so}\quad \chi(3),\chi(5)\in\{\pm1\},
\qquad\chi(7)=\chi(3)\chi(5).
\]
Each independent pair of signs gives exactly one character. Here is the full table on the units:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|rrrr}
modulo $8$ & $1$ & $3$ & $5$ & $7$\\\hline
$\chi_0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$\chi_1$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $1$ & $-1$\\
$\chi_2$ & $1$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $-1$\\
$\chi_3$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $-1$ & $1$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
On residues $0,2,4,6$, each of these Dirichlet characters has value $0$.

Modulo $12$, the units are $\{1,5,7,11\}$, with $5^2=7^2=1$ and $5\cdot7=11$. We obtain the same abstract group and four characters:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|rrrr}
modulo $12$ & $1$ & $5$ & $7$ & $11$\\\hline
$\psi_0$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$ & $1$\\
$\psi_1$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $1$ & $-1$\\
$\psi_2$ & $1$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $-1$\\
$\psi_3$ & $1$ & $-1$ & $-1$ & $1$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The characters vanish at every residue not coprime to $12$.

We can now check the orthogonality projector of Proposition~\ref{prop:dirichletorth} for $q=8$ and $a=3$. All values are real, so complex conjugation has no effect. Evaluate
\[
\frac1{\varphi(8)}\sum_{\chi\bmod8}\overline{\chi(3)}\chi(n)
=\frac14\sum_{\chi\bmod8}\chi(3)\chi(n).
\]
The four products $\chi(3)\chi(n)$ sum to $0$ at $n=1,5,7$ and to $4$ at $n=3$; at even $n$ every character is zero. For instance at $n=3$ every product equals $\chi(3)^2=1$, while at $n=5$ the four products are $1,-1,-1,1$.

The projector is therefore $1$ exactly when $n\equiv3\pmod8$, and $0$ otherwise. Finally, the tables prove that every character modulo $8$ is real: all its values belong to $\{0,\pm1\}$. This is a consequence of the exponent-$2$ unit group, not a general property of Dirichlet characters.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:a5}
\idea Permuting five letters gives one representation, and permuting their ten unordered pairs gives another. We can subtract characters already found, then let orthogonality determine the two remaining rows. The golden ratio will arise from a quadratic equation, not from guessing the geometry of an icosahedron.

\writeup \emph{Step 1: the five conjugacy classes.}
From Theorem~\ref{thm:a5}, the class sizes of $A_5$ are
\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{type}&1&2^2&3&5_a&5_b\\\hline
\text{size}&1&15&20&12&12.
\end{array}
\]
There are therefore exactly five irreducible complex characters. Every element of $A_5$ is conjugate in $A_5$ to its inverse: this is evident for involutions, a suitable double transposition reverses a $3$-cycle, and $(2\,5)(3\,4)$ reverses $(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)$. Consequently all character values are real, since $\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}$.

\emph{Step 2: the representations on points and pairs.}
A permutation representation has character equal to the number of objects fixed. On the five letters, those counts are
\[
(5,1,2,0,0).
\]
Subtract the constant character $\mathbf1=(1,1,1,1,1)$ to obtain
\[
\chi_4=(4,0,1,-1,-1).
\]
Its weighted squared norm is $(16+20+12+12)/60=1$, so it is irreducible.

On the ten unordered pairs, the fixed-pair counts are
\[
(10,2,1,0,0).
\]
For example, $(12)(34)$ fixes $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$, while $(123)$ fixes only the pair of letters outside that cycle. Subtract the trivial character and $\chi_4$:
\[
\chi_5=(10,2,1,0,0)-\mathbf1-\chi_4
=(5,1,-1,0,0).
\]
Its squared norm is $(25+15+20)/60=1$. Orthogonality also checks that it is perpendicular to $\mathbf1$ and $\chi_4$. Hence it is an irreducible character of degree five.

\emph{Step 3: the two missing degrees.}
The degree-sum identity gives
\[
\sum_{\chi\text{ irreducible}}\chi(1)^2=|A_5|=60.
\]
We have three of the five characters, with degrees $1$, $4$, and $5$. The remaining two positive integer squares must add to
$60-1-16-25=18$, so both remaining degrees are $3$.

Write their rows as $(3,a,b,c,d)$ and $(3,a',b',c',d')$. Column orthogonality with the identity column gives one equation for each non-identity class; the squared length of a class column equals the order of its centraliser.

For the double transpositions, the relevant equations are
\[
1+5+3(a+a')=0,\qquad
1+1+a^2+(a')^2=4.
\]
Thus $a+a'=-2$ and $a^2+(a')^2=2$, forcing $a=a'=-1$.

For the $3$-cycles,
\[
1+4-5+3(b+b')=0,\qquad
1+1+1+b^2+(b')^2=3.
\]
The second equation already forces $b=b'=0$.

For either $5$-cycle class, let $u,v$ denote its values in the two degree-three characters. The corresponding equations are
\[
1-4+3(u+v)=0,\qquad
1+1+u^2+v^2=5.
\]
So $u+v=1$ and $u^2+v^2=3$, hence $uv=-1$. The values are the roots of
\[
X^2-X-1=0:
\qquad \phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\quad
\bar\phi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}.
\]
Finally, a degree-three row must be orthogonal to the trivial row:
\[
3+15(-1)+12c+12d=0,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad c+d=1.
\]
As $c,d$ must be the two roots just obtained, each row takes $\phi$ on one $5$-cycle class and $\bar\phi$ on the other.

The complete table is therefore
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
&1&2^2&3&5_a&5_b\\\hline
\chi_1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_3&3&-1&0&\phi&\bar\phi\\
\chi_3'&3&-1&0&\bar\phi&\phi\\
\chi_4&4&0&1&-1&-1\\
\chi_5&5&1&-1&0&0
\end{array}
\]
with class weights $(1,15,20,12,12)$. For a final check, the two degree-three rows are orthogonal because
\[
\frac{9+15+12(2\phi\bar\phi)}{60}
=\frac{9+15-24}{60}=0.
\]

\tools Permutation characters, the irreducibility norm test, class-column orthogonality, and the degree-sum identity.

\leads The two values $\phi$ and $\bar\phi$ describe the traces of rotations of an icosahedron. The character table has recovered that geometry from counting and orthogonality alone.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:divides}
\idea To prove that an unfamiliar complex number is an algebraic integer, try to make multiplication by that number act on a finite collection of generators using only integer coefficients. A matrix with integer entries then supplies a monic polynomial. In character theory, the useful generators are the sums over conjugacy classes.

\writeup \emph{(a) The finite-module test.}
Suppose first that $x$ satisfies
\[
x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1x+a_0=0,
\qquad a_i\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Consider the non-zero additive subgroup
\[
M=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Zx+\cdots+\mathbb Zx^{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb C.
\]
Multiplication by $x$ sends the first $n-1$ generators to the next generator, and the polynomial equation expresses $x^n$ as an integer combination of the same generators. Thus $xM\subseteq M$.

Conversely, suppose $M=\mathbb Zm_1+\cdots+\mathbb Zm_k\ne\{0\}$ and $xM\subseteq M$. For each $j$ choose integers $a_{ij}$ such that
\[
xm_j=\sum_{i=1}^k a_{ij}m_i.
\]
Let $A=(a_{ij})$. With $m=(m_1,\ldots,m_k)^{\mathsf T}$ we have, after transposing the coefficient matrix if needed, $(xI-A^{\mathsf T})m=0$. Since $m$ is not the zero complex vector,
\[
\det(xI-A^{\mathsf T})=0.
\]
But $\det(XI-A^{\mathsf T})$ is monic with integer coefficients. Hence $x$ is an algebraic integer.

If $x$ and $y$ are algebraic integers with monic equations of degrees $n$ and $m$, respectively, the subgroup generated by the $nm$ numbers
\[
x^iy^j\qquad(0\le i<n,\ 0\le j<m)
\]
contains $1$ and is stable under multiplication by both $x$ and $y$. It is therefore stable under multiplication by $x+y$ and by $xy$. The criterion just proved shows that sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers.

Finally, if $a/b\in\mathbb Q$ is in lowest terms and is a root of
$X^n+c_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0$, with all $c_i\in\mathbb Z$, multiply by $b^n$. We obtain
\[
a^n+b(c_{n-1}a^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0b^{n-1})=0.
\]
So $b\mid a^n$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, we must have $b=1$. Every rational algebraic integer is an integer.

\emph{(b) Class sums give algebraic integers.}
Let $C$ be a conjugacy class of a finite group $G$ and put
\[
E_C=\sum_{x\in C}\rho(x).
\]
For every $g\in G$, conjugation by $g$ permutes $C$, so $\rho(g)E_C\rho(g)^{-1}=E_C$. Schur's lemma makes $E_C$ a scalar matrix $\omega_C I$. Taking its trace gives
\[
\boxed{\omega_C=\frac{|C|\chi(c)}d},\qquad c\in C.
\]

Why is this scalar an algebraic integer? Multiply two class sums. For classes $C,D,E$, fix any $z\in E$, and let $a_{CDE}$ be the number of pairs $(x,y)\in C\times D$ such that $xy=z$. Conjugation transports these pairs bijectively as $z$ varies inside $E$, so $a_{CDE}$ is well defined and is a non-negative integer. Therefore
\[
E_CE_D=\sum_Ea_{CDE}E_E
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega_C\omega_D=\sum_Ea_{CDE}\omega_E.
\]
The subgroup $M=\sum_E\mathbb Z\omega_E$ of $\mathbb C$ is finitely generated, is non-zero because $\omega_{\{e\}}=1$, and is stable under multiplication by $\omega_C$. Part (a) now proves that every $\omega_C$ is an algebraic integer.

\emph{(c) Why the degree divides the group order.}
Each matrix $\rho(g)$ has finite order, so its eigenvalues are roots of unity. Each such root is an algebraic integer (it satisfies $X^k-1=0$ for some $k$). Since $\chi(g)$ is their sum, both $\chi(g)$ and $\overline{\chi(g)}$ are algebraic integers.

Character orthogonality gives
\[
1=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\chi(g)|^2
 =\frac1{|G|}\sum_C|C|\,\chi(c)\overline{\chi(c)}.
\]
Multiply by $|G|/d$ and use part (b):
\[
\frac{|G|}{d}
  =\sum_C\frac{|C|\chi(c)}d\,\overline{\chi(c)}
  =\sum_C\omega_C\,\overline{\chi(c)}.
\]
Every term on the right is a product of algebraic integers. Their finite sum is therefore an algebraic integer. The left side is rational, so part (a) tells us that it is an ordinary integer:
\[
\boxed{d\mid |G|}.
\]
For the quaternion example, $d=2$ and $|G|=8$, as the theorem predicts. Notice that the argument proved divisibility, not merely the weaker bound $d^2\le|G|$.

\tools Schur's lemma, character orthogonality, traces, and elementary polynomial divisibility.

\leads Conjugacy classes are not only a way to organise a character table. Their sums turn the non-commutative group into an integer algebra, and integrality in that algebra forces an arithmetic restriction on representation dimensions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:glnz}
\idea An integer matrix has more restrictions than a real matrix. Its finite-order eigenvalues are roots of unity, but its trace is an integer. That limits the possible rotation angles. In higher dimensions, reducing integer matrices modulo an odd prime detects all finite-order elements.

\writeup \emph{(a) Restrict finite-order matrices in dimension two.}
Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/m}$. The number $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2\cos(2\pi/m)$ is an algebraic integer, since roots of unity are algebraic integers and their sums are too. If this number is rational, it must be an integer. It lies in $[-2,2]$, so
\[
\cos(2\pi/m)\in\{0,\pm\tfrac12,\pm1\}.
\]
The angles in $[0,2\pi)$ with these cosine values are integer multiples of $\pi/3$ or $\pi/2$ (including $0$ and $\pi$). A primitive $m$-th root with such an angle has order
\[
\boxed{m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}}.
\]

Now take a finite-order $A\in GL_2(\mathbb Z)$. Its minimal polynomial divides $X^m-1$ for some $m$, and $X^m-1$ has no repeated complex roots. Hence $A$ is diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$. If both eigenvalues are real, each is $1$ or $-1$, and $A^2=I$, so its order is $1$ or $2$. Otherwise they are conjugate roots of unity $\lambda,\bar\lambda$. The integer trace is
\[
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\lambda+\bar\lambda
=2\cos(2\pi a/d),
\]
where $d$ is the exact order of $\lambda$ and $\gcd(a,d)=1$. The preceding rational-cosine argument forces $d\in\{3,4,6\}$ (the real values $\pm2$ would make the eigenvalues real). Since $A$ is diagonalisable, its order is the least common multiple of its eigenvalue orders, so
\[
\boxed{o(A)\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}}.
\]

\emph{(b) Why reduction modulo an odd prime is injective on finite subgroups.}
Fix $p\ge3$. Suppose $A\in GL_n(\mathbb Z)$ has finite order greater than one and $A\equiv I\pmod p$. Choose a prime $q$ dividing the order of $A$ and replace $A$ by a suitable positive power of order exactly $q$. The new matrix still satisfies the congruence and is not the identity.

Choose the largest $k\ge1$ such that every entry of $A-I$ is divisible by $p^k$, and write
\[
A=I+p^kB
\]
with some entry of $B$ not divisible by $p$. The binomial theorem applies because $I$ commutes with $B$:
\[
0=A^q-I=qp^kB+
\sum_{j=2}^q\binom qj p^{kj}B^j.
\]
If $q\ne p$, the first term has an entry divisible by $p^k$ but not $p^{k+1}$, while every term in the sum is divisible by $p^{2k}$ and hence by $p^{k+1}$. Impossible.

If $q=p$, the first term is $p^{k+1}B$, with some entry not divisible by $p^{k+2}$. For $2\le j<p$, the binomial coefficient $\binom pj$ is divisible by $p$, so those terms have a factor $p^{1+kj}$, at least $p^{k+2}$. The last term is $p^{kp}B^p$, also divisible by $p^{k+2}$ because $p\ge3$. Again impossible. Therefore no non-identity element of finite order can be congruent to $I$ modulo an odd prime.

The reduction homomorphism
\[
GL_n(\mathbb Z)\longrightarrow GL_n(\mathbb F_p)
\]
is consequently injective on any finite subgroup $G$. Thus $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb F_p)$, and Lagrange's theorem gives $|G|\mid|GL_n(\mathbb F_p)|$. In dimension two, taking $p=3$ gives
\[
\boxed{|G|\mid |GL_2(\mathbb F_3)|=(9-1)(9-3)=48}.
\]

\emph{(c) From integer matrices to planar rotations and reflections.}
For a finite subgroup $G\le GL_2(\mathbb Z)$, start with the ordinary Euclidean dot product and average it:
\[
\langle u,v\rangle_G=\sum_{g\in G}(gu)\cdot(gv).
\]
It is positive definite, and it is invariant under $G$, because for $h\in G$ the substitution $g\mapsto gh$ permutes the terms. Choosing an orthonormal basis for this new inner product turns the elements of $G$ into orthogonal real matrices.

Let $G^+$ be the subgroup with determinant $1$, so its elements are rotations. A finite subgroup of the circle group is cyclic, say generated by a rotation of order $m$. By (a), $m\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}$. If every element of $G$ is a rotation, $G=G^+$ is cyclic. Otherwise choose an orientation-reversing element $s$. In the plane every such orthogonal matrix is a reflection, so $s^2=I$, and
\[
srs^{-1}=r^{-1}
\]
for every rotation $r$. The group is $G=G^+\cup sG^+$, a dihedral group of order $2m$. Thus every finite subgroup is cyclic or dihedral, and
\[
\boxed{|G|\le12}.
\]

The bound is attained. The integer matrices
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
satisfy $A^6=I$, with $A$ of exact order $6$, $B^2=I$, and $BAB^{-1}=A^{-1}$. They generate a dihedral group of order $12$, realised by the symmetries of a hexagonal lattice.

\tools Rational algebraic integers, finite-order eigenvalues, elementary $p$-adic divisibility and invariant inner products.

\leads A lattice permits rotations only of orders $1,2,3,4,6$. That familiar crystallographic restriction emerges here from the integer entries of the matrices, not from a drawing of a lattice.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:commutators}
\idea Counting commutators looks like a problem about pairs of elements. The count is constant on conjugacy classes, so characters can encode it. Schur's lemma evaluates the relevant character sums.

\writeup \emph{(a) Frobenius's formula.}
Write $[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$. Simultaneous conjugation by $h$ sends $[x,y]$ to $h[x,y]h^{-1}$. Thus $N(g)$ is constant on each conjugacy class: $N$ is a class function.

Let $\rho:G\to GL_d(\mathbb C)$ be irreducible with character $\chi$. For any $d\times d$ matrix $M$, consider the averaged matrix
\[
S(M)=\sum_{y\in G}\rho(y)M\rho(y)^{-1}.
\]
Conjugation by any $\rho(h)$ just permutes the summands. Schur's lemma therefore makes $S(M)$ a scalar matrix. Taking traces gives its scalar exactly:
\[
S(M)=\frac{|G|\operatorname{tr}(M)}d I.
\]
Set $M=\rho(x)^{-1}$, multiply by $\rho(x)$, and take traces again:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{y\in G}\chi([x,y])
&=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\rho(x)S(\rho(x)^{-1})\bigr)\\
&=\frac{|G|}d\chi(x)\chi(x^{-1})\\
&=\frac{|G|}d|\chi(x)|^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The final equality uses $\chi(x^{-1})=\overline{\chi(x)}$, since finite-group representations can be made unitary.

Sum over $x$ and apply the row-orthogonality relation:
\[
\sum_{x,y\in G}\chi([x,y])
=\frac{|G|}d\sum_x|\chi(x)|^2
=\frac{|G|^2}d.
\]
This number is real. Consequently
\[
\langle N,\chi\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g N(g)\overline{\chi(g)}
=\frac1{|G|}\overline{\sum_{x,y}\chi([x,y])}
=\frac{|G|}d.
\]
The irreducible characters are an orthonormal basis for class functions, so the expansion of $N$ in that basis is
\[
\boxed{N(g)=|G|\sum_{\chi\text{ irreducible}}
                \frac{\chi(g)}{\chi(1)}.}
\]

\emph{(b) Every element of $A_5$ occurs as a commutator.}
Use the five rows of the character table from Exercise~\ref{ex:a5}. Put $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $\bar\phi=(1-\sqrt5)/2$, so $\phi+\bar\phi=1$. In the order of classes
\[
1,\qquad 2^2,\qquad 3,\qquad5_a,\qquad5_b,
\]
the character degrees are $1,3,3,4,5$, and the class sizes are $1,15,20,12,12$.

Divide Frobenius's formula by $60$ and add the five character contributions. For each class, the result is
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{class}&N(g)/60&N(g)\\\hline
1&1+1+1+1+1=5&300\\
2^2&1-\frac13-\frac13+\frac15=\frac8{15}&32\\
3&1+\frac14-\frac15=\frac{21}{20}&63\\
5_a,5_b&1+\frac{\phi+\bar\phi}{3}-\frac14
=\frac{13}{12}&65
\end{array}
\]
Every entry in the final column is positive, so every element of $A_5$ is a commutator.

A useful consistency check counts all ordered pairs at once. Because $|A_5|=60$, there are $60^2=3600$ pairs, and our class totals give
\[
1\cdot300+15\cdot32+20\cdot63+12\cdot65+12\cdot65
=\boxed{3600}.
\]
For the identity class the result $N(e)=300=60\cdot5$ also matches the five conjugacy classes: commuting pairs are counted by summing centraliser sizes over the elements of $G$.

\tools Schur's lemma, the orthonormal basis of irreducible characters, and Exercise~\ref{ex:a5}.

\leads The formula changes a non-linear equation in two group elements into a finite sum of characters. For $A_5$ that sum is positive on every class, making existence of a commutator a numerical conclusion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:frobschur}
\idea A representation of dimension $d$ acts on pairs of vectors. Symmetric pairs and antisymmetric pairs contribute differently to the trace of $\rho(g)^2$. Their invariant vectors tell us whether an irreducible character is real, complex, or quaternionic.

\writeup \emph{(a) The three possible indicator values.}
Let $V$ be an irreducible complex representation with character $\chi$, and take $g\in G$. Since $\rho(g)$ has finite order, it is diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$, say with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$. On the tensor product $V\otimes V$, the operator $\rho(g)\otimes\rho(g)$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_i\lambda_j$. Its restriction to the symmetric square $\operatorname{Sym}^2V$ has one for every pair $i\le j$, while the exterior square $\Lambda^2V$ has one for every $i<j$. Writing their characters as $\chi_S$ and $\chi_A$ gives
\[
\chi_S(g)=\frac{\chi(g)^2+\chi(g^2)}2,\qquad
\chi_A(g)=\frac{\chi(g)^2-\chi(g^2)}2.
\]
Subtract:
\[
\chi(g^2)=\chi_S(g)-\chi_A(g).
\]
Averaging a representation's character over $G$ gives the dimension of its invariant subspace. Thus, if $s$ and $a$ denote the dimensions of the invariant subspaces of $\operatorname{Sym}^2V$ and $\Lambda^2V$,
\[
\nu(\chi)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\chi(g^2)=s-a.
\]

The two spaces form the decomposition $V\otimes V=\operatorname{Sym}^2V\oplus\Lambda^2V$, so
\[
s+a=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\chi(g)^2
=\langle\chi,\bar\chi\rangle.
\]
Here $\bar\chi$ is another irreducible character, the character of the dual representation. By orthogonality the last inner product is $1$ if $\chi=\bar\chi$ and $0$ otherwise. As $s,a$ are non-negative integers, either $(s,a)=(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, or $(0,1)$. Hence
\[
\boxed{\nu(\chi)\in\{-1,0,1\},\qquad
\nu(\chi)\ne0\iff\chi=\bar\chi.}
\]
The equality $\chi=\bar\chi$ means exactly that $\chi$ is real-valued. The values $1$ and $-1$ distinguish whether the invariant bilinear form is symmetric or alternating.

\emph{(b) Expand the square-root counting function.}
Write $r(g)=|\{x\in G:x^2=g\}|$. Conjugation preserves squares, so $r$ is a class function. Its coefficient along any irreducible character is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle r,\chi\rangle
&=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g r(g)\overline{\chi(g)}\\
&=\frac1{|G|}\sum_x\overline{\chi(x^2)}
=\overline{\nu(\chi)}=\nu(\chi).
\end{aligned}
\]
The last equality holds because part (a) made the indicator an integer. Expanding in the orthonormal basis of irreducible characters gives
\[
\boxed{r=\sum_{\chi}\nu(\chi)\chi}.
\]

\emph{(c) The quaternion character has indicator $-1$.}
In $Q_8$, only $1$ and $-1$ have square equal to $1$, so $r(1)=2$. The four one-dimensional characters take values in $\{\pm1\}$ and have indicator $1$ (their squares are identically $1$). The only remaining irreducible character has degree $2$, by the table in Exercise~\ref{ex:centralchar}. Evaluating the identity of (b) at $1$ gives
\[
2=4\cdot1+2\nu,\quad\text{so}\quad\boxed{\nu=-1}.
\]
Its character is real-valued, but the representation does not admit a real form of dimension $2$.

\emph{(d) Representations defined over the reals.}
Suppose an irreducible complex representation $\rho$ can be realised by real matrices. On its real form, average the ordinary Euclidean inner product over all matrices $\rho(g)$. The result is a positive-definite, symmetric, $G$-invariant real bilinear form. Extend it complex-bilinearly to $V$. This gives a non-zero invariant in $\operatorname{Sym}^2V^*$, and the form also identifies $V$ with $V^*$ as representations. Since an irreducible module has at most one invariant line in $V\otimes V$, the form is symmetric rather than alternating: $s=1$, $a=0$, and $\nu(\chi)=1$.

Every irreducible complex representation of $\mathfrak S_n$ has a real form (for example through the Specht-module construction). Thus every one of its irreducible characters has indicator $1$. Evaluate (b) at the identity:
\[
\boxed{\#\{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_n:\sigma^2=e\}
=\sum_{\chi\text{ irreducible}}\chi(1).}
\]
For $n=4$, the degrees are $1,1,2,3,3$ and sum to $10$. There are also exactly $10$ permutations whose square is the identity: the identity, six transpositions and three products of two disjoint transpositions.

\tools Tensor products, symmetric and exterior squares, character orthogonality, and real inner products.

\leads The value $-1$ for $Q_8$ is not a defect of its character being real-valued. It marks a different kind of invariant form, and explains why quaternionic representations need special care.
\end{solution}
