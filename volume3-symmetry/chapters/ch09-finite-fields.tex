\chapter{Finite Fields}

\begin{center}\itshape
At its highest error-correction level, a QR code can restore roughly $30\%$ of its codewords.\\
How can a message repair itself?\\
The answer is polynomial arithmetic in a field with $256$ elements.
\end{center}

In the field $\mathbb F_2=\{0,1\}$, adding $1$ to $1$ gives $0$. There are only two elements. Could there be a field with four elements, or eight, or nine?

A first attempt with four elements is to take the classes of $0,1,X,1+X$ modulo $X^2+X+1$. In this ring,
\[
X^2=X+1
\]
because $-1=1$ in characteristic $2$. The polynomial $X^2+X+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_2$, so it is irreducible; the quotient is a field. We have built $\mathbb F_4$ using nothing but polynomial arithmetic.

This example raises three questions. Why must the size of every finite field be a prime power? Why is there, up to isomorphism, exactly one field of each such size? And why do its non-zero elements always form a cyclic multiplicative group?

The Frobenius map $x\mapsto x^p$ supplies a large part of the answer. It preserves addition because the intermediate binomial coefficients vanish in characteristic $p$. Its fixed points and iterates reveal the structure of all finite fields.

There is a practical reason to care. Send the values of a low-degree polynomial at several field elements rather than sending only its coefficients. If a few values are damaged, enough others still determine the polynomial. That is the idea behind Reed--Solomon error correction. We will derive the distance bound, then see how a linear system recovers the message.

\section{The characteristic and the Frobenius map}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:primefield}
Let $K$ be a finite field. There is a prime $p$, the \emph{characteristic} of $K$, such that $p\cdot1=0$ in $K$; the subring generated by $1$ is a field isomorphic to $\mathbb F_p=\Z/p\Z$, the \emph{prime field} of $K$; and $|K|=p^n$, where $n=[K:\mathbb F_p]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Begin with the ordinary integers inside $K$.
The map
\[
\eta:\mathbb Z\longrightarrow K,\qquad
m\longmapsto m\cdot1_K
\]
is a ring homomorphism. Because $\mathbb Z$
is infinite but $K$ is finite, it cannot
be injective. Its kernel is therefore
a non-zero ideal of $\mathbb Z$, of the form
$p\mathbb Z$ for some positive integer $p$.

The image $\eta(\mathbb Z)$ is a subring
of a field, so it has no zero divisors.
Also $1_K\ne0_K$, so $p\ne1$.
The first isomorphism theorem gives
\[
\eta(\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z.
\]
For this quotient to have no zero divisors,
$p$ must be prime: if $p=ab$ with
$1<a,b<p$, the non-zero residue classes
of $a$ and $b$ would multiply to zero.
Thus $p$ is prime. The equality
$p\cdot1_K=0_K$ and the minimality of
$p$ give the characteristic of $K$.

The image is the field $\mathbb F_p$,
the smallest subfield of $K$ because
every subfield contains $1_K$ and
its integer multiples. Now regard $K$
as a vector space over $\mathbb F_p$.
Its dimension $n$ is finite, since
the underlying set is finite.
A choice of basis identifies it as
a set with $\mathbb F_p^n$, which has
$p^n$ elements. Hence
\[
\boxed{|K|=p^n}.
\]
No finite field can have, for example,
six or ten elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Frobenius]\label{lem:frob}
Let $K$ be a field of characteristic $p$. Then $(a+b)^p=a^p+b^p$ for all $a,b\in K$, and $\varphi:x\mapsto x^p$ is an injective ring homomorphism $K\to K$ that fixes $\mathbb F_p$. If $K$ is finite, $\varphi$ is an automorphism of $K$, the \emph{Frobenius automorphism}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The binomial theorem gives
\[
(a+b)^p
=\sum_{j=0}^p\binom pj a^jb^{p-j}.
\]
For $0<j<p$, the prime $p$ divides
$\binom pj$: it appears in the numerator
$p!$ but not in the denominator
$j!(p-j)!$. In characteristic $p$,
all those intermediate terms vanish.
The two remaining terms are $a^p$
and $b^p$, proving
\[
\boxed{(a+b)^p=a^p+b^p}.
\]

Define $\varphi(x)=x^p$.
The displayed identity shows that
$\varphi$ preserves addition.
It also preserves multiplication,
since $(xy)^p=x^py^p$, and sends
$1$ to $1$. So $\varphi:K\to K$
is a ring homomorphism.

If $\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)$,
then $(x-y)^p=0$. A field has no
non-zero nilpotent elements, so
$x-y=0$. Thus $\varphi$ is injective.
For any $a\in\mathbb F_p$,
Fermat's theorem says $a^p=a$;
hence Frobenius fixes the prime
field pointwise.

Finally, when $K$ is finite,
every injective map from $K$ to itself
is surjective. Frobenius is then a
bijective ring homomorphism, that is,
an automorphism.

A useful consequence is obtained
by iterating:
\[
(a+b)^{p^r}=a^{p^r}+b^{p^r}
\]
for every $r\ge1$. We will use
this identity repeatedly.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:f4}
$X^2+X+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_2$, so it is irreducible (degree $2$), and $\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X+1)$ is a field with $4$ elements $0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1$, where $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$. Then $\alpha^3=\alpha^2+\alpha=1$: the group $\mathbb F_4^*=\{1,\alpha,\alpha^2\}$ is cyclic of order $3$. The ring $\Z/4\Z$ also has four elements, but it is not a field ($2\cdot2=0$); a field with $p^n$ elements is \emph{not} $\Z/p^n\Z$ for $n\ge2$.
\end{example}

Every finite field has a multiplicative group of non-zero elements. Rather than examine its multiplication table, we can use the fact that a polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots. That simple bound forces the whole group to be cyclic.

\section{The multiplicative group is cyclic}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cyclicmult}
Let $K$ be a field. Every finite subgroup $G$ of $K^*$ is cyclic. In particular, if $K$ is finite with $q$ elements, $K^*$ is cyclic of order $q-1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $G$ be a finite subgroup of
$K^*$, and put $m=|G|$.
Multiplication in a field is
commutative, so $G$ is an abelian group.
By the classification of finite
abelian groups (Corollary~\ref{cor:abelian}),
we can write
\[
G\cong
\mathbb Z/d_1\mathbb Z\oplus\cdots
\oplus\mathbb Z/d_r\mathbb Z,
\quad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
\]
with each $d_i>1$ (unless $G$ is
the trivial group, which is already cyclic).

Every element of this direct sum
has order dividing $d_r$: all the
coordinate orders divide $d_r$.
Consequently every $x\in G$
satisfies
\[
x^{d_r}=1.
\]
So all $m=d_1\cdots d_r$ elements
of $G$ are distinct roots in $K$
of the polynomial $X^{d_r}-1$.

A non-zero polynomial of degree $d_r$
over a field has at most $d_r$
distinct roots. Hence
\[
m\le d_r.
\]
But $m=d_1\cdots d_r\ge d_r$,
and if $r\ge2$ the inequality would
be strict, since the other factors
are greater than $1$. Thus $r=1$.
The group $G$ is cyclic.

For a finite field $K$ with $q$
elements, the non-zero elements
form a multiplicative group $K^*$
of order $q-1$. Applying the result
to this group shows
\[
\boxed{K^*\text{ is cyclic of order }q-1}.
\]
A generator is called a
\emph{primitive element} of the field.
\end{proof}

Here a \emph{primitive element} means a generator of $\mathbb F_q^*$,
a stronger condition than merely generating the field over $\mathbb F_p$.
For example, $X^2+1$ has no root over $\mathbb F_3$, so its quotient
is a field of nine elements. The class $i$ generates that field, but
$i^2=-1$ gives multiplicative order $4$, not $8$. If $\gamma$ is a multiplicative generator, every non-zero element is a power of $\gamma$, so $\mathbb F_q=\mathbb F_p(\gamma)\cong\mathbb F_p[X]/(m_\gamma)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:simple}): every finite field is obtained by Kronecker's construction. Multiplication in $\mathbb F_q^*$ is addition of exponents modulo $q-1$, as on the circle of Figure~\ref{fig:f16}; addition is addition of coordinates in $\mathbb F_p^n$. A finite field is the meeting point of these two very different pictures.

We know the size of a finite field must be a prime power, and we know its non-zero elements form a cyclic group. Now we must show that a field of each prime-power size exists and that two fields of the same size cannot have different multiplication laws up to isomorphism.

\section{Existence and uniqueness}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:xqx}
If $K$ is a field with $q$ elements, then $x^q=x$ for every $x\in K$, and
\[
X^q-X=\prod_{a\in K}(X-a)\quad\text{in }K[X].
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $K$ have $q$ elements.
Its non-zero elements form the group
$K^*$ of order $q-1$.
For any $x\ne0$, Lagrange's theorem
applied to that group says
\[
x^{q-1}=1.
\]
Multiplying by $x$ gives $x^q=x$.
The same equality holds for $x=0$,
so every element of $K$ is a root
of the polynomial $X^q-X$.

There are exactly $q$ distinct
elements of $K$, and the polynomial
$X^q-X$ is monic of degree $q$.
Each root $a$ supplies a linear
factor $X-a$. Since the roots are
distinct and the product of their
linear factors also has degree $q$,
we have
\[
\boxed{X^q-X=\prod_{a\in K}(X-a)}.
\]
There is no extra scalar factor,
because both sides are monic.

This identity will let us recognise
a finite field as the full set of
roots of one simple polynomial.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Galois, Moore]\label{thm:fq}
For every prime $p$ and $n\ge1$ there is a field with $q=p^n$ elements, and any two such fields are isomorphic: it is the splitting field of $X^q-X$ over $\mathbb F_p$. We denote it by $\mathbb F_q$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We must show both that a field of each required size exists and that two fields of the same size cannot have different multiplication laws.

\emph{Existence.} Fix $q=p^n$ and begin with the polynomial
\[
F(X)=X^q-X\in\mathbb F_p[X].
\]
Let $L$ be a splitting field of $F$ over $\mathbb F_p$, which exists by Proposition~\ref{prop:splitexists}. Thus $F$ splits completely into linear factors in $L$.

How many different roots does it have? Its formal derivative is
\[
F'(X)=qX^{q-1}-1=-1
\]
because $q=p^n$ vanishes in characteristic $p$. A repeated root of $F$ would also be a root of $F'$, which is impossible. Therefore $F$ has exactly $q$ \emph{distinct} roots in $L$.

Call their set $E$:
\[
E=\{x\in L:x^q=x\}.
\]
We claim that $E$ is a field. The Frobenius law in characteristic $p$ gives
\[
(x+y)^q=x^q+y^q,\qquad (xy)^q=x^qy^q.
\]
Hence the sum and product of two roots are roots again. The same argument with $-y$ gives closure under subtraction, and if $x\ne0$ satisfies $x^q=x$, then
\[
(x^{-1})^q=(x^q)^{-1}=x^{-1},
\]
so every non-zero root has its inverse in $E$. Finally $0,1\in E$. This proves all the field axioms by inheritance from $L$.

Every element of $\mathbb F_p$ satisfies $x^p=x$, and therefore $x^q=x$, so $\mathbb F_p\subseteq E$. Moreover, $E$ contains every root of $F$. Since $L$ is \emph{generated over $\mathbb F_p$ by those roots}, we have $L\subseteq E$, while $E\subseteq L$ by construction. Thus $E=L$ and
\[
|L|=|E|=q.
\]

\emph{Uniqueness.} Suppose $K$ is any field with $q=p^n$ elements. By Proposition~\ref{prop:primefield}, its characteristic is $p$, and it contains a prime subfield identified with $\mathbb F_p$. Every non-zero element of the multiplicative group $K^*$ has order dividing $q-1$, by Lagrange. Hence
\[
x^{q-1}=1\quad(x\in K^*),\qquad x^q=x\quad(x\in K).
\]
This is also Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}. In other words, all $q$ elements of $K$ are distinct roots of $X^q-X$, and the polynomial splits there. These roots generate all of $K$ over its prime subfield, because they \emph{are} all the elements of $K$. Therefore $K$ is a splitting field of the same polynomial over $\mathbb F_p$.

By the uniqueness theorem for splitting fields (Theorem~\ref{thm:splitunique}), the two fields are isomorphic. We may consequently write $\mathbb F_q$ without ambiguity up to isomorphism.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
The familiar identity $x^p=x$ holds for the $p$ elements of $\mathbb F_p$. To build a field with $p^n$ elements, we ask for all solutions of $x^{p^n}=x$. Two facts make this work. Its derivative is $-1$, so the polynomial has exactly $p^n$ distinct roots in its splitting field. And in characteristic $p$ the map $x\mapsto x^{p^n}$ preserves addition as well as multiplication, so the roots are closed under the field operations. Thus the roots form a field with exactly the desired size. Uniqueness comes from the reverse observation: every element of an existing field of this size solves the same polynomial.

The equation $x^{p^n}=x$ describes every element of $\mathbb F_{p^n}$. Applying the Frobenius map repeatedly reveals which elements lie in smaller fields, and which automorphisms can fix the prime field.

\section{Subfields and the Galois group}

We use twice that if $d\mid n$, then $a^d-1$ divides $a^n-1$, for integers and for polynomials alike: with $n=de$, $a^n-1=(a^d-1)(a^{d(e-1)}+\dots+a^d+1)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:subfields}
The subfields of $\mathbb F_{p^n}$ are the sets $\{x:x^{p^d}=x\}$ for $d\mid n$; there is exactly one subfield with $p^d$ elements for each divisor $d$ of $n$, and no other.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $F=\mathbb F_{p^n}$.

\emph{Every subfield has the stated form.}
Suppose $E\subseteq F$ is a subfield.
It contains the prime field $\mathbb F_p$,
and by Proposition~\ref{prop:primefield}
it has $p^d$ elements, where
$d=[E:\mathbb F_p]$.
The tower law gives
\[
n=[F:\mathbb F_p]=[F:E]\,[E:\mathbb F_p],
\]
so $d\mid n$. Every element of $E$
satisfies $x^{p^d}=x$
by Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}. Thus
\[
E\subseteq\{x\in F:x^{p^d}=x\}.
\]
The polynomial $X^{p^d}-X$ has
degree $p^d$, so it has at most
$p^d$ roots in $F$. Since $E$
already supplies $p^d$ of them,
the two sets are equal.

\emph{Every divisor really gives a subfield.}
Now fix $d\mid n$ and let
\[
E_d=\{x\in F:x^{p^d}=x\}.
\]
As $d\mid n$, the integer
$p^d-1$ divides $p^n-1$.
The identity
$X^{p^d-1}-1\mid X^{p^n-1}-1$
then yields
\[
X^{p^d}-X\mid X^{p^n}-X
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_p[X].
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}, the latter
polynomial splits completely in $F$
and has distinct roots.
Its factor $X^{p^d}-X$ therefore
also has $p^d$ distinct roots in $F$.

The map $\varphi^d:x\mapsto x^{p^d}$
is a field automorphism by Lemma~\ref{lem:frob}.
The set $E_d$ is its fixed-point set.
Fixed points are closed under addition,
multiplication, and inverses of
non-zero elements:
if $\varphi^d(x)=x$, then
$\varphi^d(x^{-1})=\varphi^d(x)^{-1}=x^{-1}$.
They also contain $0$ and $1$.
Thus $E_d$ is a subfield with
$p^d$ elements.

The first part shows that any
subfield with $p^d$ elements is
this exact set. Therefore there is
one and only one such subfield
for each divisor $d$ of $n$,
and no others.
\end{proof}

So the subfields of $\mathbb F_{p^n}$ form a copy of the lattice of divisors of $n$: $\mathbb F_{2^{12}}$ contains $\mathbb F_{2^4}$ and $\mathbb F_{2^6}$, which meet in $\mathbb F_{2^2}$, but no copy of $\mathbb F_8$ is a subfield of $\mathbb F_{16}$, since $3\nmid4$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:galfq}
The automorphisms of $\mathbb F_{p^n}$ form a cyclic group of order $n$, generated by the Frobenius automorphism $\varphi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $F=\mathbb F_{p^n}$ and let $\varphi(x)=x^p$ be Frobenius.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}, every $x\in F$ satisfies
$x^{p^n}=x$, so $\varphi^n=\mathrm{id}$.
No smaller positive power is the identity:
if $\varphi^k=\mathrm{id}$ for $1\le k<n$,
the polynomial $X^{p^k}-X$ of degree $p^k$
would vanish at all $p^n>p^k$ field elements.
That is impossible. Thus $\varphi$ has order $n$
and produces $n$ different automorphisms.

Every automorphism of $F$ fixes $1$,
hence all of $\mathbb F_p$. Also $F$
is the splitting field of $X^{p^n}-X$
over $\mathbb F_p$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:fq}.
The derivative of that polynomial is $-1$,
so its roots are all distinct.
The splitting-field automorphism theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:splitunique}) gives
exactly $[F:\mathbb F_p]=n$ automorphisms
fixing $\mathbb F_p$. We have already found
$n$ of them. Therefore they are precisely
\[
\boxed{\mathrm{id},\varphi,\ldots,\varphi^{n-1}},
\]
and the automorphism group is cyclic of order $n$.
\end{proof}

This is the first complete example of the Galois correspondence of Chapter~10: the subgroups of $\Z/n\Z$ (one for each $d\mid n$) match the subfields (one for each $d\mid n$), the subgroup $\langle\varphi^d\rangle$ fixing exactly $\mathbb F_{p^d}$.

Every element of $\mathbb F_{q^n}$ is a root of $X^{q^n}-X$. Factoring this polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$ lets us count irreducible polynomials by degree, just as the divisors of an integer help us count numbers with a given order.

\section{Counting irreducible polynomials}

\begin{think}
How many monic irreducible polynomials of degree $2$ are there over $\mathbb F_2$? Over $\mathbb F_3$? (A monic quadratic is reducible iff it has a root.) Can you guess a formula over $\mathbb F_q$?
\end{think}

We can also work relative to a finite field $\mathbb F_q$, where $q=p^m$.
For $n\ge1$, Theorem~\ref{thm:subfields} identifies a unique copy of
$\mathbb F_q$ inside $\mathbb F_{q^n}$, since $m\mid mn$, and the tower
law gives degree $n$. The map $x\mapsto x^q=\varphi^m(x)$ fixes exactly
that subfield. Its order is $n$: the $n$th power is the identity, while
a smaller positive power would violate the root bound for $X^{q^j}-X$.
The splitting-field count therefore shows that it generates all $n$
automorphisms fixing $\mathbb F_q$. The characteristic itself remains $p$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:xqnx}
For $n\ge1$, in $\mathbb F_q[X]$, $X^{q^n}-X$ is the product of all monic irreducible polynomials whose degree divides $n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The derivative of $F_n(X)=X^{q^n}-X$ is $-1$
in characteristic $p$, because $q$ is a power of $p$.
Thus $F_n$ is squarefree.

Let $P\in\mathbb F_q[X]$ be monic and irreducible
of degree $d$. Put $E=\mathbb F_q[X]/(P)$ and let
$\alpha$ be the class of $X$. Then $E$ has $q^d$
elements, $E=\mathbb F_q(\alpha)$, and
\[
P\mid F_n\iff \alpha^{q^n}=\alpha.
\]

If $d\mid n$, every element $x$ of $E$ satisfies
$x^{q^d}=x$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}.
Iterating this equality $n/d$ times gives
$\alpha^{q^n}=\alpha$, hence $P\mid F_n$.

Conversely suppose $P\mid F_n$.
The automorphism $\psi_n:x\mapsto x^{q^n}$
fixes $\alpha$ and every element of $\mathbb F_q$.
Since these generate $E$, it fixes all of $E$.
We also have $\psi_d=\mathrm{id}$.
Define $S=\{k\ge0:\psi_k=\mathrm{id}\}$.
The relation $\psi_{k+\ell}=\psi_k\circ\psi_\ell$
shows that $S$ is closed under differences
of non-negative elements: if $k\ge\ell$ both
belong to $S$, then $\psi_{k-\ell}=\mathrm{id}$.
Apply the Euclidean algorithm to $n,d$.
It shows $g=\gcd(n,d)\in S$.

Every one of the $q^d$ elements of $E$ is now
a root of $X^{q^g}-X$. A polynomial of degree
$q^g$ cannot have more than $q^g$ roots.
So $q^d\le q^g$, implying $d\le g$.
Since $g\le d$, we obtain $g=d$, or $d\mid n$.

Thus $P\mid F_n$ exactly when $\deg P\mid n$.
Since $F_n$ is monic and squarefree, it equals
the product of all these monic irreducibles,
each with multiplicity one.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gauss]\label{thm:countirr}
For $n\ge1$, let $N_q(n)$ be the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\mathbb F_q$. Then
\[
\sum_{d\mid n}d\,N_q(d)=q^n,\qquad N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,q^{n/d},
\]
and
\[
\Bigl|N_q(n)-\frac{q^n}n\Bigr|<\frac{2q^{n/2}}n.
\]
In particular $N_q(n)\ge1$ for every $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The factorisation of Theorem~\ref{thm:xqnx}
lists each monic irreducible polynomial of
degree dividing $n$ exactly once. Count degrees
on both sides of that factorisation:
\[
\boxed{\sum_{d\mid n}d\,N_q(d)=q^n}.
\]
The left side adds $d$ for each of the
$N_q(d)$ irreducibles of degree $d$;
the right side is the degree of $X^{q^n}-X$.

Put $f(d)=dN_q(d)$.
Then $q^n=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)$.
Möbius inversion from Volume~2 gives
\[
f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}.
\]
Divide by $n$ to obtain
\[
\boxed{N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}}.
\]

We now estimate the difference from the
leading term $q^n/n$. Separate the term $d=1$:
\[
nN_q(n)-q^n=
\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\d>1}}\mu(d)q^{n/d}.
\]
Every exponent $n/d$ in this remaining sum
is a distinct positive integer not exceeding
$\lfloor n/2\rfloor$.
Since $|\mu(d)|\le1$ and $q\ge2$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|nN_q(n)-q^n|
&\le\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}q^j\\
&=\frac{q(q^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-1)}{q-1}\\
&<\frac{q}{q-1}q^{n/2}
\le2q^{n/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For $n=1$ the first sum is empty and
the same strict bound holds directly.
Dividing by $n$ proves
\[
\boxed{\left|N_q(n)-\frac{q^n}{n}\right|
<\frac{2q^{n/2}}{n}}.
\]

Finally $N_q(1)=q>0$. For $n\ge2$
we have $q^{n/2}\ge2$, hence
$q^n-2q^{n/2}\ge0$.
The \emph{strict} lower bound just proved gives
\[
nN_q(n)>q^n-2q^{n/2}\ge0.
\]
So $N_q(n)$ is a positive integer
and in particular $N_q(n)\ge1$.

As a check, $n=2$ gives
$N_q(2)=(q^2-q)/2$, matching the
direct count of monic irreducible quadratics.
\end{proof}

So there are $(q^2-q)/2$ irreducible monic quadratics over $\mathbb F_q$: one over $\mathbb F_2$ ($X^2+X+1$) and three over $\mathbb F_3$ ($X^2+1$, $X^2+X+2$, $X^2+2X+2$). Directly: there are $q^2$ monic quadratics, of which $q$ are squares $(X-a)^2$ and $\binom q2$ are products $(X-a)(X-b)$ with $a\ne b$; $q^2-q-\frac{q(q-1)}2=\frac{q^2-q}2$.

\begin{remark}
Think of $q^n$, the number of monic polynomials of degree $n$, as ``$x$''. Then $n=\log_qx$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:countirr} says that the number of irreducibles of degree $n$ is $\frac x{\log_qx}$ up to an error of size $\sqrt x$: a prime number theorem for $\mathbb F_q[X]$, with an error term as small as the one the Riemann hypothesis would give for the primes (up to a logarithm). For polynomials the estimate is proved above. For integer primes, a corresponding square-root scale leads to the Riemann hypothesis, studied later in the series. Weil's theorem on curves over finite fields is a deep extension of this ``Riemann hypothesis over finite fields''.
\end{remark}

A finite field is a place where we can add, multiply and divide without leaving a finite alphabet. Evaluating a polynomial at several distinct field elements turns those operations into a way of detecting and correcting errors.

\section{Correcting errors: Reed--Solomon codes}

To send $k$ symbols $m_0,\dots,m_{k-1}\in\mathbb F_q$ over a noisy channel, encode them as the polynomial $f=\sum m_jX^j$ of degree $<k$, choose $n$ distinct points $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\mathbb F_q$ ($1\le k\le n\le q$), and send the \emph{codeword} $(f(a_1),\dots,f(a_n))$. The \emph{Hamming distance} between two words of $\mathbb F_q^n$ is the number of positions where they differ; it satisfies the triangle inequality, since if $u_i\ne w_i$ then $u_i\ne v_i$ or $v_i\ne w_i$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rsdist}
Two different codewords are at distance at least $n-k+1$. Consequently, if at most $t=\lfloor\frac{n-k}2\rfloor$ symbols are corrupted, the sent codeword is the unique codeword closest to the received word.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A codeword consists of the values of a polynomial
$f$ of degree less than $k$ at the distinct points
$a_1,\ldots,a_n$.

For two different polynomials $f,g$, their difference
$h=f-g$ is non-zero of degree at most $k-1$.
It has at most $k-1$ roots in a field, so the
codewords for $f$ and $g$ agree in at most $k-1$
positions. Thus their Hamming distance is at least
$\boxed{n-k+1}$.

Suppose $c$ was sent, while the received word $y$
has $e=d(c,y)\le t$ errors, with
$t=\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor$.
For any other codeword $c'\ne c$,
the triangle inequality gives
\[
d(c',y)\ge d(c',c)-d(c,y)
\ge n-k+1-e\ge n-k+1-t.
\]
Because $2t\le n-k$, the last number is greater
than $t\ge e$.
Therefore $d(c',y)>d(c,y)$ for every $c'\ne c$.
The sent codeword is uniquely closest to the
received word.

This proves the decoding radius, not yet an
algorithm for finding that codeword.
\end{proof}

Finding the closest codeword by trying all $q^k$ messages is hopeless when $q=256$ and $k=200$. Berlekamp and Welch found a way with linear algebra: an unknown \emph{error locator} polynomial $E$ vanishes at the bad positions, and $fE$ is then determined by a linear system.

\begin{theorem}[Berlekamp--Welch decoding]\label{thm:bw}
Let $y\in\mathbb F_q^n$ differ from the codeword of $f$ in at most $t=\lfloor\frac{n-k}2\rfloor$ positions. Then the linear system
\[
Q(a_i)=y_i\,E(a_i)\qquad(1\le i\le n),
\]
in the unknowns $E$ monic of degree $t$ and $Q$ of degree $<k+t$, has a solution, and every solution satisfies $Q=fE$. So $f$ is the quotient of $Q$ by $E$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We must prove two things: there really is a solution of the displayed linear system, and no solution can lead us to the wrong message.

\emph{A solution exists.} Let $B$ be the set of corrupted positions, of size $e\le t$. Choose the monic polynomial
\[
E(X)=X^{t-e}\prod_{i\in B}(X-a_i).
\]
Its degree is exactly $t$. It vanishes at every corrupted evaluation point. Put $Q=fE$, whose degree is less than $k+t$. At a good position, $y_i=f(a_i)$, so $Q(a_i)=y_iE(a_i)$. At a bad position, $E(a_i)=0$, so both sides of the same equation are zero. Thus $(E,Q)$ solves all $n$ equations.

\emph{Every solution gives the sent message.} Now let $(E,Q)$ be \emph{any} solution having the required degrees. We may not assume that its polynomial $E$ has the same roots as the one just constructed. Instead consider
\[
R=Q-fE.
\]
Its degree, unless it is zero, is less than $k+t$. At each of the at least $n-t$ good positions,
\[
R(a_i)=Q(a_i)-f(a_i)E(a_i)
      =y_iE(a_i)-f(a_i)E(a_i)=0.
\]
The evaluation points are distinct, and
\[
n-t\ge k+t
\]
because $2t\le n-k$. So a non-zero polynomial of degree less than $k+t$ would have at least $k+t$ distinct roots, which is impossible. Hence $R=0$, that is, $Q=fE$.

The polynomial $E$ is monic and therefore non-zero. Divide $Q$ by $E$ in $\mathbb F_q[X]$ to recover $f$. This is the unique message compatible with the stated error bound.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
The product $E(x)$ hides the corrupted positions by making them zeros. At a good position, $y_i=f(a_i)$, so the equation $Q(a_i)=y_iE(a_i)$ says $Q(a_i)=f(a_i)E(a_i)$. At a bad position, both sides vanish because $E(a_i)=0$. We solve for $Q$ and $E$ together using linear equations, even though the message polynomial $f$ is unknown. Why is the result trustworthy? For any candidate solution, $Q-fE$ vanishes at at least $n-t$ points and has degree less than $k+t$. The correction bound $2t\le n-k$ makes those points too numerous for a non-zero polynomial. Therefore every solution gives the same message $f=Q/E$.

The leading coefficient of $E$ is fixed to $1$, so the displayed equations
are linear in its remaining coefficients and those of $Q$. If fewer than
$t$ errors occurred, several pairs $(E,Q)$ may solve the system; the quotient
$f$ is nevertheless the same for all of them.

The system has $n$ equations and $k+2t\le n$ unknowns (the $t$ lower coefficients of $E$ and the $k+t$ coefficients of $Q$); Gaussian elimination solves it in $O(n^3)$ operations in $\mathbb F_q$, and faster algorithms (Berlekamp--Massey, Euclid's algorithm on polynomials) need $O(n^2)$. QR codes use Reed--Solomon arithmetic on byte symbols. Denso Wave gives
Level~H a restoration rate of about $30\%$ of the \emph{total codewords};
one codeword is eight bits. This is a codeword count, not a promise about
an arbitrary fraction of a damaged picture. The same source describes the
use of Reed--Solomon codes for music CDs. The same field, built as $\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^8+X^4+X^3+X+1)$, is the arithmetic of the AES cipher.

For the AES modulus $m=X^8+X^4+X^3+X+1$, its irreducibility can be
checked with the tools just proved. Repeated squaring modulo $m$ gives
\[
X^{256}\equiv X,\qquad X^{16}-X\equiv r=X^6+X^4+X^3+X^2.
\]
The exact polynomial identity
\[
(X^4+X+1)m+(X^6+X^4+1)r=1
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2[X]
\]
proves the gcd is $1$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:xqnx}, every irreducible factor
of $m$ has degree dividing $8$; the gcd excludes degrees $1,2,4$.
Since $\deg m=8$, it must itself be irreducible.

\begin{beauty}
A finite field is completely described by two numbers, $p$ and $n$, and yet it carries a rich structure: a cyclic multiplicative group, a cyclic Galois group generated by $x\mapsto x^p$, a lattice of subfields copying the divisors of $n$, and a count of irreducible polynomials that mirrors the distribution of primes. Galois discovered these ``imaginary'' fields in 1830, the year of his work on equations. Today they are everywhere in computing: error-correcting codes, AES, elliptic-curve cryptography, hashing, pseudo-random generators. The Frobenius automorphism, in turn, becomes in Volume~13 the bridge between primes and Galois groups (Frobenius elements, and Chebotarev's theorem).
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:f4tables}
Write the addition and multiplication tables of $\mathbb F_4$ (Example~\ref{ex:f4}). Show that $\mathbb F_4$ and $\Z/4\Z$ are not even isomorphic as additive groups, and that $\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+1)$ is not a field.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:irrF2}
List the monic irreducible polynomials of degree $\le4$ over $\mathbb F_2$, check the count of Theorem~\ref{thm:countirr}, and factor $X^{16}-X$ in $\mathbb F_2[X]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:primpoly}
An irreducible $P\in\mathbb F_p[X]$ is \emph{primitive} if the class of $X$ generates $(\mathbb F_p[X]/(P))^*$. Show that $X^4+X+1$ is primitive over $\mathbb F_2$, but $X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1$ is not. How many primitive elements does $\mathbb F_{16}$ have, and how many primitive polynomials of degree $4$ are there over $\mathbb F_2$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:minus1}
Let $q$ be odd. Show that $-1$ is a square in $\mathbb F_q$ iff $q\equiv1\pmod4$. Deduce that $-1$ is a square in $\mathbb F_9$, and find a square root of $-1$ in $\mathbb F_9=\mathbb F_3[X]/(X^2+X+2)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:twosq}
Show that every element of a finite field is a sum of two squares.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:powersums}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that for an integer $k\ge0$, $\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_q}x^k$ is $-1$ if $k\ge1$ and $(q-1)\mid k$, and $0$ otherwise (with $0^0=1$).
\item (Wilson) Show that the product of all non-zero elements of $\mathbb F_q$ is $-1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rs7}
Over $\mathbb F_7$, a message $f$ of degree $<2$ is sent as $(f(1),f(2),\dots,f(6))$, and $y=(5,3,2,4,0,1)$ is received. Assuming at most two errors, decode it with Theorem~\ref{thm:bw}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:chevalley}
(Chevalley--Warning.) Let $n\ge1$ and let $F\in\mathbb F_q[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ be nonzero with total degree $d<n$. Show that the number $N$ of zeros of $F$ in $\mathbb F_q^n$ is divisible by $p$, the characteristic. Handle the zero polynomial separately, and deduce that if $F(0)=0$, then $F$ has a non-zero zero; for instance, every quadratic form in $3$ variables over $\mathbb F_q$ has a non-trivial zero.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:fibmodp}
Let $p\ne2,5$ be a prime and $\left(\frac5p\right)$ the Legendre symbol. Let $(F_n)$ be the Fibonacci numbers.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $p$ divides $F_{p-(5/p)}$: so $p\mid F_{p-1}$ when $p\equiv\pm1\pmod5$, and $p\mid F_{p+1}$ when
$p\equiv\pm2\pmod5$.
\item Show that the period of $(F_n\bmod p)$ divides $p-1$ in the first case and $2(p+1)$ in the second.
\item Find the periods for $p=7$ and $p=11$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:pascal2}
Let $k,n\ge0$ be integers and let $s(n)$ be the number of $1$s in the binary expansion of $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\binom{2^k+m}j\equiv\binom mj+\binom m{j-2^k}\pmod2$ for $0\le m<2^k$ (for $j\ge0$, with $\binom mi=0$ outside $0\le i\le m$),
and explain the pattern of Figure~\ref{fig:pascal}.
\item Deduce that row $n$ of Pascal's triangle contains exactly $2^{s(n)}$ odd numbers.
\item Show that the rows $0,1,\dots,2^k-1$ contain $3^k$ odd numbers in all, and that the proportion of odd entries in them
tends to $0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:lucaslehmer}
\emph{The Lucas--Lehmer test.} Let $s_0=4$ and $s_{k+1}=s_k^2-2$. Let $p$ be an odd prime and $M_p=2^p-1$. Show that $M_p$
is prime if and only if $M_p\mid s_{p-2}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:moore}
\emph{The Moore determinant.} Let $q$ be a prime power, $n\ge0$, $m\ge1$, and $\Delta(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\det\bigl(x_i^{q^{j-1}}\bigr)_{1\le i,j\le n}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Moore, 1896.) Show that
\[
\Delta(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{i=1}^n\ \prod_{c\in\mathbb F_q^{i-1}}\bigl(x_i+c_1x_1+\dots+c_{i-1}x_{i-1}\bigr)
\]
in $\mathbb F_q[x_1,\dots,x_n]$.
\item Deduce that $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb F_{q^m}$ are linearly independent over $\mathbb F_q$ if and only if
$\Delta(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)\ne0$.
\item Let $V\subset\mathbb F_{q^m}$ be an $\mathbb F_q$-subspace. Show that $P_V(X)=\prod_{v\in V}(X-v)$ is a linear combination
of $X,X^q,X^{q^2},\dots$, so that $P_V(x+y)=P_V(x)+P_V(y)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:mseq}
\emph{Maximal length sequences.} Let $n\ge1$ and let $\alpha$ generate the cyclic group $\mathbb F_{2^n}^*$, $f=X^n+c_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\dots+c_0$ its
minimal polynomial over $\mathbb F_2$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\beta)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\beta^{2^j}$, and
$s_k=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^k)\in\mathbb F_2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $s_{k+n}=c_{n-1}s_{k+n-1}+\dots+c_0s_k$ for all $k$.
\item Show that the map $\beta\mapsto\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\beta),\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\alpha),\dots,\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\alpha^{n-1})\bigr)$ is a bijection
$\mathbb F_{2^n}\to\mathbb F_2^n$. Deduce that $(s_k)$ has period $2^n-1$, that every non-zero block of $n$ bits appears exactly
once among the windows $(s_k,\dots,s_{k+n-1})$, $0\le k<2^n-1$, and that every non-zero sequence satisfying the recurrence of (a)
is a shift of $(s_k)$.
\item Show that a period contains $2^{n-1}$ ones and $2^{n-1}-1$ zeros, and that for $0<t<2^n-1$,
$\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-2}(-1)^{s_k+s_{k+t}}=-1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:f4tables}
\idea Construct $\mathbb F_4$ from an irreducible quadratic over $\mathbb F_2$, and use its defining relation to reduce every sum and product to one of four basis elements. Write the complete tables rather than listing only a few products.

\writeup Let
\[
\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X+1)
\]
and write $\alpha=[X]$. The polynomial $X^2+X+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_2$, since its values at $0$ and $1$ are both $1$. Therefore the quotient is a field. Its four elements are
\[
0,\quad1,\quad\alpha,\quad\beta=1+\alpha.
\]
Because the characteristic is $2$, the defining equation gives
\[
\alpha^2=\alpha+1=\beta,\qquad
\alpha\beta=\alpha(\alpha+1)=\alpha^2+\alpha=1,\qquad
\beta^2=(\alpha+1)^2=\alpha^2+1=\alpha.
\]

Now calculate \emph{every} entry by addition in characteristic $2$ and these three identities:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$+$&$0$&$1$&$\alpha$&$\beta$\\\hline
$0$&$0$&$1$&$\alpha$&$\beta$\\
$1$&$1$&$0$&$\beta$&$\alpha$\\
$\alpha$&$\alpha$&$\beta$&$0$&$1$\\
$\beta$&$\beta$&$\alpha$&$1$&$0$
\end{tabular}
\qquad
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
$\cdot$&$0$&$1$&$\alpha$&$\beta$\\\hline
$0$&$0$&$0$&$0$&$0$\\
$1$&$0$&$1$&$\alpha$&$\beta$\\
$\alpha$&$0$&$\alpha$&$\beta$&$1$\\
$\beta$&$0$&$\beta$&$1$&$\alpha$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The multiplicative table confirms that all three non-zero elements are invertible: $1^{-1}=1$, $\alpha^{-1}=\beta$, and $\beta^{-1}=\alpha$. The addition table shows that every element is its own additive inverse, so
\[
(\mathbb F_4,+)\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2.
\]
But the additive group $(\Z/4\Z,+)$ has an element $1$ of order $4$. Group isomorphisms preserve element orders, so these two additive groups are not isomorphic. Consequently $\mathbb F_4$ cannot be the integers modulo $4$ with a different multiplication table.

Finally, consider the ring
\[
A=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+1).
\]
In characteristic $2$ the polynomial factors as
\[
X^2+1=(X+1)^2.
\]
The residue $u=[X+1]$ is non-zero because the degree-one polynomial $X+1$ is not a multiple of $(X+1)^2$. Yet $u^2=0$ in $A$. Thus $A$ has a non-zero zero divisor and cannot be a field. The irreducibility of the modulus, not merely its degree, was essential in constructing $\mathbb F_4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:irrF2}
\idea For degrees $2$ and $3$, a polynomial is irreducible exactly when it has no root in $\mathbb F_2$. In degree $4$ we must also rule out a factor of degree $2$. There is only one monic irreducible quadratic to test. Check the resulting list against the counting formula before forming the degree-$16$ product.

\writeup Over $\mathbb F_2$ a polynomial has a root at $0$ precisely when its constant coefficient vanishes, and a root at $1$ precisely when the number of non-zero coefficients is even.

The irreducible polynomials of degree $1$ are
\[
X,\qquad X+1.
\]
The only monic quadratic with neither root is $X^2+X+1$, so this is the sole irreducible quadratic. A cubic is reducible if and only if it has a root, which gives exactly
\[
X^3+X+1,\qquad X^3+X^2+1
\]
as irreducible cubics.

For degree $4$, the four monic polynomials with no root in $\mathbb F_2$ are
\[
\begin{gathered}
F_1=X^4+X+1,\qquad F_2=X^4+X^3+1,\\
F_3=X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1,\qquad
F_4=X^4+X^2+1.
\end{gathered}
\]
A root-free quartic is reducible only if it factors into irreducible quadratics; the only candidate factor is $P=X^2+X+1$. In the quotient by $P$, let $\alpha=X\bmod P$. Then $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, $\alpha^3=1$, and $\alpha^4=\alpha$. We obtain
\[
F_1(\alpha)=1,\quad
F_2(\alpha)=\alpha,\quad
F_3(\alpha)=\alpha+1,\quad
F_4(\alpha)=0.
\]
In fact $F_4=P^2$ in characteristic $2$. Thus the irreducible quartics are precisely $F_1,F_2,F_3$.

The counts in degrees $1,2,3,4$ are consequently $2,1,2,3$. The formula
\[
N_2(d)=\frac1d\sum_{e\mid d}\mu(e)\,2^{d/e}
\]
gives, independently,
\[
N_2(1)=2,\quad N_2(2)=\frac{4-2}{2}=1,\quad
N_2(3)=\frac{8-2}{3}=2,\quad
N_2(4)=\frac{16-4}{4}=3.
\]

Finally, Theorem~\ref{thm:xqnx} says that $X^{16}-X$ is the product of \emph{all} monic irreducibles whose degrees divide $4$, each appearing once. Degree $3$ therefore does not occur. The factorisation is
\[
\begin{aligned}
X^{16}-X={}&X(X+1)(X^2+X+1)\\
&{}\cdot(X^4+X+1)(X^4+X^3+1)\\
&{}\cdot(X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1).
\end{aligned}
\]
It is square-free since its formal derivative is $-1=1$ in characteristic $2$. The total degree is $1+1+2+4+4+4=16$, as expected.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:primpoly}
\idea There are $16-1=15$ non-zero elements of $\mathbb F_{16}$. An element is primitive if its multiplicative order is $15$. Since the prime divisors of $15$ are $3$ and $5$, it is enough to rule out orders dividing $3$ or $5$.

\writeup Let $P=X^4+X+1$. By Exercise~\ref{ex:irrF2}, $P$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_2$. Thus
\[
K=\mathbb F_2[X]/(P)
\]
is a field with $16$ elements. Write $x$ for the class of $X$. Its minimal polynomial has degree $4$, so $1,x,x^2,x^3$ form an $\mathbb F_2$-basis. In particular $x^3\ne1$.

The relation $P(x)=0$ gives $x^4=x+1$ in characteristic $2$, and hence
\[
x^5=x(x+1)=x^2+x\ne1
\]
by linear independence of $1,x,x^2$. By Lagrange, the order of $x$ divides $15$. Every proper divisor of $15$ is $1$, $3$, or $5$. The two inequalities $x^3\ne1$ and $x^5\ne1$ rule out all proper orders. Therefore $x$ has order $15$, and $P$ is primitive.

Now take $Q=X^4+X^3+X^2+X+1$. It is irreducible by the same previous exercise. In characteristic $2$,
\[
(X+1)Q=X^5+1=X^5-1.
\]
The class $y$ of $X$ modulo $Q$ therefore satisfies $y^5=1$. Since $y\ne1$ (otherwise its degree-four minimal polynomial would have root $1$), its order is exactly $5$, not $15$. So $Q$ is irreducible but \emph{not} primitive.

The group $\mathbb F_{16}^*$ is cyclic of order $15$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:cyclicmult}. The number of its generators is
\[
\varphi(15)=15(1-1/3)(1-1/5)=8.
\]
Every primitive element $\gamma$ has a minimal polynomial of degree $4$ over $\mathbb F_2$: it generates all of the multiplicative group and therefore all of the field. Its four Frobenius conjugates
\[
\gamma,\gamma^2,\gamma^4,\gamma^8
\]
are distinct and primitive; distinctness follows because the orbit under squaring has length $4$, and primitivity follows because each exponent $2^j$ is coprime to $15$.

Indeed, $\gamma^{2^j}=\gamma$ would require $15\mid2^j-1$, which first
occurs at $j=4$. Applying Frobenius to a polynomial equation with
coefficients in $\mathbb F_2$ shows that each of these four elements
is a root of the minimal polynomial of $\gamma$.

Those four conjugates have the same degree-four minimal polynomial, and no primitive element outside the orbit has that polynomial. The $8$ primitive elements therefore split into $8/4=2$ disjoint orbits. Hence there are
\[
\boxed{8\text{ primitive elements and }2\text{ primitive quartics}}
\]
over $\mathbb F_2$. They are $X^4+X+1$ and $X^4+X^3+1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:minus1}
\idea In a cyclic multiplicative group of even order, the unique element of order $2$ is an even power of a generator exactly when half the group order is even. Then compute directly in the chosen quotient model of $\mathbb F_9$.

\writeup Let $q$ be odd. The multiplicative group $\mathbb F_q^*$ is cyclic of order $q-1$; choose a generator $\gamma$. Because $q-1$ is even,
\[
\gamma^{(q-1)/2}
\]
has order exactly $2$. The polynomial $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$ has exactly two roots in a field of odd characteristic, namely $1$ and $-1$. Thus $-1$ is the unique element of order $2$ and
\[
-1=\gamma^{(q-1)/2}.
\]

The non-zero squares are precisely the even powers $\gamma^{2j}$. An exponent modulo $q-1$ has well-defined parity because $q-1$ is even. Therefore $-1$ is a square if and only if $(q-1)/2$ is even, or equivalently
\[
\boxed{-1\text{ is a square in }\mathbb F_q\iff q\equiv1\pmod4}.
\]
This depends on $q$, not just the characteristic prime: $-1$ is a non-square in $\mathbb F_3$ but a square in $\mathbb F_9$.

For an explicit square root in
\[
\mathbb F_9=\mathbb F_3[X]/(X^2+X+2),
\]
put $\alpha=[X]$. The defining equation gives $\alpha^2=-\alpha-2=2\alpha+1$ in characteristic $3$. Compute
\[
(\alpha+2)^2=\alpha^2+4\alpha+4
=(2\alpha+1)+\alpha+1
=3\alpha+2=2=-1.
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{\alpha+2\quad\text{and}\quad-(\alpha+2)=2\alpha+1}
\]
are the two square roots of $-1$ in $\mathbb F_9$. They are distinct because the characteristic is odd.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twosq}
\idea The two sets of possible values $x^2$ and $a-y^2$ must overlap if their sizes add to more than the number $q$ of field elements. The size of the set of squares depends on whether the characteristic is $2$ or odd.

\writeup Let $K=\mathbb F_q$ and fix $a\in K$. We seek $x,y\in K$ satisfying
\[
x^2+y^2=a.
\]

If $q$ is even, then $q=2^n$. The Frobenius map $x\mapsto x^2$ is injective because $x^2=y^2$ implies $(x-y)^2=0$, hence $x=y$ in a field. An injective self-map of a finite set is bijective. So there exists $x$ with $x^2=a$; simply choose $y=0$.

Now suppose $q$ is odd. If $x^2=y^2$, then
\[
(x-y)(x+y)=0
\]
forces $x=y$ or $x=-y$. For $x\ne0$, these are two different elements because the characteristic is not $2$. The map $x\mapsto x^2$ is therefore two-to-one on $K^*$, so there are $(q-1)/2$ non-zero squares. Including $0$, the set
\[
S=\{x^2:x\in K\}
\]
has exactly $(q+1)/2$ elements.

Consider also
\[
T=\{a-y^2:y\in K\}=a-S.
\]
Translation by $a$ and negation are bijections, so $|T|=|S|=(q+1)/2$. Both lie inside the $q$-element set $K$. Since
\[
|S|+|T|=q+1>q,
\]
the pigeonhole principle gives $S\cap T\ne\varnothing$. Pick $z\in S\cap T$. By definition, $z=x^2=a-y^2$ for suitable $x,y\in K$, and hence $a=x^2+y^2$.

The argument works for \emph{every} $a$ and \emph{every} finite field, not just prime fields. It proves existence; it does not count the number of representations of each $a$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:powersums}
\idea The non-zero elements form a cyclic group of order $q-1$. Sum their $k$-th powers as a geometric progression. For the product identity, either pair each element with its inverse or compare the constant term of the polynomial having all non-zero field elements as roots.

\writeup Let $K=\mathbb F_q$ and choose a generator $\gamma$ of $K^*$, which has order $q-1$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:cyclicmult}.

\emph{(a) Power sums.} First take $k=0$. With the convention $0^0=1$, every term in
\[
\sum_{x\in K}x^0
\]
equals $1$, so the sum is $q\cdot1=0$ \emph{as an element of $K$}, since the characteristic is $p$ and $q=p^n$ vanishes in $K$.

Now let $k\ge1$. The term from $x=0$ equals $0$. Every non-zero field element is a unique power $\gamma^j$ with $0\le j\le q-2$, so
\[
\sum_{x\in K}x^k
=\sum_{j=0}^{q-2}(\gamma^k)^j.
\]
If $(q-1)\mid k$, then $\gamma^k=1$, every term equals $1$, and the sum is $q-1=-1$ in $K$.

If $(q-1)\nmid k$, then $\gamma^k\ne1$ and the finite geometric-series identity applies:
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{q-2}(\gamma^k)^j
=\frac{(\gamma^k)^{q-1}-1}{\gamma^k-1}
=\frac{1-1}{\gamma^k-1}=0.
\]
The denominator is non-zero precisely because $\gamma^k\ne1$. Hence
\[
\boxed{\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_q}x^k=
\begin{cases}
-1,&k\ge1\text{ and }(q-1)\mid k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}}
\]
where both values are interpreted inside the field.

\emph{(b) Wilson's product identity.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:xqx}, the polynomial $X^q-X$ has every element of $K$ as a root, and all are distinct. Dividing out the root $0$ gives
\[
X^{q-1}-1=\prod_{a\in K^*}(X-a).
\]
Compare the constant coefficients:
\[
-1=(-1)^{q-1}\prod_{a\in K^*}a.
\]
If $q$ is odd, then $q-1$ is even, and the product equals $-1$. If $q$ is even, the field has characteristic $2$ and $-1=1$, so the product again equals $-1$. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\prod_{a\in\mathbb F_q^*}a=-1}.
\]
For a prime field, this reduces to Wilson's congruence $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. It continues to hold in all finite extension fields, where the expression $(q-1)!$ would not be the right interpretation of the product.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rs7}
\idea A polynomial of degree less than $2$ can be written $f(X)=aX+b$. Berlekamp--Welch hides the two incorrect positions inside a monic degree-two polynomial $E$, and solves the linear equations for $E$ and $Q=fE$. Check the recovered pair at all six positions, including the errors.

\writeup Everything is computed in $\mathbb F_7$. The code evaluates a message polynomial of degree less than $2$ at the six distinct points $1,2,\ldots,6$. Its minimum distance is $6-2+1=5$, so up to
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{5-1}{2}\right\rfloor=2
\]
errors can be corrected uniquely.

We receive
\[
y=(5,3,2,4,0,1).
\]
Set
\[
E(X)=X^2+e_1X+e_0,\qquad
Q(X)=q_3X^3+q_2X^2+q_1X+q_0.
\]
The six equations
\[
Q(j)=y_jE(j),\qquad j=1,\ldots,6,
\]
are linear in the six unknown coefficients $e_0,e_1,q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3$. Gaussian elimination modulo $7$ gives the candidate
\[
E(X)=X^2+3,\qquad
Q(X)=2X^3+3X^2+6X+2.
\]

Rather than trusting the elimination without a check, divide:
\[
Q(X)=(2X+3)(X^2+3)
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_7[X],
\]
since the product is $2X^3+3X^2+6X+9$ and $9=2$ modulo $7$. Thus the recovered message is
\[
\boxed{f(X)=2X+3}.
\]

Check all six equations:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{c|rrrrrr}
$j$&$1$&$2$&$3$&$4$&$5$&$6$\\\hline
$y_j$&5&3&2&4&0&1\\
$E(j)$&4&0&5&5&0&4\\
$Q(j)=y_jE(j)$&6&0&3&6&0&4\\
$f(j)$&5&0&2&4&6&1
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In particular $E(2)=E(5)=0$, while
\[
E(X)=X^2+3=(X-2)(X-5)
\quad\text{modulo }7.
\]
These are precisely the erroneous positions: the second received symbol $3$ should be $0$, and the fifth received symbol $0$ should be $6$. The other four received symbols agree with the decoded polynomial.

Theorem~\ref{thm:bw} proves that any candidate satisfying these equations and the degree bound yields the \emph{unique} message compatible with at most two errors. The explicit table verifies that our candidate satisfies the required equations, so the decoding is complete.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:chevalley}
\idea In a finite field, the expression $1-y^{q-1}$ detects whether $y$ is zero. Add this indicator over the whole grid. The degree assumption ensures that each monomial disappears when summed independently in all variables.

\writeup Write $q=p^r$ with $p$ prime and let $K=\mathbb F_q$. For any $y\in K$,
\[
1-y^{q-1}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }y=0,\\0&\text{if }y\ne0.\end{cases}
\]
The second case follows from the fact that $K^*$ has order $q-1$. In this way the integer $N$ of zeros of $F$ can be counted inside $K$:
\[
N\cdot1_K=\sum_{x\in K^n}(1-F(x)^{q-1})
=q^n\cdot1_K-\sum_{x\in K^n}F(x)^{q-1}.
\]
For the zero polynomial, $N=q^n$ is divisible by $p$ because $n\ge1$.
For the stated nonzero polynomial, $K$ has characteristic $p$, so the term
$q^n\cdot1_K$ is zero. It remains to show that the last sum also vanishes.

Expand $F^{q-1}$ into monomials
\[
c\,X_1^{e_1}\cdots X_n^{e_n}.
\]
Every exponent is non-negative, and the total degree obeys
\[
e_1+\cdots+e_n\le d(q-1)<n(q-1).
\]
Consequently at least one index $j$ has $e_j<q-1$. The sum of this monomial over all tuples $x\in K^n$ factors into one-variable sums:
\[
\sum_{x\in K^n}c\,x_1^{e_1}\cdots x_n^{e_n}
=c\prod_{i=1}^n\left(\sum_{t\in K}t^{e_i}\right).
\]
The $j$-th factor is zero. When $e_j=0$, it equals $q\cdot1_K=0$. For $0<e_j<q-1$, use the power-sum result of Exercise~\ref{ex:powersums}, or note that multiplication by a generator $a$ of $K^*$ multiplies the sum by $a^{e_j}\ne1$, forcing the sum to be zero.

Every monomial therefore contributes zero. Returning to the indicator calculation gives
\[
N\cdot1_K=0.
\]
An integer maps to zero in a field of characteristic $p$ exactly when it is a multiple of $p$. Thus
\[
\boxed{p\mid N}.
\]
The statement does not assert that $q\mid N$ when $q$ is a proper prime power.

If $F(0)=0$, the zero vector already counts as one solution, so $N\ge1$. Since $N$ is a positive multiple of $p$, we have $N\ge p\ge2$. There must be at least one other, non-zero solution. A quadratic form in three variables has total degree at most $2<3$ and vanishes at $0$, so this conclusion applies.

For a check, consider $F(X,Y)=X+Y$ with $n=2$ and $d=1$. Each $X$ has exactly one corresponding $Y=-X$, giving $N=q$, which is indeed divisible by $p$.

\tools Finite-field multiplicative groups, Frobenius, and Exercise~\ref{ex:powersums}.

\leads The key was not to solve a polynomial equation explicitly. The small total degree made the total number of solutions obey a divisibility law, strong enough to guarantee a non-zero solution.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:fibmodp}
\idea We need a version of Binet's formula that works modulo $p$. The two roots of $X^2-X-1$ either lie in $\mathbb F_p$ or are exchanged by Frobenius in $\mathbb F_{p^2}$. Those two behaviours account for both possible period bounds.

\writeup Write $\varepsilon=(5/p)\in\{1,-1\}$; this is defined because $p\ne2,5$. In a splitting field $K$ of $X^2-X-1$, let its two roots be $\alpha,\beta$. Its discriminant is $5\ne0$, so $\alpha\ne\beta$. Viète's relations give
\[
\alpha+\beta=1,\qquad \alpha\beta=-1.
\]
In particular both roots are non-zero. The sequence
\[
U_n=\frac{\alpha^n-\beta^n}{\alpha-\beta}
\]
satisfies $U_0=0$, $U_1=1$, and $U_{n+2}=U_{n+1}+U_n$: verify the recurrence separately for $\alpha^n$ and $\beta^n$ using $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$ and $\beta^2=\beta+1$. These initial values uniquely define the Fibonacci sequence, so $F_n=U_n$ in $K$.

\emph{Case $\varepsilon=1$: the discriminant is a square.}
The two roots lie in $\mathbb F_p^*$. Fermat's theorem gives $\alpha^{p-1}=\beta^{p-1}=1$. Thus for every $n\ge0$,
\[
F_{n+p-1}=\frac{\alpha^{n+p-1}-\beta^{n+p-1}}
                  {\alpha-\beta}=F_n.
\]
In particular $F_{p-1}=F_0=0$ in $\mathbb F_p$.

\emph{Case $\varepsilon=-1$: the discriminant is not a square.}
The polynomial is irreducible and $K=\mathbb F_{p^2}$. Frobenius $x\mapsto x^p$ permutes its roots. It cannot fix either one, since the fixed elements of this automorphism are exactly $\mathbb F_p$. Therefore
\[
\alpha^p=\beta,\qquad\beta^p=\alpha.
\]
Multiplying by $\alpha$ or $\beta$, respectively, gives
\[
\alpha^{p+1}=\beta^{p+1}=\alpha\beta=-1.
\]
Consequently $F_{p+1}=0$, and
\[
F_{n+2(p+1)}
=\frac{(-1)^2\alpha^n-(-1)^2\beta^n}{\alpha-\beta}
=F_n.
\]
So in both cases
\[
\boxed{p\mid F_{p-\varepsilon}}.
\]

\emph{Why the least period divides the stated bound.}
A repetition of individual terms does not automatically prove a period from the beginning. Here we have proved the equality for \emph{every} $n\ge0$. Equivalently the state pair $(F_n,F_{n+1})$ repeats after the given bound. Its transition
\[
T(u,v)=(v,u+v)
\]
is invertible over $\mathbb F_p$, with inverse $T^{-1}(u,v)=(v-u,u)$. Hence the states follow a cycle from their first step. The set of return times to $(0,1)$ is exactly the set of multiples of its least positive return time. The least period therefore divides $p-1$ for $\varepsilon=1$, and $2(p+1)$ for $\varepsilon=-1$.

Quadratic reciprocity supplies the familiar congruence cases. Since $5\equiv1\pmod4$, we have $(5/p)=(p/5)$. The non-zero squares modulo $5$ are $1,4$, so $\varepsilon=1$ when $p\equiv\pm1\pmod5$, and $\varepsilon=-1$ when $p\equiv\pm2\pmod5$.

\emph{The two requested periods.}
For $p=7$, $\varepsilon=-1$, so the least period divides $16$. The states at times $8$ and $16$ are
\[
(F_8,F_9)=(21,34)\equiv(0,6)\pmod7,
\qquad
(F_{16},F_{17})=(987,1597)\equiv(0,1)\pmod7.
\]
The period cannot divide $8$, so it must be $16$. For $p=11$, $\varepsilon=1$, so the period divides $10$. The states at times $2$ and $5$ are $(1,2)$ and $(5,8)$, neither equal to $(0,1)$ modulo $11$. No proper divisor of $10$ remains possible. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\pi(7)=16,\qquad\pi(11)=10}.
\]

\tools Fibonacci numbers from Volume~1, quadratic reciprocity from Volume~2, and finite-field Frobenius from Chapter~9.

\leads The same recurrence can behave differently modulo different primes. The arithmetic reason is whether its characteristic polynomial splits or stays irreducible over the prime field.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:pascal2}
\idea A row of Pascal's triangle records the coefficients of $(1+X)^n$. Over $\mathbb F_2$, squaring a sum erases its mixed term. Repeated squaring then makes the triangle reproduce itself in two disjoint copies.

\writeup \emph{(a) Two copies of a smaller row.} In characteristic two,
\[
(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2.
\]
Apply this identity $k$ times, starting from $1+X$:
\[
(1+X)^{2^k}=1+X^{2^k}\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2[X].
\]
Multiplying by $(1+X)^m$ for $0\le m<2^k$ gives
\[
(1+X)^{2^k+m}
=(1+X)^m+X^{2^k}(1+X)^m.
\]
The coefficient of $X^j$ on the left is $\binom{2^k+m}{j}$; on the right it is the sum of the coefficients of $X^j$ and $X^{j-2^k}$ in $(1+X)^m$. Thus
\[
\boxed{\binom{2^k+m}j\equiv
\binom mj+\binom m{j-2^k}\pmod2.}
\]
As usual, a binomial coefficient with an index outside $0,\ldots,m$ is zero. The two copies cannot overlap, because one is supported on degrees $0,\ldots,m$ and the other on $2^k,\ldots,2^k+m$. This accounts for the triangular gaps in Figure~\ref{fig:pascal}.

\emph{(b) Count the odd entries in one row.}
Let $N(n)$ count the odd binomial coefficients in row $n$. The disjoint-copy argument gives
\[
N(2^k+m)=2N(m)\qquad(0\le m<2^k).
\]
Removing the leading $1$ from the binary representation of $n$ therefore halves the count. Repeating until no one-bits remain, and using $N(0)=1$, yields
\[
\boxed{N(n)=2^{s(n)}}.
\]
For example, $13=1101_2$ has three one-bits; row $13$ contains $2^3=8$ odd entries.

There is another way to see it. If $n=\sum_j\varepsilon_j2^j$, then
\[
(1+X)^n=\prod_{\varepsilon_j=1}(1+X^{2^j})
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2[X].
\]
Each factor offers a choice of either $1$ or $X^{2^j}$. Distinct choices give distinct sums of powers of two, so no coefficient can cancel. There are exactly $2^{s(n)}$ choices.

\emph{(c) Count the odd entries in a whole triangle.}
Among rows $0,\ldots,2^k-1$, each row corresponds to one binary word $(\varepsilon_0,\ldots,\varepsilon_{k-1})$. Hence the total number of odd entries is
\[
\sum_{n=0}^{2^k-1}2^{s(n)}
=\sum_{\varepsilon_0,\ldots,\varepsilon_{k-1}\in\{0,1\}}
\prod_j2^{\varepsilon_j}
=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1+2)
=\boxed{3^k}.
\]
The total number of entries in those rows is
\[
1+2+\cdots+2^k=\frac{2^k(2^k+1)}2.
\]
Thus the fraction of odd entries is
\[
\frac{3^k}{2^{k-1}(2^k+1)}
\le 2\left(\frac34\right)^k\longrightarrow0.
\]
So odd entries persist at every scale, even though their proportion tends to zero.

\tools Binomial coefficients from Volume~1 and repeated squaring in characteristic two from Chapter~9.

\leads Binary arithmetic turns Pascal's triangle into a self-repeating pattern. Its exact count, $3^k$, gives a rigorous measure of how sparse that pattern becomes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:lucaslehmer}
\idea The update $s\mapsto s^2-2$ is the doubling law for $u+u^{-1}$. If the Lucas--Lehmer value vanishes modulo a prime divisor of $2^p-1$, it forces a unit of very large order in a small finite ring. That observation proves one direction. The other uses quadratic reciprocity and Frobenius.

\writeup Put $M=2^p-1$ and let
\[
\omega=2+\sqrt3,\qquad
\omega^{-1}=2-\sqrt3.
\]
We have $\omega\omega^{-1}=1$ and $\omega+\omega^{-1}=4=s_0$. The identity
\[
(u+u^{-1})^2-2=u^2+u^{-2}
\]
then proves by induction that
\[
\boxed{s_k=\omega^{2^k}+\omega^{-2^k}}.
\]
This is an algebraic identity over $\mathbb Z[\sqrt3]$, so it may be reduced modulo any integer.

\emph{First suppose $M\mid s_{p-2}$.}
Choose a prime divisor $q$ of $M$. It is odd, and $q\ne3$, because for odd $p$ we have $M=2^p-1\equiv(-1)^p-1\equiv1\pmod3$.

Use the ring
\[
R=\mathbb F_q[T]/(T^2-3).
\]
It contains $q^2$ elements whether or not $T^2-3$ is irreducible. Denote the class of $T$ by $\sqrt3$. Since $(2+\sqrt3)(2-\sqrt3)=1$, the element $\omega=2+\sqrt3$ is a unit of $R$.

The divisibility assumption makes $s_{p-2}$ zero in $R$. From the displayed formula,
\[
\omega^{2^{p-2}}+\omega^{-2^{p-2}}=0.
\]
Multiplying by the unit $\omega^{2^{p-2}}$ yields
\[
\omega^{2^{p-1}}=-1.
\]
Because $q$ is odd, $-1\ne1$ in $R$. On squaring, $\omega^{2^p}=1$. Its multiplicative order must therefore be exactly $2^p$: the order divides $2^p$ but not $2^{p-1}$.

By Lagrange's theorem in the finite group of units $R^*$,
\[
2^p\le |R^*|\le q^2-1.
\]
If $M$ were composite, its smallest prime factor would satisfy $q^2\le M=2^p-1$. Then
\[
2^p\le q^2-1\le2^p-2,
\]
an impossibility. Thus $M$ must be prime.

\emph{Conversely suppose $M$ is prime.}
Since $p$ is odd and at least $3$,
\[
M\equiv7\pmod8,\qquad
M\equiv1\pmod3,\qquad
M\equiv3\pmod4.
\]
The first congruence gives $(2/M)=1$ by the supplementary quadratic-reciprocity law. The other two, together with reciprocity, give
\[
\left(\frac3M\right)=-\left(\frac M3\right)=-1.
\]
So $T^2-3$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_M$, and
\[
K=\mathbb F_M(\sqrt3)
\]
is a field of order $M^2$.

Euler's criterion gives $3^{(M-1)/2}=-1$ and $2^{(M-1)/2}=1$ in $\mathbb F_M$. Therefore Frobenius sends
\[
(\sqrt3)^M=\sqrt3\,3^{(M-1)/2}=-\sqrt3.
\]
Set $\tau=1+\sqrt3$. We have
\[
\tau^M=1-\sqrt3,\qquad
\tau^{M+1}=(1+\sqrt3)(1-\sqrt3)=-2,
\]
and
\[
\tau^2=4+2\sqrt3=2\omega.
\]
Since $(M+1)/2=2^{p-1}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\omega^{2^{p-1}}
&=\left(\frac{\tau^2}{2}\right)^{(M+1)/2}\\
&=\frac{\tau^{M+1}}{2^{(M+1)/2}}\\
&=\frac{-2}{2\cdot2^{(M-1)/2}}=-1.
\end{aligned}
\]
The division is legitimate in $K$, since $M$ is odd. Multiply the identity
$\omega^{2^{p-1}}+1=0$ by the invertible $\omega^{-2^{p-2}}$ to get
\[
\omega^{2^{p-2}}+\omega^{-2^{p-2}}=0.
\]
The left side is $s_{p-2}$, so this integer is divisible by $M$. Both directions together prove
\[
\boxed{M_p\text{ is prime}\iff M_p\mid s_{p-2}}
\qquad(p\text{ an odd prime}).
\]
For $p=3$, the sequence begins $4,14,\ldots$ and $M_3=7$ divides $s_1=14$, as it should.

\tools Quadratic reciprocity and Euler's criterion (Volume~2), finite quotient rings and Frobenius (this volume).

\leads The test turns primality into the existence of an element of forced order. The finite-ring argument explains why a composite Mersenne number cannot pass it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:moore}
\idea The Moore determinant behaves like an ordinary Vandermonde determinant, except that dependence is measured over $\mathbb F_q$ rather than by equality of two entries. The Frobenius map $x\mapsto x^q$ turns a linear relation over $\mathbb F_q$ into a relation between complete rows.

\writeup \emph{(a) Factor the determinant.}
For $n=0$, the empty determinant and empty product are both $1$.
For $n=1$ the determinant is $\Delta(x_1)=x_1$, exactly as the claimed product says. Assume the formula is known for $n-1$. Regard
\[
D(X)=\Delta(x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1},X)
\]
as a polynomial in $X$ with coefficients in the rational-function field $K=\mathbb F_q(x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1})$. Here the $x_j$ are independent indeterminates: a polynomial in them is zero only when every coefficient is zero. This field is the fraction field of the polynomial domain $\mathbb F_q[x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}]$, constructed as in Chapter~6. Expanding the determinant along the last row shows that its highest power of $X$ is $X^{q^{n-1}}$, with coefficient $\Delta(x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1})$. This coefficient is non-zero by the induction hypothesis.

For every $c=(c_1,\ldots,c_{n-1})\in\mathbb F_q^{n-1}$, substitute
\[
X=-c_1x_1-\cdots-c_{n-1}x_{n-1}.
\]
Because $c_j^{q^t}=c_j$ and Frobenius respects addition,
\[
X^{q^t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_jx_j^{q^t}.
\]
The last row of the Moore matrix is now a linear combination of its earlier rows, so $D(X)=0$ at this value. The $q^{n-1}$ chosen values are distinct in $K$, because the $x_j$ are independent indeterminates. We have found as many distinct roots as the degree of $D$. Therefore
\[
D(X)=\Delta(x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1})
\prod_{c\in\mathbb F_q^{n-1}}
\left(X+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_jx_j\right).
\]
The leading coefficient determines the constant factor exactly. Apply the induction hypothesis to the smaller determinant. Both sides are polynomials over $\mathbb F_q$, so the identity established in $K[X]$ is the desired polynomial identity.

\emph{(b) Detect independence.}
Substitute $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb F_{q^m}$ into the product from (a). The determinant vanishes exactly when at least one factor
\[
\alpha_i+c_1\alpha_1+\cdots+c_{i-1}\alpha_{i-1}
\]
vanishes. Such an equation says that $\alpha_i$ belongs to the span of its predecessors. If the family is dependent, take the first index at which this happens. Conversely, an equation of that form is a dependence relation. Hence
\[
\boxed{\Delta(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)\ne0
\iff \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n
\text{ are linearly independent over }\mathbb F_q.}
\]

\emph{(c) Construct an additive polynomial.}
Let $V\subseteq\mathbb F_{q^m}$ have dimension $d$ and choose a basis $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d$ (when $d=0$, simply take $P_V(X)=X$). By (b), $\Delta(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\ne0$. The factorisation in (a), with $X$ as the last entry, becomes
\[
\Delta(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d,X)
=\Delta(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\prod_{v\in V}(X+v).
\]
Since $V=-V$, the product is $P_V(X)=\prod_{v\in V}(X-v)$. On the other hand, expanding this determinant along its last row expresses it as a linear combination of
\[
X,\ X^q,\ X^{q^2},\ldots,X^{q^d}.
\]
Dividing by the non-zero scalar $\Delta(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)$ therefore gives
\[
P_V(X)=a_0X+a_1X^q+\cdots+a_dX^{q^d}.
\]
Each power $X^{q^j}$ is additive in characteristic $p$, so
\[
\boxed{P_V(x+y)=P_V(x)+P_V(y).}
\]
In fact $P_V(cx)=cP_V(x)$ for $c\in\mathbb F_q$ as well.

For a small example, $V=\mathbb F_q$ has $P_V(X)=X^q-X$, already an additive polynomial.

\tools Determinants (Volume~1), Frobenius (Chapter~9), and roots of polynomials over a field.

\leads The determinant turns linear dependence into a polynomial equation. The same identity produces the linearised polynomials used in rank-metric coding.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:mseq}
\idea The generator $\alpha$ runs through every non-zero element of the finite field. The trace is a linear test taking values in $\mathbb F_2$. Our bit sequence simply applies that test to successive powers of $\alpha$, so field arithmetic describes the entire cycle of bit windows.

\writeup Before using the trace as a bit, verify its values. Squaring gives
\[
\operatorname{Tr}(\beta)^2-\operatorname{Tr}(\beta)
=\beta^{2^n}-\beta=0.
\]
The roots of $X^2-X$ are exactly $0$ and $1$, so the trace lies in
$\mathbb F_2$. We identify these values with the bits $0,1$.

\emph{(a) Derive the recurrence.}
The trace is additive because Frobenius is additive in characteristic $2$:
\[
\operatorname{Tr}(x+y)
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(x+y)^{2^j}
=\operatorname{Tr}(x)+\operatorname{Tr}(y).
\]
It is also $\mathbb F_2$-linear. The minimal polynomial satisfies
\[
f(\alpha)=\alpha^n+c_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0=0.
\]
Multiply by $\alpha^k$ and take traces. Because subtraction and addition agree in characteristic two,
\[
\boxed{s_{k+n}=c_{n-1}s_{k+n-1}+\cdots+c_0s_k.}
\]

\emph{(b) Every non-zero window appears.}
First, $\alpha$ has minimal polynomial of degree $n$. Indeed, if $\alpha$ lay in a proper subfield $\mathbb F_{2^d}$ of $\mathbb F_{2^n}$ with $d<n$, its multiplicative order would divide $2^d-1<2^n-1$, contradicting the assumption that it generates $\mathbb F_{2^n}^*$. Thus
\[
1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}
\]
is a basis of the field over $\mathbb F_2$.

The trace is not the zero linear functional: its defining polynomial
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}X^{2^j}
\]
is a non-zero polynomial of degree $2^{n-1}$, less than the $2^n$ elements of the field, so it cannot vanish everywhere.

Consider the linear map
\[
T:\beta\longmapsto
\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}(\beta),\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\alpha),
\ldots,\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\alpha^{n-1})\bigr).
\]
If $T(\beta)=0$, linearity and the displayed basis imply $\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\gamma)=0$ for every $\gamma$ in the field. If $\beta\ne0$, the map $\gamma\mapsto\beta\gamma$ is a bijection, so this would make the trace vanish everywhere, a contradiction. Thus $T$ is injective; both source and target contain $2^n$ elements, so $T$ is bijective.

The length-$n$ window at time $k$ is precisely $T(\alpha^k)$. As $k$ runs through $0,\ldots,2^n-2$, the powers $\alpha^k$ visit all non-zero field elements exactly once. Hence the windows visit all non-zero binary $n$-tuples exactly once.

The powers repeat with period $2^n-1$, so the bits do too. No smaller positive period is possible: a smaller period would repeat a length-$n$ window, contradicting the bijection. Finally, any binary sequence satisfying the recurrence of (a) is determined by its first $n$ bits. If that block is non-zero, it matches exactly one window of $(s_k)$, and the recurrence forces the entire sequence to be that cyclic shift.

\emph{(c) Balance and correlation.}
A non-zero linear functional from an $n$-dimensional $\mathbb F_2$-space to $\mathbb F_2$ has a kernel of size $2^{n-1}$ and a fibre of the same size over $1$. Of the $2^n-1$ non-zero field elements, $2^{n-1}$ have trace $1$, while $2^{n-1}-1$ have trace $0$. These are the numbers of ones and zeros in a period.

For $0<t<2^n-1$, the element $1+\alpha^t$ is non-zero. By linearity,
\[
s_k+s_{k+t}
=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\alpha^k(1+\alpha^t)\bigr).
\]
Multiplication by the fixed non-zero factor $1+\alpha^t$ permutes all non-zero field elements. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-2}(-1)^{s_k+s_{k+t}}
&=\sum_{\beta\ne0}(-1)^{\operatorname{Tr}(\beta)}\\
&=(2^{n-1}-1)-2^{n-1}=\boxed{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last line counts $+1$ for trace zero and $-1$ for trace one.

\tools Minimal polynomials, finite-field multiplicative groups, and traces (Chapter~9).

\leads This construction produces a long deterministic cycle with precisely balanced linear statistics. Its predictability is the reason it is useful in coding and synchronisation but insufficient on its own for cryptographic secrecy.
\end{solution}
