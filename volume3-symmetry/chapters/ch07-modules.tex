\chapter{Modules over a Principal Ideal Domain}

\begin{center}\itshape
How many abelian groups of order $360$ are there?\\
And why does every square matrix satisfy its own characteristic polynomial?\\
One theorem answers both: the structure of finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain.
\end{center}

How many different abelian groups have $360$ elements? The number factors as
\[
360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5.
\]
There are three ways to split the $2^3$ part into cyclic prime-power factors, two ways for $3^2$, and one for $5$. That gives $3\cdot2=6$ groups up to isomorphism. Why should counting partitions of exponents classify groups?

There is another question that seems unrelated. Volume~1, Chapter~7,
proved the Cayley--Hamilton identity by comparing coefficients in an
adjugate formula: every square matrix $A$ satisfies
\[
\det(XI-A)\big|_{X=A}=0.
\]
How does that identity fit with the classification of finite abelian
groups? The module structure theorem will give a second proof and show
what the two questions have in common.

Both questions become easier in the language of \emph{modules}. An abelian group is a module over $\Z$: multiplying by an integer means repeated addition. A vector space with a linear transformation $A$ is a module over $K[X]$: the polynomial $P$ acts as $P(A)$. The underlying idea is the same, even though one case starts with groups and the other with matrices.

Over a principal ideal domain, finitely generated modules have a precise structure. The Smith normal form gives a matrix description of that structure; the classification of finite abelian groups is one consequence, and rational and Jordan canonical forms are others.

We will prove the structure statement rather than treat it as a black box. Watch how Bézout combinations turn a matrix of relations into a list of invariant factors. Over $\Z$ or $K[X]$ we can compute those combinations by division with remainder; Proposition~\ref{prop:pid-not-euclidean} showed why a general PID need not supply such a division algorithm. Those factors will continue to matter when we study field extensions.

\section{Modules}

Here $R$ is a commutative ring with identity, and scalars act on the left.

\begin{definition}
An \emph{$R$-module} is an abelian group $(M,+)$ with a map $R\times M\to M$, $(r,x)\mapsto rx$, such that $r(x+y)=rx+ry$, $(r+s)x=rx+sx$, $(rs)x=r(sx)$ and $1x=x$. A \emph{submodule} is an additive subgroup $N$ closed under all scalars. On the additive quotient $M/N$, set $r(x+N)=rx+N$; this is well defined because $r(x-y)\in N$ when $x-y\in N$. A homomorphism is an additive map satisfying $f(rx)=rf(x)$; its kernel and image are submodules. The group isomorphism $M/\ker f\to f(M)$, $x+\ker f\mapsto f(x)$, also respects scalars, so the first isomorphism theorem holds for modules (Theorem~\ref{thm:iso1}).
\end{definition}

The axioms also give $0x=0$ and $r0=0$: expand $(0+0)x$ and
$r(0+0)$, then cancel in the additive group. Expanding $(r+(-r))x$
gives $(-r)x=-(rx)$. Thus negative integer scalars in the examples
below really do act by additive inverses. If $R$ is the zero ring,
$0x=0$ and $1x=x$ force $M=\{0\}$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:modules}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item Over a field, modules are vector spaces.
\item Over $\Z$, modules are abelian groups: $nx$ must be $x+\dots+x$, so the module structure is forced.
\item The ideals of $R$ are its submodules; $R/I$ is a module.
\item $R^n$, with componentwise operations. A module is \emph{free} if it has a \emph{basis}, a family $(e_i)$ such that every element is uniquely $\sum r_ie_i$ (finitely many $r_i\ne0$); a free module with a basis of $n$ elements is isomorphic to $R^n$.
\item If $V$ is a vector space over $K$ and $A:V\to V$ is linear, $V$ becomes a $K[X]$-module $V_A$ by $P\cdot v=P(A)v$. Submodules are the $A$-stable subspaces. Indeed polynomial evaluation gives $(P+Q)(A)=P(A)+Q(A)$ and $(PQ)(A)=P(A)Q(A)$ by multiplying powers of the same $A$. Stability under $A$ gives stability under every power and linear combination; the converse uses the scalar $X$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{think}
Why should a matrix be ``the same thing'' as a $K[X]$-module? If $V_A\cong V_B$ as $K[X]$-modules, by a bijection $S$, what does $S$ do to $A$ and $B$?
\end{think}

A module is \emph{finitely generated} if it equals $Rx_1+\dots+Rx_n$ for some $x_i$, equivalently if there is a surjective homomorphism $R^n\to M$, $(r_i)\mapsto\sum r_ix_i$. If $R$ is a domain, the number of elements of a finite basis of a free module is well defined: an isomorphism $R^s\to R^t$ and its inverse are given by matrices $B$ ($t\times s$) and $C$ ($s\times t$) with $CB=I_s$, $BC=I_t$; these are also inverse linear maps between $K^s$ and $K^t$, $K=\operatorname{Frac}R$. The exchange lemma of Chapter~1 (\ref{lem:anyfield}) gives $\dim K^s=s$ over every field, including this fraction field, so $s=t$. This number is the \emph{rank}.

For finitely many modules, $M_1\oplus\cdots\oplus M_t$ denotes their
componentwise product with componentwise scalar action. For submodules of one
module, an internal sum $N_1\oplus\cdots\oplus N_t$ means that every
element in the sum is written uniquely as $x_1+\cdots+x_t$, $x_i\in N_i$.
The addition map from the external sum to the internal sum is then an isomorphism.

\subsection*{A first example: norm and lattice index in the Gaussian integers}

Before we prove the Smith normal form, here is a two-dimensional calculation
that shows what it will do for us. We use the Gaussian integers
$\Z[i]=\{a+bi:a,b\in\Z\}$, a ring already familiar from Chapter~6.
Multiplication by $a+bi$ is a linear operation on the integer lattice,
but its image need not be all of the lattice.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:normindex}
For any non-zero Gaussian integer $z=a+bi$, the subgroup
$z\Z[i]\subseteq\Z[i]$ has finite index
\[
\boxed{[\Z[i]:z\Z[i]]=a^2+b^2=N(z)}.
\]
More precisely, if $d=\gcd(a,b)>0$, then
\[
\Z[i]/z\Z[i]\cong
\Z/d\Z\oplus\Z/\bigl((a^2+b^2)/d\bigr)\Z
\]
as additive groups.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Identify $x+iy$ with the integer column vector $(x,y)^{\mathsf T}$.
Multiplication by $z=a+bi$ is represented by
\[
T=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T\binom xy=\binom{ax-by}{bx+ay}.
\]
Thus $z\Z[i]$ corresponds to the image $T\Z^2$.

Put $a=da'$ and $b=db'$, where
$d=\gcd(a,b)>0$ and $\gcd(a',b')=1$.
Bézout's identity, proved in Volume~2, Theorem~1.4, supplies
integers $u,v$ with $ua'+vb'=1$.
The integer matrix
\[
U=\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\-b'&a'\end{pmatrix}
\]
has determinant $ua'+vb'=1$; its inverse is the integer matrix
$\begin{psmallmatrix}a'&-v\\b'&u\end{psmallmatrix}$. Thus it is an
automorphism of the lattice $\Z^2$.
Multiplying out gives
\[
UT=
\begin{pmatrix}
d&d(-ub'+va')\\
0&d(a'^2+b'^2)
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Write $t=-ub'+va'$. Subtract $t$ times
the first column from the second column.
This is an invertible integer column operation,
and it reduces the matrix to
\[
D=\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\0&d(a'^2+b'^2)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\0&(a^2+b^2)/d\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Because the row and column operations are
invertible over $\Z$, they do not change
the isomorphism type of the quotient of $\Z^2$
by the image. Consequently
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Z[i]/z\Z[i]
&\cong\Z^2/T\Z^2
\cong\Z^2/D\Z^2\\
&\cong\Z/d\Z\oplus
\Z/\bigl((a^2+b^2)/d\bigr)\Z.
\end{aligned}
\]
The right side has $d\cdot(a^2+b^2)/d=a^2+b^2$
elements. This proves the index formula.

As a check, $z=1+i$ gives $d=1$ and
$N(z)=2$. The ideal $(1+i)$ therefore has
two additive residue classes, consistent
with $\Z[i]/(1+i)\cong\Z/2\Z$.
\end{proof}

The two simple integer operations above are a preview of
\emph{Smith normal form}. Later, Volume~13 studies ideals in general
number fields. That theory goes beyond what we need here:
the Gaussian-integer calculation is completely self-contained.


A module can be described by generators and the relations among them. Writing those relations as a matrix lets us simplify the description using allowed row and column operations. The Smith normal form records what survives.

\section{The Smith normal form}\label{sec:smith}

From now on $R$ is a PID. We allow zero rows or columns: $R^0=\{0\}$, $GL_0(R)$ has its unique empty identity matrix, and its determinant is $1$. A square matrix over $R$ is invertible over $R$ iff its determinant is a unit (the adjugate formula $B\operatorname{adj}B=(\det B)I$ holds over any commutative ring); these matrices form the group $GL_n(R)$. Row operations (swapping two rows, adding $r$ times one row to another, multiplying a row by a unit) are multiplications on the left by matrices of $GL_m(R)$, and column operations are multiplications on the right.

The determinant arguments of Volume~1 still apply here. Its permutation
formula uses only finite sums and products in a commutative ring.
Multilinearity follows by expansion, and terms cancel in pairs when
two rows agree, including in characteristic $2$. Expanding the columns
of $BC$ in the standard basis then gives
$\det(BC)=\det B\det C$, just as before. For the adjugate formula,
diagonal entries of $B\operatorname{adj}B$ are cofactor expansions of
$\det B$; off-diagonal entries are the same expansions with two rows
equal and hence are zero. If $\det B$ is a unit, this formula gives
the inverse; if $B$ is invertible, determinant multiplicativity makes
$\det B$ a unit. The proof below also uses simultaneous two-row or
two-column Bézout changes with determinant $1$.

\begin{theorem}[Smith normal form]\label{thm:smith}
Let $m,n\ge0$ and let $A$ be an $m\times n$ matrix over a PID $R$. There are $P\in GL_m(R)$ and $Q\in GL_n(R)$ such that
\[
\begin{gathered}PAQ=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qq D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_r),\\ d_i\ne0,\quad d_1\mid d_2\mid\dots\mid d_r.\end{gathered}
\]
The number $r$ and the $d_i$ (up to units) are determined by $A$: if $\Delta_k(A)$ denotes a gcd of the $k\times k$ minors of $A$, then $d_1\cdots d_k=\Delta_k(A)$ up to a unit for $1\le k\le r$, and $\Delta_k(A)=0$ for $r<k\le\min(m,n)$. We put $\Delta_0(A)=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All operations below use invertible changes of rows or columns. Swapping two rows, adding a multiple of one row to another, or multiplying a row by a unit corresponds to multiplying on the left by a matrix in $GL_m(R)$; columns work the same way on the right. Thus every step preserves equivalence under $A\mapsto PAQ$.

\emph{Find a pivot that divides all entries.}
If $A=0$, its Smith form is already the zero matrix. Otherwise choose a non-zero entry, move it to position $(1,1)$, and call it $a$.

Suppose an entry $b$ below $a$ in the first column is not divisible by $a$. Because $R$ is a PID, the ideal $(a,b)$ is generated by a gcd $d$, so write
\[
d=ua+vb,\qquad a=da',\qquad b=db'
\]
for some $u,v,a',b'\in R$. Dividing the first identity by $d$ gives $ua'+vb'=1$. Therefore the row operation
\[
\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\-b'&a'\end{pmatrix}
\binom ab=\binom d0
\]
uses a $2\times2$ matrix with determinant $ua'+vb'=1$, hence is invertible over $R$. It replaces the pivot $a$ by $d$. Since $a$ does not divide $b$, $d$ is a \emph{proper} divisor of $a$.

The same operation on columns handles an entry of the first row not divisible by $a$. Each time we replace the pivot by a proper divisor, the number $\lambda(a)$ of its irreducible factors, counted with multiplicity, decreases strictly. This number is a non-negative integer because a PID is a UFD (Theorem~\ref{thm:edpidufd}). Hence these reductions cannot continue indefinitely.

Once $a$ divides every entry in its row and column, subtract suitable multiples of the first row and first column to clear those entries. We obtain a block matrix
\[
\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&B\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If $a$ divides every entry of $B$, the first pivot is ready. If some entry $b$ of $B$ is not divisible by $a$, add its row to the first row. The pivot stays $a$ because the first-column entry of that row is zero; the first row now contains $b$. Perform the column gcd operation just described. It replaces $a$ by a proper divisor. Thus we eventually reach a block decomposition in which the non-zero pivot $d_1$ divides \emph{every} entry of the remaining block.

\emph{Repeat on the smaller block.}
Apply the procedure recursively to the $(m-1)\times(n-1)$ block. Its row and column operations act only on that block, so they do not disturb the first row or column. Since every original entry of the block was divisible by $d_1$, and the operations replace entries by $R$-linear combinations of them, all subsequent pivots stay divisible by $d_1$. The next pivot $d_2$ therefore satisfies $d_1\mid d_2$. Continuing, we obtain
\[
PAQ=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0,\ldots,0),
\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
\]
with non-zero $d_i$. The product of all the invertible row and column operations gives the required $P,Q$.

\emph{Why the diagonal entries are determined by $A$.}
Let $I_k(A)$ be the ideal generated by all $k\times k$ minors of $A$. Any invertible change of rows, including the two-row Bézout change above, makes each $k\times k$ minor an $R$-linear combination of old minors. Expand each new row as a combination of old rows and use multilinearity; terms choosing an old row twice vanish, and the other terms are old minors up to sign. Thus $I_k(PA)\subseteq I_k(A)$. Apply the inverse row operation to obtain the reverse inclusion. The same argument holds for invertible column operations, so
\[
I_k(PAQ)=I_k(A).
\]

For the diagonal matrix, every non-zero $k\times k$ minor is a product of $k$ diagonal entries $d_{i_1}\cdots d_{i_k}$ with $i_1<\cdots<i_k$. Since $d_j\mid d_{i_j}$ for every $j\le k$, every such product is divisible by $d_1\cdots d_k$. The first $k$ diagonal entries give a minor \emph{equal} to that product. Hence
\[
I_k(A)=(d_1\cdots d_k)\quad(1\le k\le r),
\]
and $I_k(A)=(0)$ for $k>r$.

An ideal of a PID has a generator unique up to multiplication by a unit. These determinantal ideals therefore determine $r$ and successively each $d_k$, up to a unit: $d_1$ generates $I_1$, and the relation $d_1\cdots d_k=\Delta_k(A)$ determines the later $d_k$. That proves uniqueness.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Think of a presentation matrix as a list of relations. We may replace one relation by a sum of relations, or replace the generators by another basis, without changing the module being presented. The Smith proof uses Bézout combinations to replace a pivot by a proper divisor until it divides all remaining entries. Termination follows from factorisation in a PID, not from a Euclidean size function. The final diagonal entries are not just convenient numbers: they describe the cyclic pieces of the quotient module. This is why the same algorithm works for an abelian group presented over $\Z$ and for a linear operator presented over $K[X]$.

Over a Euclidean ring such as $\Z$ or $K[X]$, gcd steps can be replaced by Euclidean divisions, and the proof is an algorithm, carried out in Figure~\ref{fig:smith} .

An element of a module over a domain is \emph{torsion} if some nonzero
scalar kills it. The diagonal entries of a Smith form tell us how these
elements fit into the whole module. Over a PID, the module splits into a
free part and cyclic torsion factors.

\section{The structure theorem}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:subfree}
Over a PID, every submodule of $R^n$ is free of rank at most $n$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We use induction on $n$. For $n=0$,
the module $R^0$ is $\{0\}$; its only
submodule is free of rank zero.

Suppose the result holds in $R^{n-1}$,
and let $L\subseteq R^n$ be a submodule.
Look only at the last coordinate:
\[
\pi:L\longrightarrow R,\qquad
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\longmapsto x_n.
\]
Its image is an ideal of $R$, and $R$
is a PID, so $\pi(L)=(a)$ for some $a\in R$.
Its kernel $L_0$ consists of the elements
whose last coordinate is zero. Hence
$L_0$ is a submodule of $R^{n-1}$,
and by induction it is free of rank
at most $n-1$.

If $a=0$, then $L=L_0$ and we are done.
Otherwise choose $v\in L$ with last
coordinate exactly $a$, possible
since $a\in\pi(L)$. For any $x\in L$,
its last coordinate is $ra$ for some
$r\in R$. Therefore $x-rv\in L_0$,
showing
\[
L=L_0+Rv.
\]

This sum is direct. Indeed, if $sv\in L_0$,
its last coordinate equals $sa=0$.
Since $R$ is an integral domain and
$a\ne0$, we get $s=0$. Thus
$L_0\cap Rv=\{0\}$ and
\[
L=L_0\oplus Rv.
\]
Take a basis of the free module $L_0$
and append the vector $v$.
It is a basis of $L$, whose rank is
at most $(n-1)+1=n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[structure of finitely generated modules over a PID]\label{thm:structure}
Let $M$ be a finitely generated module over a PID $R$. Then
\[
M\cong R^s\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\dots\oplus R/(d_r),
\]
with $d_1\mid d_2\mid\dots\mid d_r$ non-zero non-units. The integer $s$ (the \emph{rank}) and the \emph{invariant factors} $d_i$, up to units, are determined by $M$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Existence: record generators and their relations.}
Choose generators $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ of $M$.
There is a surjective module map
\[
\varphi:R^n\to M,\qquad
(r_1,\ldots,r_n)\mapsto\sum_i r_ix_i.
\]
Its kernel $K$ is a submodule of the free
module $R^n$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:subfree},
$K$ has a basis $k_1,\ldots,k_m$ with
$m\le n$. Put these basis vectors in
the columns of a matrix $A\in M_{n\times m}(R)$.
Then $K$ is the image of $A$ and
\[
M\cong R^n/K=R^n/AR^m.
\]

Use Smith normal form (Theorem~\ref{thm:smith}):
there are invertible matrices $P,Q$
over $R$ such that
\[
PAQ=D,
\]
where the non-zero diagonal entries
$d_1,\ldots,d_t$ satisfy
$d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_t$.
Changing the generating basis by $P$
and the relation basis by $Q$
does not change the quotient module.
Thus
\[
M\cong R^n/DR^m
\cong
R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_t)
\oplus R^{n-t}.
\]
When a $d_i$ is a unit, $R/(d_i)=0$,
so we omit that summand.
Because the divisibility chain is ordered,
all unit factors occur before the non-unit
ones. We arrive at the asserted form.

\emph{Uniqueness: first the free rank.}
Let
\[
T=\{x\in M:ax=0\text{ for some }a\in R,\ a\ne0\}
\]
be the torsion submodule. This really is a submodule: if $ax=0$ and
$by=0$ with $a,b\ne0$, then $ab(x+y)=0$ and $ab\ne0$ in a
domain; a scalar multiple of $x$ is still killed by $a$.
In a domain, the only element of a free
module $R^s$ killed by a non-zero scalar
is zero. Every element of $R/(d_i)$
is killed by $d_i\ne0$. Thus in the
decomposition above, $T$ is exactly
the direct sum of the torsion summands,
and $M/T\cong R^s$.
Both $T$ and $M/T$ are defined from
$M$ alone, so an isomorphism of modules
preserves them. The rank $s$ is unique,
because free modules of different finite
ranks cannot be isomorphic over a domain.

\emph{Uniqueness: recover each prime-power size.}
Fix a prime element $p\in R$.
For $k\ge0$, the quotient
\[
p^kT/p^{k+1}T
\]
is a vector space over the field $R/(p)$ (the irreducible ideal $(p)$
is maximal in a PID). Its dimension is the one from the exchange lemma of Chapter~1, which applies to every field. Multiplication by $p$ kills the quotient, so
$(r+(p))\cdot(x+p^{k+1}T)=rx+p^{k+1}T$ is independent of
both representatives; the module laws give the vector-space laws.
Its dimension is therefore an invariant
of $T$.

Look at one summand $R/(d)$.
Write $d=p^ve$, where $p\nmid e$,
and $v=v_p(d)\ge0$.
For $k<v$, multiplication by $p^k$
identifies
\[
p^k(R/(d))/p^{k+1}(R/(d))
\cong R/(p);
\]
the map $R\to p^k(R/(d))/p^{k+1}(R/(d))$
sending $r$ to the class of $p^kr$
is onto, and its kernel is $(p)$,
because $p^kr\in(p^{k+1},d)=(p^{k+1})$ is equivalent, by
cancellation, to $r\in(p)$. For $k\ge v$,
the quotient is zero: $p^k$ and $p^{k+1}$
generate the same subgroup modulo $(d)$,
since Bézout applied to $p^{k-v}$ and $e$ gives
$(p^k,d)=(p^v)=(p^{k+1},d)$.

Taking direct sums gives
\[
\dim_{R/(p)}(p^kT/p^{k+1}T)
=\#\{i:v_p(d_i)>k\}.
\]
For each prime $p$, these dimensions
tell us how many invariant factors
are divisible by $p,p^2,\ldots$.
They determine the multiset of positive
exponents $v_p(d_i)$.

Finally we must align the exponents
for different primes. If $T=0$, there
are no non-unit invariant factors:
$r=0$, and uniqueness of the torsion
part is immediate. This also handles
the case where $R$ is a field,
which has no prime elements.

Suppose now that $T\ne0$, so $r\ge1$.
Because $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$, their
$p$-adic exponents are non-decreasing
for every prime $p$. All $r$ invariant
factors are non-units, and $d_1$
divides each of them. Choose a prime
dividing $d_1$; for that prime the
dimension $\dim_{R/(p)}(T/pT)$ equals $r$.
For every other prime it is at most $r$.
Hence, in this non-zero torsion case,
\[
r=\max_p\dim_{R/(p)}(T/pT).
\]
This recovers the number of factors.
Place each prime's exponent list in
non-decreasing order, padding with
zeros on the left to length $r$.
Multiplying the corresponding
prime powers in each position
recovers $d_1,\ldots,d_r$ up to units.
So the invariant factors are unique.

The proof explains why divisibility is
part of the statement: it fixes the way
prime-power contributions are grouped.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Existence and uniqueness require different ideas. Existence comes from a presentation $R^n\twoheadrightarrow M$ and the Smith form of its relation matrix. Uniqueness comes from quantities we can recover from $M$ itself: its free rank and, for each prime element $p$, the dimensions of the quotients $p^kT/p^{k+1}T$. Each such dimension counts the cyclic factors that still have at least one more power of $p$ left. For example, in $\Z/8\Z\oplus\Z/4\Z$, the dimensions over $\mathbb F_2$ for $k=0,1,2$ are $2,2,1$. We can read the exponents $3$ and $2$ back from that sequence.

For $R=\Z$, $\Z/d\Z\cong\bigoplus_p\Z/p^{v_p(d)}\Z$ by the Chinese remainder theorem, which gives two equivalent forms of the classification.

\begin{corollary}[finitely generated abelian groups]\label{cor:abelian}
Every finitely generated abelian group is isomorphic to $\Z^s\oplus\Z/d_1\Z\oplus\dots\oplus\Z/d_r\Z$ with $1<d_1\mid\dots\mid d_r$, and also to $\Z^s\oplus\bigoplus_j\Z/p_j^{a_j}\Z$ with prime powers $p_j^{a_j}$; $s$, the $d_i$, and the multiset of the $p_j^{a_j}$ are unique. In particular, for $n\ge1$ with $n=\prod p^{a_p}$, the number of abelian groups of order $n$ is $\prod_p\mathrm{p}(a_p)$, where $\mathrm p(a)$ is the number of partitions of $a$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An abelian group $A$ is exactly a
$\Z$-module: multiplication by an
integer means repeated addition,
with negative integers giving inverses.
Since $\Z$ is a PID, the structure
theorem gives
\[
A\cong\Z^s\oplus
\Z/d_1\Z\oplus\cdots\oplus\Z/d_r\Z,
\qquad1<d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r.
\]
The rank and invariant factors are
unique by Theorem~\ref{thm:structure}.

Factor every $d_i$ into prime powers.
Because distinct prime powers are
coprime, the Chinese remainder theorem
splits each cyclic group:
\[
\Z/d_i\Z\cong
\bigoplus_{p\mid d_i}\Z/p^{v_p(d_i)}\Z.
\]
Doing this for each $i$ produces the
elementary-divisor form, the direct sum
of cyclic groups of prime-power order.

Conversely, suppose the multiset of
prime-power factors is given.
For each prime $p$, sort its exponents
in increasing order, and align their
largest entries on the right.
Multiply the prime powers in each
aligned column. These products form
$d_1,\ldots,d_r$ with
$d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$.
For example, $\Z/4\Z\oplus\Z/2\Z
\oplus\Z/9\Z$ corresponds to
invariant factors $2$ and $36$.
Thus the prime-power multiset determines
the invariant factors and is unique.

Now suppose $A$ is finite, of order
$n=\prod_p p^{a_p}$. The free rank
$s$ must be zero, otherwise $A$
would be infinite. For a fixed prime
$p$, the $p$-primary part has the form
\[
\Z/p^{b_1}\Z\oplus\cdots\oplus
\Z/p^{b_t}\Z,\qquad
b_j\ge1,\quad\sum_jb_j=a_p.
\]
Reordering the summands does not change
the group. Thus its isomorphism type
is determined by a partition of $a_p$.
Choices for different primes are independent,
so the number of possible types is
\[
\boxed{\prod_{p\mid n}\mathrm p(a_p)}.
\]
For $n=1$, the empty product is $1$, counting just the trivial group.
For $n=360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$,
this gives $\mathrm p(3)\mathrm p(2)
\mathrm p(1)=3\cdot2\cdot1=6$.
\end{proof}

Order alone does not distinguish the six groups of order $360$
(Exercise~\ref{ex:ab360}). Their largest invariant factors do distinguish
some of them. The \emph{exponent} of a finite group is the least positive
$e$ with $g^e=1$ for every element $g$; such an $e$ exists because
Lagrange's theorem makes $|G|$ one choice. For a nontrivial finite
abelian group in invariant-factor form, it is $d_r$: that number kills every additive
coordinate, and the last cyclic factor contains an element of order
$d_r$. Thus $\Z/360\Z$ has exponent $360$, while
$\Z/2\Z\oplus\Z/6\Z\oplus\Z/30\Z$ has exponent $30$.
The trivial group has exponent $1$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:index}
If $A\in M_n(\Z)$ has $\det A\ne0$, the subgroup $A\Z^n$ has index $|\det A|$ in $\Z^n$, and $\Z^n/A\Z^n\cong\bigoplus\Z/d_i\Z$ with the $d_i$ of the Smith form.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take the Smith normal form of the integer
matrix $A$: there are invertible matrices
$P,Q\in GL_n(\Z)$ such that
\[
PAQ=D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n),
\quad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_n.
\]
The determinant of $A$ is non-zero,
so none of the $d_i$ is zero.

Why do $P$ and $Q$ preserve the quotient?
An invertible integer matrix is an
automorphism of the additive group
$\Z^n$. Since $Q\Z^n=\Z^n$,
\[
A\Z^n=AQ\Z^n.
\]
Applying the automorphism $P$ takes
this subgroup to $PAQ\Z^n=D\Z^n$.
We therefore have an isomorphism
\[
\Z^n/A\Z^n\cong\Z^n/D\Z^n.
\]

The diagonal subgroup $D\Z^n$
consists of all tuples
$(d_1z_1,\ldots,d_nz_n)$.
Taking residues in each coordinate gives
\[
\Z^n/D\Z^n\cong
\bigoplus_{i=1}^n\Z/d_i\Z.
\]
This finite group contains exactly
$\prod_i|d_i|$ elements. Finally,
$\det P$ and $\det Q$ are units
of $\Z$, hence equal to $\pm1$, so
\[
|\det A|=|\det(PAQ)|
=|\det D|=\prod_i|d_i|.
\]
The index of $A\Z^n$ is the number
of elements of the quotient, proving
\[
\boxed{[\Z^n:A\Z^n]=|\det A|}.
\]
This is the general lattice-index
principle foreshadowed by the Gaussian
integer example earlier in this chapter.
\end{proof}

An abelian group is a module over $\Z$. Now let a polynomial act on a vector by evaluating it at a linear transformation. The same structure theorem that classified finite abelian groups can then describe matrices.

\section{Linear algebra over \texorpdfstring{$K[X]$}{K[X]}}

Let $K$ be a field, $n\ge0$ and $A\in M_n(K)$, and $V_A=K^n$ with $P\cdot v=P(A)v$ (Example~\ref{ex:modules}(5)). Two matrices $A,B$ are similar ($B=SAS^{-1}$) iff $V_A\cong V_B$ as $K[X]$-modules: a $K[X]$-isomorphism is a linear bijection $S$ with $S(Av)=B(Sv)$, i.e.\ $SA=BS$. So classifying matrices up to similarity is classifying these modules.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:presentation}
$V_A\cong K[X]^n/(XI-A)K[X]^n$. Consequently the Smith form of $XI-A$ over $K[X]$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1,d_1,\dots,d_r)$ with $d_i$ monic of positive degree, $d_1\mid\dots\mid d_r$, $d_1\cdots d_r=\chi_A=\det(XI-A)$, and
\[
V_A\cong K[X]/(d_1)\oplus\dots\oplus K[X]/(d_r).
\]
The $d_i$ are the \emph{invariant factors} of $A$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We make the linear transformation $A$
into multiplication by a formal variable $X$.
The module relations then become the columns
of $XI-A$.

Define the $K[X]$-linear map
\[
\varphi:K[X]^n\to V_A,\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^n F_je_j
\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^nF_j(A)e_j.
\]
It is surjective: every vector of $K^n$
is the image of the corresponding constant
vector. For each standard basis vector,
the $j$-th column of $XI-A$ is
\[
Xe_j-\sum_i a_{ij}e_i.
\]
Its image under $\varphi$ is
$Ae_j-\sum_i a_{ij}e_i=0$.
Thus
\[
(XI-A)K[X]^n\subseteq\ker\varphi.
\]

To prove the reverse inclusion, work
modulo the image of $XI-A$.
The column relation says
\[
Xe_j\equiv\sum_i a_{ij}e_i.
\]
The right side is constant. Any
polynomial vector of degree at least
one can therefore be reduced in
degree: replace every occurrence of
$Xe_j$ using this relation.
By induction on the highest degree,
every class in the quotient has
a representative $c\in K^n$
that is a constant vector.

Now suppose $F\in\ker\varphi$.
Choose such a representative $c$,
so $F-c$ belongs to the image of
$XI-A$. That image is already in
$\ker\varphi$, hence
\[
0=\varphi(F)=\varphi(c)=c.
\]
Therefore $c=0$ and $F$ belongs to
the image. This proves
\[
\ker\varphi=(XI-A)K[X]^n,
\]
and the first isomorphism theorem
gives the claimed presentation.

The determinant $\chi_A(X)=\det(XI-A)$
is monic of degree $n$, so it is not
the zero polynomial. The Smith normal
form of $XI-A$ over the PID $K[X]$
therefore has $n$ non-zero diagonal
entries. Multiply the entries by
units of $K[X]$ (non-zero constants)
to make them monic, and discard
entries equal to $1$ when taking
quotients. Let the remaining
positive-degree entries be
$d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$.
The Smith operations multiply the
determinant by non-zero constants,
and both $\prod_i d_i$ and
$\chi_A$ are monic. Thus
\[
\prod_{i=1}^r d_i=\chi_A.
\]
Finally the diagonal presentation
decomposes the module as
\[
\boxed{V_A\cong
K[X]/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus K[X]/(d_r)}.
\]
These are the invariant factors
used to classify $A$.
\end{proof}

A polynomial $F$ \emph{annihilates} $A$ if $F(A)=0$.
These polynomials form an ideal of $K[X]$: sums still annihilate,
and $(QF)(A)=Q(A)F(A)=0$. The preceding cyclic decomposition shows
that $d_1\cdots d_r$ annihilates every summand, so the ideal is nonzero.
Its unique monic generator is the \emph{minimal polynomial} $\mu_A$;
polynomial division also shows it is the monic annihilator of least degree.
For $n=0$ the only operator is zero on the zero space, even the identity
is that same map, and this ideal is all of $K[X]$: $\mu_A=\chi_A=1$.

For a monic polynomial of degree $m\ge1$, $d=X^m+c_{m-1}X^{m-1}+\dots+c_0$, the module $K[X]/(d)$ has the basis $1,x,\dots,x^{m-1}$ ($x$ the class of $X$; use Euclidean division), and multiplication by $x$ has the \emph{companion matrix}
\[
C(d)=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&-c_0\\1&\ddots&&-c_1\\&\ddots&0&\vdots\\&&1&-c_{m-1}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For degree zero, set $C(1)$ equal to the empty $0\times0$ matrix.
For $m=1$, $C(d)=(-c_0)$ has characteristic polynomial $X+c_0=d$.
For $m\ge2$, its characteristic polynomial is $d$: expanding $\det(XI-C(d))$ along the first row gives $X\det(XI-C(d'))+c_0$ with $d'=X^{m-1}+c_{m-1}X^{m-2}+\dots+c_1$ (the other minor is triangular with $-1$ on its diagonal, determinant $(-1)^{m-1}$, and the sign of position $(1,m)$ is $(-1)^{m+1}$), and induction gives $Xd'+c_0=d$. Also $g(C(d))=0$ iff $g\cdot1=0$ in $K[X]/(d)$ iff $d\mid g$: the class of $1$ generates this module, so multiplication by $g$ kills the whole module exactly when it kills $1$.

\begin{theorem}[rational canonical form; Frobenius]\label{thm:frobenius}
Every $A\in M_n(K)$ is similar to the block diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(C(d_1),\dots,C(d_r))$ of its invariant factors. Two matrices are similar iff they have the same invariant factors. For $n\ge1$ the minimal polynomial is $\mu_A=d_r$, and $\chi_A=d_1\cdots d_r$. For $n=0$ the block list is empty and both polynomials are $1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The case $n=0$ has the empty block list and $\mu_A=\chi_A=1$, as above.
Assume now $n\ge1$. The action of the polynomial variable
$X$ on $V_A$ is precisely the linear
map $A$. We therefore need to understand
multiplication by $X$ on each cyclic
summand of the module decomposition.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:presentation},
\[
V_A\cong
\bigoplus_{i=1}^rK[X]/(d_i),
\qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r.
\]
Write one monic invariant factor as
\[
d_i=X^m+c_{m-1}X^{m-1}+\cdots+c_0.
\]
The residue classes of
$1,X,\ldots,X^{m-1}$ form a basis
of the $K$-vector space $K[X]/(d_i)$.
Multiplication by $X$ sends
$X^j$ to $X^{j+1}$ for $j<m-1$.
At the last vector, use $d_i=0$
inside the quotient:
\[
X^m=-c_0-c_1X-\cdots-c_{m-1}X^{m-1}.
\]
So the matrix of multiplication
by $X$ in this basis is exactly
the companion matrix $C(d_i)$.

Choose these bases in every direct
summand and join them. In the
resulting basis of $V_A$,
multiplication by $X$, hence $A$,
has the block diagonal form
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}
(C(d_1),\ldots,C(d_r))}.
\]

Two matrices $A,B$ are similar
exactly when their corresponding
$K[X]$-modules $V_A,V_B$ are isomorphic:
an intertwining linear bijection
$S$ satisfies $SA=BS$.
By the uniqueness in the PID
structure theorem, isomorphic
modules have the same invariant
factors. Conversely, equal factors
produce the same companion-block
matrix. This proves the similarity
classification.

Finally a polynomial $F\in K[X]$
annihilates $V_A$ precisely when
it acts as zero on every cyclic summand.
On $K[X]/(d_i)$, this happens exactly
when $d_i\mid F$. Because the factors
form a divisibility chain, the
condition for all $i$ is simply
$d_r\mid F$. Thus the monic
annihilating polynomial of least
degree is
\[
\boxed{\mu_A=d_r}.
\]
The characteristic polynomial is
$\boxed{\chi_A=d_1\cdots d_r}$ by
the preceding presentation proposition.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Cayley--Hamilton]\label{cor:cayleyhamilton}
$\chi_A(A)=0$. Moreover $\mu_A$ divides $\chi_A$, and they have the same irreducible factors.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $n=0$, both polynomials are $1$ and evaluation is the zero map
on the zero space, so every assertion holds. Assume $n\ge1$.
From the rational canonical form theorem,
the characteristic polynomial and minimal
polynomial have the descriptions
\[
\chi_A=d_1\cdots d_r,\qquad
\mu_A=d_r,
\]
where the invariant factors satisfy
$d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r$.

Therefore $\mu_A=d_r$ divides the
product $d_1\cdots d_r=\chi_A$.
There is a polynomial $Q\in K[X]$
with $\chi_A=\mu_A Q$.
Evaluate this identity at $A$:
\[
\chi_A(A)=\mu_A(A)Q(A)=0,
\]
because the definition of the minimal
polynomial is $\mu_A(A)=0$.
This proves the Cayley--Hamilton identity.

It remains to compare irreducible factors.
Since $\mu_A\mid\chi_A$, every
irreducible factor of $\mu_A$ also
divides $\chi_A$. Conversely, if an
irreducible $p$ divides $\chi_A=d_1\cdots d_r$,
the primality of irreducibles in the
PID $K[X]$ makes it divide some $d_i$.
But $d_i\mid d_r=\mu_A$, so $p$
also divides $\mu_A$.
Thus the two polynomials have exactly
the same irreducible factors,
possibly with different multiplicities.

For example, the matrix $\begin{psmallmatrix}\lambda&1&0\\0&\lambda&1\\0&0&\lambda\end{psmallmatrix}$ has both polynomials
equal to $(X-\lambda)^3$, while the
diagonal matrix $\lambda I_3$ has
$\chi_A=(X-\lambda)^3$ and
$\mu_A=X-\lambda$. Cayley--Hamilton
holds in either case.
\end{proof}

When $\chi_A$ splits into linear factors (always if $K=\C$), the Chinese remainder theorem refines the decomposition. Writing $d_i=\prod_\lambda(X-\lambda)^{e_{i,\lambda}}$, Theorem~\ref{thm:crtring} gives $K[X]/(d_i)\cong\bigoplus_\lambda K[X]/\bigl((X-\lambda)^{e_{i,\lambda}}\bigr)$. In $K[X]/((X-\lambda)^e)$, with $y=x-\lambda$, the basis $b_1=y^{e-1},b_2=y^{e-2},\dots,b_e=1$ satisfies $xb_1=\lambda b_1$ and $xb_k=\lambda b_k+b_{k-1}$: multiplication by $x$ is the \emph{Jordan block} $J_e(\lambda)$, with $\lambda$ on the diagonal and $1$ just above it.

\begin{theorem}[Jordan normal form]\label{thm:jordan}
If $\chi_A$ splits over $K$, then $A$ is similar to a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks $J_{e}(\lambda)$, unique up to the order of the blocks. The blocks for $\lambda$ have sizes $e_{1,\lambda}\le\dots\le e_{r,\lambda}$ (the non-zero ones), the exponents of $X-\lambda$ in the invariant factors.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A Jordan block records what multiplication by $X$ does on one cyclic
$K[X]$-module. We use the structure theorem to produce these modules, then
recover their sizes directly from the kernels of powers of $A-\lambda I$.

\emph{Existence.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:presentation} and the PID structure theorem give
\[
V_A\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^r K[X]/(d_i),
\qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
\]
where $X$ acts on $V_A$ as $A$. Since $\chi_A=\prod_i d_i$ splits over
$K$, every $d_i$ is a product of powers of linear factors:
\[
d_i=\prod_{\lambda}(X-\lambda)^{e_{i,\lambda}}.
\]
The factors for different $\lambda$ are pairwise coprime. The Chinese
remainder theorem therefore splits the $i$-th cyclic module into
\[
K[X]/(d_i)\cong
\bigoplus_{\lambda:e_{i,\lambda}>0}
K[X]/\bigl((X-\lambda)^{e_{i,\lambda}}\bigr).
\]
Consider a summand with exponent $e$. Write $y=X-\lambda$ modulo
$(X-\lambda)^e$. It has the $K$-basis
\[
y^{e-1},\,y^{e-2},\,\dots,\,y,\,1.
\]
Multiplication by $X=\lambda+y$ sends the first basis vector to $\lambda$
times itself, and sends each later basis vector to $\lambda$ times
itself plus the preceding basis vector. Its matrix is exactly
$J_e(\lambda)$, with $1$s just above the diagonal. Joining these bases
produces a Jordan form of $A$. The blocks for $\lambda$ have the
positive sizes $e_{i,\lambda}$ in the order inherited from the
invariant factors.

\emph{Uniqueness.}
We must check that another choice of Jordan basis cannot change the
block sizes. Fix an eigenvalue $\lambda$ and let $n=\dim V_A$. The
number
\[
h_k(\lambda)=\dim_K\ker(A-\lambda I)^k,\qquad k\ge0,
\]
is determined by $A$ alone and is unchanged under similarity; put
$h_0(\lambda)=0$.

On a Jordan block $J_e(\lambda)$, the operator $A-\lambda I$ is the
nilpotent shift and its $k$-th power has kernel of dimension
$\min(k,e)$. On a block $J_f(\mu)$ with $\mu\ne\lambda$, the operator
$J_f(\mu)-\lambda I$ is invertible (an upper triangular matrix whose
diagonal entries are $\mu-\lambda\ne0$), so it contributes no kernel.
Consequently every Jordan form satisfies
\[
h_k(\lambda)=\sum_{\text{blocks for }\lambda}\min(k,e).
\]
Subtract the identity for $k-1$:
\[
h_k(\lambda)-h_{k-1}(\lambda)
=\#\{\text{blocks for }\lambda\text{ of size at least }k\}.
\]
The number of blocks of size \emph{exactly} $k$ is therefore
\[
\bigl(h_k-h_{k-1}\bigr)
-\bigl(h_{k+1}-h_k\bigr)
=2h_k-h_{k-1}-h_{k+1},
\]
with all $h_j$ evaluated at $\lambda$.
These counts depend only on the original matrix, not on a Jordan basis.
Every eigenvalue and every block size is thus fixed, proving uniqueness
up to reordering the blocks.

For example, if the $\lambda$-blocks have sizes $3$ and $1$, their
kernel dimensions for $k=0,1,2,3$ are $0,2,3,4$. The successive
differences $2,1,1$ tell us that there are two blocks of size at least
$1$, but only one each of size at least $2$ and $3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The repeated factors now explain a phenomenon left open in Volume~1's
linear recurrences. Over $\Q$, take
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its minimal polynomial is $(X-1)^2$: $A-I\ne0$ but $(A-I)^2=0$.
Induction, multiplying by $A$ once more, gives
$A^t=\begin{psmallmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{psmallmatrix}$ for every
integer $t\ge0$. A repeated linear factor permits the extra factor $t$;
the diagonal matrix $I_2$, whose minimal polynomial is $X-1$, has
constant powers. Appendix~A develops the matrix-power estimates needed
for positive matrices. Volume~4, Chapter~9, applies matrix powers to
finite Markov chains. These are further applications, not premises here.

The finite abelian groups have another use already met in Volume~2, Chapter~11:
a Dirichlet character restricts to a homomorphism
$(\Z/q\Z)^*\to\C^*$. Exercise~\ref{ex:unitsmodn} makes the source
group explicit. Chapter~11 studies characters of more general finite
groups; Volume~6 uses Dirichlet characters in its analytic arguments.

For an outlook beyond our route, Hatcher's Example~2.42 identifies the
first integral homology group of the real projective plane as
$\Z/2\Z$. The geometric construction belongs to topology. Once that
construction supplies one generator $x$ with relation $2x=0$, its
algebraic calculation is exactly the Smith form of the matrix $(2)$.
This is an order-two \emph{torsion class}, rather than an ordinary hole
count. No topological result is used in our module classification.
\end{beauty}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:ab360}
List the six abelian groups of order $360$, in both forms of Corollary~\ref{cor:abelian}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:smithcalc}
Identify the abelian group with generators $x,y$ and relations $4x+6y=0$, $6x+4y=0$, i.e.\ $\Z^2/A\Z^2$ with $A=\begin{psmallmatrix}4&6\\6&4\end{psmallmatrix}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:unitsmodn}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Let $k\ge3$. Show that for every integer $j\ge0$, $5^{2^j}=1+2^{j+2}t_j$ with $t_j$ odd, that $5$ has order $2^{k-2}$ modulo $2^k$, and that $(\Z/2^k\Z)^*\cong\Z/2\Z\times\Z/2^{k-2}\Z$, generated by $-1$ and $5$.
\item Find the invariant factors of $(\Z/360\Z)^*$, and the smallest $e\ge1$ with $a^e\equiv1\pmod{360}$ for all $a$ prime to $360$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:similarQ}
Let $K\subseteq L$ be fields and $A,B\in M_n(K)$. Show that if $A$ and $B$ are similar over $L$, they are similar over $K$. (For instance, two rational matrices similar over $\C$ are similar over $\Q$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:nilpotent}
Show that the similarity classes of nilpotent $n\times n$ matrices over a field correspond to the partitions of $n$, and that the partition of $N$ can be read from the numbers $\dim\ker N^k$. List the classes for $n=4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:diagonalisable}
Show that $A\in M_n(K)$ is diagonalisable over $K$ iff $\mu_A$ is a product of distinct factors $X-\lambda$, $\lambda\in K$. Deduce that a matrix with $A^2=A$ is diagonalisable, and that a complex matrix with $A^k=I$ for some $k\ge1$ is diagonalisable.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:cyclicvector}
Show that the following are equivalent: (i) $\mu_A=\chi_A$; (ii) there is a vector $v$ such that $v,Av,\dots,A^{n-1}v$ is a basis; (iii) $A$ is similar to a companion matrix.
For $n=0$, read the family in (ii) as empty and the companion matrix as $C(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:sublattices}
Let $n\ge1$ be an integer. Show that every subgroup of index $n$ of $\Z^2$ can be written uniquely as $\Z(a,0)+\Z(b,c)$ with $a,c\ge1$, $ac=n$ and $0\le b<a$. Deduce that $\Z^2$ has exactly $\sigma(n)=\sum_{a\mid n}a$ subgroups of index $n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:ab360}
\idea First factor the order. The classification theorem works prime by prime, and there is one independent choice of partition for each prime exponent. To convert to invariant factors, align the prime-power cyclic factors on the right, filling missing positions with $1$.

\writeup We have
\[
360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5.
\]
An abelian group of order $8$ has three possible elementary-divisor descriptions:
\[
\Z/8,\qquad\Z/4\oplus\Z/2,\qquad(\Z/2)^3.
\]
An abelian group of order $9$ has two:
\[
\Z/9,\qquad(\Z/3)^2.
\]
The $5$-primary part must be $\Z/5$. There are therefore $3\cdot2\cdot1=6$ non-isomorphic abelian groups of order $360$. The uniqueness part of the structure theorem ensures that different choices really do give different groups.

The table lists their elementary divisors on the left and their invariant factors on the right:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
elementary divisors & invariant factors\\\hline
$8,9,5$ & $\Z/360$\\
$8,3,3,5$ & $\Z/3\oplus\Z/120$\\
$4,2,9,5$ & $\Z/2\oplus\Z/180$\\
$4,2,3,3,5$ & $\Z/6\oplus\Z/60$\\
$2,2,2,9,5$ & $\Z/2\oplus\Z/2\oplus\Z/90$\\
$2,2,2,3,3,5$ & $\Z/2\oplus\Z/6\oplus\Z/30$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, consider $\Z/4\oplus\Z/2\oplus(\Z/3)^2\oplus\Z/5$. Align the prime powers into two columns:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&\text{first}&\text{second}\\\hline
2\text{-part}&2&4\\
3\text{-part}&3&3\\
5\text{-part}&1&5\\\hline
\text{product}&6&60
\end{array}.
\]
The Chinese remainder theorem combines factors in each column, giving $\Z/6\oplus\Z/60$. The divisibility $6\mid60$ confirms that this is invariant-factor form. Every row in the table similarly has product of invariant factors equal to $360$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:smithcalc}
\idea The Smith normal form classifies the quotient $\Z^2/A\Z^2$. In a $2\times2$ example we can find its diagonal without guessing any row operations: take the gcd of the entries and then the absolute determinant.

\writeup Let
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\6&4\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The first determinantal divisor is the gcd of the $1\times1$ minors, which are just the four entries:
\[
\Delta_1=\gcd(4,6,6,4)=2.
\]
The second determinantal divisor is the gcd of the full-size minors. There is only one:
\[
\Delta_2=|\det A|=|4\cdot4-6\cdot6|=|-20|=20.
\]
By Smith normal form, the invariant factors satisfy $d_1=\Delta_1$ and $d_1d_2=\Delta_2$. Hence
\[
d_1=2,\qquad d_2=10,
\]
and the quotient group is
\[
\boxed{\Z^2/A\Z^2\cong\Z/2\Z\oplus\Z/10\Z}.
\]

We can check the answer without the determinantal formula. Start with the defining matrix and perform invertible integer row and column operations:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\6&4\end{pmatrix}
&\xrightarrow{R_2\gets R_2-R_1}
\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{R_1\leftrightarrow R_2}
\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\4&6\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{R_2\gets R_2-2R_1}
\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\0&10\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{C_2\gets C_2+C_1}
\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&10\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Every operation is invertible over $\Z$ and hence preserves the isomorphism type of the presented group. The resulting relations are simply $2u=0$ and $10v=0$.

As a final check, the group has $2\cdot10=20$ elements, matching the index $|\det A|=20$. The divisibility $2\mid10$ confirms this is the invariant-factor form.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:unitsmodn}
\idea For powers of $2$, the element $5$ generates the unit classes congruent to $1$ modulo $4$. Track the precise power of $2$ dividing $5^{2^j}-1$; combine $5$ with $-1$ to get all odd residue classes. Then use the Chinese remainder theorem for the modulus $360$.

\writeup \emph{(a) Units modulo $2^k$.} Start with $5=1+4$, so
\[
5^{2^0}=1+2^2t_0,\qquad t_0=1\text{ odd}.
\]
Suppose inductively that
\[
5^{2^j}=1+2^{j+2}t_j,\qquad t_j\text{ odd}.
\]
Squaring gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
5^{2^{j+1}}
&=(1+2^{j+2}t_j)^2\\
&=1+2^{j+3}t_j+2^{2j+4}t_j^2\\
&=1+2^{j+3}(t_j+2^{j+1}t_j^2).
\end{aligned}
\]
The parenthesis is odd, since the second summand is even. Thus the formula persists with $t_{j+1}$ odd for all $j\ge0$, and in particular
\[
v_2(5^{2^j}-1)=j+2.
\]
Consequently $5^{2^{k-2}}\equiv1\pmod{2^k}$, whereas $5^{2^{k-3}}\not\equiv1\pmod{2^k}$ for $k\ge3$. The order of $5$ modulo $2^k$ divides $2^{k-1}=|(\Z/2^k\Z)^*|$ and is therefore a power of $2$. The last two inequalities force that order to be exactly
\[
\operatorname{ord}_{2^k}(5)=2^{k-2}.
\]

Every power of $5$ is congruent to $1$ modulo $4$, while $-1$ is congruent to $3$ modulo $4$. Hence the cyclic subgroups $\langle5\rangle$ and $\langle-1\rangle$ intersect only at $1$. The unit group is abelian, so their product is a subgroup of order
\[
2^{k-2}\cdot2=2^{k-1},
\]
which equals the full number of odd classes modulo $2^k$. Therefore
\[
\boxed{(\Z/2^k\Z)^*
\cong\Z/2^{k-2}\Z\times\Z/2\Z}
\]
for $k\ge3$, with generators $5$ and $-1$.

\emph{(b) Units modulo $360$.} Factor $360=8\cdot9\cdot5$, with pairwise coprime factors. The Chinese remainder theorem for rings restricts to an isomorphism of unit groups:
\[
(\Z/360\Z)^*
\cong(\Z/8\Z)^*\times(\Z/9\Z)^*\times(\Z/5\Z)^*.
\]
Part (a) gives $(\Z/8\Z)^*\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2$. Modulo $9$, the powers of $2$ are $2,4,8,7,5,1$; modulo $5$ they are $2,4,3,1$. Thus those unit groups are cyclic of orders $6$ and $4$, directly checked here. Hence
\[
(\Z/360\Z)^*
\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2\times\Z/6\Z\times\Z/4\Z.
\]
Split the order-six factor as $\Z/2\Z\times\Z/3\Z$ and align the prime-power factors into invariant-factor columns:
\[
\boxed{(\Z/360\Z)^*
\cong\Z/2\Z\times\Z/2\Z\times\Z/2\Z\times\Z/12\Z}.
\]
Indeed $2\mid2\mid2\mid12$, and the order is
\[
2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot12=96=\varphi(360).
\]
The exponent of a finite abelian group in invariant-factor form is its largest invariant factor. Therefore the least positive $e$ satisfying $a^e\equiv1\pmod{360}$ for \emph{every} unit $a$ is
\[
\boxed{e=12}.
\]
The congruence with exponent $12$ follows from the decomposition; its minimality follows because an element of the $\Z/12\Z$ factor has order $12$. This is Carmichael's function $\lambda(360)$, considerably smaller than Euler's universal exponent $\varphi(360)=96$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:similarQ}
\idea The rational canonical form records a matrix using invariant factors in the polynomial ring $K[X]$. Extending scalars to $L$ cannot change a Smith form already computed using operations over $K[X]$.

\writeup Fix $A\in M_n(K)$. Form the polynomial matrix
\[
X I_n-A\in M_n(K[X]).
\]
Because $K[X]$ is a PID, Smith normal form gives invertible polynomial matrices
\[
P,Q\in GL_n(K[X])
\]
with
\[
P(XI_n-A)Q
=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,d_1,\ldots,d_r),
\]
where the non-unit invariant factors $d_i\in K[X]$ are monic and satisfy $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$.

Now regard all entries as polynomials over the larger field $L$. The same matrices $P,Q$ remain invertible over $L[X]$: their inverse matrices already have entries in $K[X]\subseteq L[X]$. The displayed diagonalisation is therefore a valid Smith form over $L[X]$ as well. The divisibility relations remain true, and a monic polynomial in $K[X]$ remains monic in $L[X]$.

By uniqueness of Smith normal form, the invariant factors of $A$ over $L$ are \emph{the same polynomials} $d_i$ we computed over $K$, not new factors obtained by splitting the $d_i$ into irreducibles.

Repeat this construction for $B\in M_n(K)$. Suppose that $A$ and $B$ are similar over $L$. The rational canonical form theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:frobenius}) says that similar matrices have the same invariant factors. Thus their invariant factors agree over $L$. But those are exactly the invariant factors already computed over $K$, so they agree over $K$ too.

Applying the rational canonical form theorem over $K$, we conclude that $A$ and $B$ are similar over $K$. In particular, if two rational matrices become similar after passing to complex coefficients, they were already similar by a rational change of basis.

Notice the distinction from \emph{diagonalisation}: a polynomial irreducible over $K$ may split over $L$, so diagonalisation can become possible only after extending the field. The invariant factors themselves do not change.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:nilpotent}
\idea A nilpotent map is killed by a power of $X$, so each invariant factor divides that power and is itself a power of $X$. The sizes of their exponents are a partition, and the dimensions of successive kernels tell us the number of blocks of each size. This uses annihilation directly, without assuming that the characteristic polynomial already splits.

\writeup Let $N:K^n\to K^n$ be nilpotent. Some power $N^m$ vanishes, so its minimal polynomial divides $X^m$. Under the structure theorem for the $K[X]$-module associated with $N$, every non-unit invariant factor divides a power of $X$ and must itself equal $X^{a_i}$, with
\[
1\le a_1\le\cdots\le a_r,\qquad a_1+\cdots+a_r=n.
\]
The positive integers $a_i$ form a partition of $n$. Conversely, for any such partition, the direct sum of nilpotent Jordan blocks of those sizes defines a nilpotent $n\times n$ matrix, and the structure theorem says that two such matrices are similar exactly when their partitions agree.

How do we \emph{read} the partition from a matrix? A block of size $a$ has a basis $v_1,\ldots,v_a$ such that $Nv_1=0$ and $Nv_j=v_{j-1}$ for $j\ge2$. Its $k$-th power kills exactly the first $\min(k,a)$ basis vectors. Hence
\[
\dim\ker N^k=\sum_{i=1}^r\min(k,a_i).
\]
Subtract consecutive values and define $d_k=\dim\ker N^k-\dim\ker N^{k-1}$, with $\ker N^0=\{0\}$. For each block of size $a_i$, this difference contributes $1$ exactly when $a_i\ge k$. Thus
\[
d_k=\#\{i:a_i\ge k\}.
\]
The sequence $d_1,d_2,\ldots$ determines the number of blocks of size \emph{exactly} $k$ as $d_k-d_{k+1}$. It therefore recovers the whole partition, with no need to guess a basis.

For $n=4$ there are five partitions and five similarity classes:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\text{block sizes}&\dim\ker N&\dim\ker N^2&\dim\ker N^3&\dim\ker N^4\\\hline
4&1&2&3&4\\
3+1&2&3&4&4\\
2+2&2&4&4&4\\
2+1+1&3&4&4&4\\
1+1+1+1&4&4&4&4
\end{array}
\]
The last partition gives $N=0$. The partitions $3+1$ and $2+2$ both have a two-dimensional kernel at the first step, but their second-kernel dimensions distinguish them. This illustrates why the entire sequence, not merely $\dim\ker N$, is needed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:diagonalisable}
\idea A diagonal matrix is killed by the product of $X-\lambda$ over its distinct diagonal entries. Conversely, if a matrix is killed by a product of \emph{distinct} linear factors, the Chinese remainder theorem splits its associated $K[X]$-module into eigenspaces.

\writeup If $n=0$, the empty matrix is diagonal, $\mu_A=1$ is the
empty product of linear factors, and all the assertions hold. Assume
$n\ge1$ from now on, so a nonzero constant cannot annihilate $A$.

Suppose $A=SDS^{-1}$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ and every $\lambda_i\in K$. A polynomial $f\in K[X]$ kills $A$ precisely when $f(D)=0$, that is, $f(\lambda_i)=0$ for every $i$. Therefore the monic polynomial of least degree killing $A$ is
\[
\mu_A(X)=\prod_{\lambda\text{ a distinct eigenvalue of }A}(X-\lambda).
\]
It splits over $K$ and has no repeated factor.

Conversely, suppose
\[
\mu_A=(X-\lambda_1)\cdots(X-\lambda_r)
\]
with the $\lambda_i$ distinct elements of $K$. The polynomials $(X-\lambda_i)$ are pairwise coprime. By the Chinese remainder theorem there exist polynomial projections $e_i(X)$ with $e_i\equiv1\pmod{X-\lambda_i}$ and $e_i\equiv0$ modulo all the other factors, and whose sum is $1$ modulo $\mu_A$. Applying these to $A$ gives
\[
K^n=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\ker(A-\lambda_i I).
\]
To check this, $(X-\lambda_i)e_i$ is divisible by every factor of
$\mu_A$, hence by $\mu_A$. Thus the image of $e_i(A)$ lies in
$\ker(A-\lambda_iI)$. If $v$ belongs to the $\lambda_j$-eigenspace,
then $e_i(A)v=e_i(\lambda_j)v$, which is $v$ when $i=j$ and zero
otherwise. Finally $\sum_i e_i(A)=I$, since $\mu_A(A)=0$.
Every vector therefore splits into these eigenspaces; applying each
$e_i(A)$ to a sum equal to zero proves that the sum is direct.
Choosing a basis inside each eigenspace gives a basis of eigenvectors.
Thus $A$ is diagonalisable over $K$.

If $A^2=A$, then $A$ is killed by $X^2-X=X(X-1)$. Its minimal polynomial divides this square-free polynomial, so the criterion applies over every field (since $0\ne1$).

Finally, if $A$ is a complex matrix with $A^k=I$ for some $k\ge1$, then $\mu_A\mid X^k-1$. Over $\C$, the latter has the distinct $k$-th roots of unity as its roots, since its derivative $kX^{k-1}$ does not vanish at them. Its divisors are products of distinct linear factors, so $A$ is diagonalisable over $\C$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:cyclicvector}
\idea A cyclic vector gives the entire space by repeatedly applying $A$, so the matrix looks like a companion matrix in that basis. The invariant-factor theorem says exactly when such a cyclic description exists.

\writeup For $n=0$, $\mu_A=\chi_A=1$, the empty family is a basis,
and $A=C(1)$ by convention; all three conditions hold. Assume $n\ge1$.
Write $V=K^n$ with $X$ acting as $A$. The structure theorem for finitely generated torsion modules over $K[X]$ gives
\[
V\cong K[X]/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus K[X]/(d_r),
\qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
\]
where every $d_i$ is monic and nonconstant. The characteristic and minimal polynomials are
\[
\chi_A=d_1\cdots d_r,\qquad \mu_A=d_r.
\]
Because every $\deg d_i\ge1$, the equality $\chi_A=\mu_A$ holds if and only if $r=1$. In this case $V\cong K[X]/(\chi_A)$ is a cyclic $K[X]$-module.

Choose the class of $1$ as its generator $v$. The classes of
\[
1,X,\ldots,X^{n-1}
\]
form a basis of the quotient because $\deg\chi_A=n$. In $V$ these correspond to
\[
v,Av,\ldots,A^{n-1}v,
\]
so condition (i) implies (ii).

Now suppose (ii) holds. Express $A^nv$ uniquely in this basis:
\[
A^nv=-c_0v-c_1Av-\cdots-c_{n-1}A^{n-1}v.
\]
The matrix of $A$ in the ordered basis $v,Av,\ldots,A^{n-1}v$ has ones on the subdiagonal and its last column is $(-c_0,\ldots,-c_{n-1})^{\mathsf T}$. This is the companion matrix of
\[
f(X)=X^n+c_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0.
\]
Thus (ii) implies (iii).

Finally, a companion matrix $C(f)$ acts on $K[X]/(f)$ by multiplication by $X$, and this quotient has a single invariant factor $f$. Therefore its minimal and characteristic polynomials both equal $f$. Similarity preserves both polynomials, so (iii) implies (i).

We have proved the equivalence of all three conditions. A useful example is a single Jordan block of size $n$: it has a cyclic vector. The zero matrix in dimension $n>1$ does not, because its minimal polynomial has degree $1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sublattices}
\idea A finite-index subgroup of $\Z^2$ is a two-dimensional lattice. Find its smallest positive horizontal step $a$ and its smallest positive vertical step $c$, then choose a representative with second coordinate $c$. Reducing the first coordinate modulo $a$ produces a unique triangular basis.

\writeup Let $L\le\Z^2$ have finite index $n$. Its second coordinates form a subgroup of $\Z$, hence $c\Z$ for some $c\ge0$. We must have $c>0$, otherwise $L$ lies in the horizontal axis and the quotient has infinite index.

The elements of $L$ whose second coordinate is zero form $a\Z\times\{0\}$ for some $a\ge1$. Indeed $n(1,0)\in L$ by Lagrange in the finite quotient $\Z^2/L$, so the horizontal subgroup is non-zero.

Choose $(b,c)\in L$. Subtracting a suitable multiple of $(a,0)$ changes $b$ by a multiple of $a$, so we may assume $0\le b<a$. We claim
\[
L=\Z(a,0)+\Z(b,c).
\]
The inclusion from right to left is clear. Conversely, for $(x,y)\in L$, the second coordinate $y$ is a multiple $tc$. Subtract $t(b,c)$; the result is in $L$ and has second coordinate $0$, so it is a multiple of $(a,0)$. This proves the claim.

The triple $(a,b,c)$ is unique. The positive generator $a$ of the horizontal slice and the positive generator $c$ of the second-coordinate image depend only on $L$. Any two choices $(b,c)$ and $(b',c)$ differ by $(b-b',0)\in L$, so $a\mid b-b'$. The restriction $0\le b,b'<a$ then forces $b=b'$.

The index of the lattice generated by the columns of
\[
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}
\]
is the absolute determinant, namely $ac$. Hence $ac=n$. Conversely, every triple
\[
a,c\ge1,\qquad ac=n,\qquad0\le b<a
\]
defines a subgroup of index $n$, and uniqueness prevents double counting.

For each positive divisor $a$ of $n$, the value $c=n/a$ is forced and there are exactly $a$ choices of $b$. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\#\{L\le\Z^2:[\Z^2:L]=n\}
=\sum_{a\mid n}a=\sigma(n)}.
\]
For example, when $n=2$, this gives $1+2=3$ subgroups, which are the three kernels of non-zero maps $\Z^2\to\Z/2\Z$.
\end{solution}
