\chapter{Field Extensions}

You can bisect a $60^\circ$ angle with straightedge and compass. Can you cut it into three equal $20^\circ$ angles with the same tools?

Let $u=2\cos20^\circ$. The triple-angle identity gives
\[
u^3-3u-1=0.
\]
The polynomial $X^3-3X-1$ has no rational root: the only possible integer roots are $1$ and $-1$, and neither works. A cubic without a rational root is irreducible over $\Q$. We will prove that this irreducibility forces $u$ to have degree $3$ over $\Q$. The relevance of that number will become clear once we understand which degrees a straightedge-and-compass construction can produce.

A field extension $L/K$ means that the field $L$ contains the smaller field $K$. We can then view $L$ as a vector space over $K$, and write its dimension as $[L:K]$. If $K\subseteq M\subseteq L$, those dimensions multiply:
\[
[L:K]=[L:M][M:K].
\]
This is the tower law. Since intersecting lines and circles only requires solving linear and quadratic equations, constructible numbers fit into towers whose successive degrees are at most $2$. Their degrees over $\Q$ must therefore be powers of $2$, ruling out $u$.

The same machinery prepares the next questions. What field must we add so that a polynomial splits completely into linear factors? When is that field determined up to isomorphism? These are the fields whose symmetries Galois theory will study.

\section{Degree and the tower law}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{field extension} $L/K$ is a field $L$ together with a subfield $K$. Then $L$ is a vector space over $K$ (add in $L$, multiply by elements of $K$); its dimension is the \emph{degree} $[L:K]\in\{1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$. The extension is \emph{finite} if $[L:K]<\infty$. A field $M$ with $K\subseteq M\subseteq L$ is an \emph{intermediate field}.
\end{definition}

For instance $[\C:\R]=2$, with basis $1,i$. We use $\infty$ to mean
that no finite basis exists; the finite-degree arguments below need no theorem
asserting that every infinite-dimensional vector space has a basis.

\begin{theorem}[tower law]\label{thm:tower}
Let $K\subseteq L\subseteq M$ be fields. Then $M/K$ is finite iff $M/L$ and $L/K$ are, and then
\[
[M:K]=[M:L]\,[L:K].
\]
More precisely, if $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ is a basis of $L$ over $K$ and $(f_j)_{j\in J}$ a basis of $M$ over $L$, then $(e_if_j)_{(i,j)\in I\times J}$ is a basis of $M$ over $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First suppose $M/K$ is finite. Its subspace $L$ has a finite $K$-basis
by the finite-dimensional basis-extension theorem of Volume~1. A finite
$K$-spanning set of $M$ also spans it over $L$, so $M/L$ is finite.
For the converse and the formula, choose bases of the two finite extensions.
The following argument also proves the conditional assertion for any given
bases, even infinite ones; it does not assume their general existence.

We first show that the products $e_if_j$ span $M$ over $K$. Take $z\in M$. Since $(f_j)$ is a basis over $L$, we may write
\[
z=\sum_j\lambda_jf_j,\qquad\lambda_j\in L,
\]
with only finitely many non-zero coefficients. Each $\lambda_j$ has its own expansion in the $K$-basis $(e_i)$:
\[
\lambda_j=\sum_i\mu_{ij}e_i,\qquad\mu_{ij}\in K.
\]
Substitution gives $z=\sum_{i,j}\mu_{ij}e_if_j$. So the products span.

Next suppose that a finite $K$-linear combination of these products is zero:
\[
0=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}e_if_j
=\sum_j\Bigl(\sum_i c_{ij}e_i\Bigr)f_j.
\]
Each parenthesis belongs to $L$. The $f_j$ are linearly independent over $L$, so every parenthesis must vanish. Now use the independence of the $e_i$ over $K$: each $c_{ij}$ must be zero. The products are therefore linearly independent.

They form a basis of $M$ over $K$, indexed by $I\times J$. Both index sets are non-empty because a field has dimension at least one over any subfield. Consequently $I\times J$ is finite exactly when both $I$ and $J$ are finite. In that case,
\[
[M:K]=|I\times J|=|I|\,|J|=[L:K]\,[M:L].
\]
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
A basis lets us count coordinates. If $L$ has basis $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ over $K$, and $M$ has basis $f_1,\ldots,f_s$ over $L$, then a coordinate in $M$ first chooses one of the $s$ vectors $f_j$, and its coefficient needs $r$ coordinates from $K$. We therefore get $rs$ coordinates altogether. The independence part of the proof is essential: merely writing down $rs$ spanning vectors does not show that the extension has degree $rs$. We will justify the four-coordinate example $\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ after the simple-extension theorem; multiplying two quadratic degrees is legitimate only after both are proved.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:towerdiv}
If $K\subseteq L\subseteq M$ and $M/K$ is finite, then $[L:K]$ and $[M:L]$ divide $[M:K]$. In particular, if $[M:K]$ is prime, there is no intermediate field other than $K$ and $M$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The tower law gives
\[
[M:K]=[M:L]\,[L:K].
\]
All three degrees here are finite positive integers.
Each factor on the right therefore divides
the product on the left. This proves the first claim.

Now suppose $[M:K]=p$ is prime.
The positive integer $[L:K]$ divides $p$,
so it equals $1$ or $p$.
If $[L:K]=1$, then $L=K$: a vector space
of dimension one over $K$ containing $K$
has no room for any further elements.
If $[L:K]=p$, the tower formula forces
$[M:L]=1$, hence $M=L$.

Thus no field can sit strictly between
$K$ and $M$ in an extension of prime degree.
The converse is not asserted: an extension
of composite degree need not have a
non-trivial intermediate field.
\end{proof}

Degrees measure the size of an extension, but a concrete extension often begins with one number: $\sqrt2$, $\sqrt[3]2$, or a complex root of a polynomial. The polynomial of smallest degree that vanishes at that number tells us its degree over the base field.

\section{Algebraic elements}

Let $L/K$ be an extension and $\alpha\in L$. We write $K[\alpha]=\{P(\alpha):P\in K[X]\}$, the smallest subring of $L$ containing $K$ and $\alpha$, and $K(\alpha)$ for the smallest subfield containing them. More generally $K(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ is the smallest subfield of $L$ containing $K$ and the $\alpha_i$; it equals $K(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{n-1})(\alpha_n)$, since both are the smallest subfield containing $K$ and all the $\alpha_i$.

These smallest objects exist inside the given field $L$. Polynomial
values form a subring, and any subring containing $K$ and $\alpha$
contains every such value. For the field, intersect all subfields of
$L$ containing the specified elements. The family is nonempty because
it contains $L$; its intersection is closed under field operations,
including the inverse of each nonzero element, and is the required
smallest subfield.

\begin{definition}
$\alpha$ is \emph{algebraic} over $K$ if $P(\alpha)=0$ for some non-zero $P\in K[X]$, and \emph{transcendental} otherwise. An extension is \emph{algebraic} if all its elements are. A field is
\emph{algebraically closed} if every nonconstant polynomial over it has a root
in it; repeated division then gives complete splitting. Volume~1 proved this
property for $\mathbb C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[minimal polynomial]\label{prop:minpoly}
Let $\alpha\in L$ be algebraic over $K$. There is a unique monic polynomial $m_\alpha\in K[X]$ such that, for $P\in K[X]$,
\[
P(\alpha)=0\iff m_\alpha\mid P.
\]
It is irreducible, and it is the only monic irreducible polynomial of $K[X]$ with root $\alpha$. Its degree is the \emph{degree of $\alpha$ over $K$}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Because $\alpha$ is algebraic,
some non-zero polynomial over $K$
vanishes at $\alpha$. Consider
the evaluation homomorphism
\[
\operatorname{ev}_{\alpha}:K[X]\to L,
\qquad P\mapsto P(\alpha).
\]
Its kernel $I$ is a non-zero ideal
of the PID $K[X]$. Hence $I=(m)$
for some non-zero polynomial $m$. The ideal is proper because
$\operatorname{ev}_{\alpha}(1)=1\ne0$, so $m$ is not a unit and
$\deg m\ge1$. Multiplying $m$ by a suitable non-zero
constant, we choose it monic.

By the meaning of a principal ideal,
\[
P(\alpha)=0
\iff P\in I=(m)
\iff m\mid P.
\]
This is exactly the required property.
Any two monic generators of the same ideal
are associates and therefore equal, since
the only units of $K[X]$ are non-zero
constants. Thus $m$ is unique.

Why is $m$ irreducible? If $m=fg$
with $\deg f,\deg g\ge1$, then
\[
0=m(\alpha)=f(\alpha)g(\alpha).
\]
A field has no zero divisors, so
one factor, say $f(\alpha)$, vanishes.
The displayed divisibility property
would give $m\mid f$, impossible because
$\deg f<\deg m$. Thus $m$ cannot
have a proper positive-degree factorisation.

Finally, if another monic irreducible
$Q$ satisfies $Q(\alpha)=0$,
then $m\mid Q$. Since $Q$ is irreducible,
$Q$ and $m$ differ only by a non-zero
constant, which must be $1$ because
both are monic. So $Q=m$.
Its degree is the uniquely determined
degree of $\alpha$ over $K$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[simple extensions]\label{thm:simple}
Let $\alpha\in L$ be algebraic over $K$, of degree $d$. Then
\[
K[X]/(m_\alpha)\;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\;K[\alpha],\qquad P+(m_\alpha)\mapsto P(\alpha),
\]
is a ring isomorphism, $K[\alpha]=K(\alpha)$ is a field, and $1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{d-1}$ is a basis of $K(\alpha)$ over $K$. In particular $[K(\alpha):K]=d$. If $\alpha$ is transcendental, $[K(\alpha):K]=\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The evaluation map $K[X]\to L$,
$P\mapsto P(\alpha)$, has image
$K[\alpha]$ and kernel $(m_\alpha)$
by Proposition~\ref{prop:minpoly}.
The first isomorphism theorem for rings
therefore gives
\[
K[X]/(m_\alpha)\cong K[\alpha].
\]

The polynomial $m_\alpha$ is irreducible
over the field $K$. In the PID $K[X]$,
an ideal generated by an irreducible
polynomial is maximal. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:primemax},
the quotient $K[X]/(m_\alpha)$
is a field. Therefore $K[\alpha]$
is a field as well. It contains $K$
and $\alpha$, so it must coincide
with the smallest such field:
\[
K[\alpha]=K(\alpha).
\]

Let $d=\deg m_\alpha$. Euclidean
division says that every polynomial
$P\in K[X]$ has a unique remainder
$R$ of degree less than $d$:
\[
P=Qm_\alpha+R,\qquad\deg R<d.
\]
Evaluating at $\alpha$ gives
$P(\alpha)=R(\alpha)$, so
$1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{d-1}$
span $K(\alpha)$ over $K$.

They are linearly independent too.
If
\[
c_0+c_1\alpha+\cdots+c_{d-1}\alpha^{d-1}=0,
\]
the polynomial $c_0+\cdots+c_{d-1}X^{d-1}$
belongs to $(m_\alpha)$.
A non-zero multiple of $m_\alpha$
cannot have degree less than $d$,
so all its coefficients vanish.
The listed powers form a basis,
and $[K(\alpha):K]=d$.

If $\alpha$ is transcendental,
no non-zero polynomial vanishes at it.
Therefore \emph{every} finite family
$1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^m$ is linearly
independent over $K$. The extension
$K(\alpha)/K$ cannot have finite
dimension, proving the last claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:finitealg}
A finite extension $L/K$ is algebraic, and the degree of each $\alpha\in L$ divides $[L:K]$. Conversely, if $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n$ are algebraic over $K$, then $K(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)/K$ is finite.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $[L:K]=n<\infty$.
For any $\alpha\in L$ the $n+1$
elements
\[
1,\alpha,\alpha^2,\ldots,\alpha^n
\]
lie in an $n$-dimensional $K$-vector space.
They are therefore linearly dependent:
there are coefficients $c_j\in K$,
not all zero, with
$\sum_{j=0}^n c_j\alpha^j=0$.
This is a non-zero polynomial over
$K$ vanishing at $\alpha$, so
$\alpha$ is algebraic.

The simple extension $K(\alpha)$
lies between $K$ and $L$.
The tower law then gives
\[
[L:K]=[L:K(\alpha)]\,[K(\alpha):K].
\]
Thus the degree of $\alpha$,
which is $[K(\alpha):K]$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:simple}, divides $n$.

Conversely suppose
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m$ are algebraic
over $K$. Set $K_0=K$ and
$K_j=K(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_j)$.
The polynomial over $K$ that annihilates
$\alpha_j$ is also a polynomial over
the larger field $K_{j-1}$.
So $\alpha_j$ remains algebraic
over $K_{j-1}$, and the simple
extension $K_j/K_{j-1}$ has finite
degree by Theorem~\ref{thm:simple}.
Multiplying these finitely many
degrees with the tower law gives
$[K_m:K]<\infty$, as required.
\end{proof}

For each integer $n\ge1$, a positive real root of $X^n-2$ exists by
the intermediate-value theorem on $[0,3]$. That root has degree
$n$ over $\mathbb Q$: Eisenstein at $2$ gives irreducibility, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:simple} gives the degree. These degrees are unbounded,
so $[\mathbb R:\mathbb Q]=\infty$. These arbitrarily large degrees rule out a finite basis for $\mathbb R$ over
$\mathbb Q$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:simple} also says how to compute in $K(\alpha)$: an element is a polynomial in $\alpha$ of degree $<d$, products are reduced modulo $m_\alpha$, and inverses come from Bézout. In $\Q(\sqrt2)$, for instance, $m=X^2-2$ and $1/(1+\sqrt2)=\sqrt2-1$ because $(X+1)(X-1)=X^2-1\equiv1\pmod{X^2-2}$. This is how computer algebra systems handle algebraic numbers exactly.

\begin{think}
What is the degree of $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ over $\Q$? It is a sum of two numbers of degree $2$; is it of degree $2$, or more?
\end{think}

\begin{example}\label{ex:sqrt2sqrt3}
Let $\alpha=\sqrt2+\sqrt3$. Then $\alpha^2=5+2\sqrt6$, so $(\alpha^2-5)^2=24$ and $\alpha$ is a root of $X^4-10X^2+1$. Its degree is $4$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item $\sqrt3\notin\Q(\sqrt2)$. Otherwise $\sqrt3=a+b\sqrt2$ with $a,b\in\Q$, and squaring gives $3=a^2+2b^2+2ab\sqrt2$, so $ab=0$ (as $\sqrt2\notin\Q$). If $b=0$, $\sqrt3=a\in\Q$; if $a=0$, $3=2b^2$, so $\sqrt{3/2}=|b|\in\Q$. Both are false. Hence $X^2-3$ has no root in $\Q(\sqrt2)$, it is irreducible there, and by the tower law $[\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3):\Q]=2\cdot2=4$.
\item $\Q(\alpha)=\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$. Expanding, $\alpha^3=11\sqrt2+9\sqrt3$, so
\[
\sqrt2=\tfrac12(\alpha^3-9\alpha),\qquad\sqrt3=\alpha-\sqrt2
\]
lie in $\Q(\alpha)$; the reverse inclusion is clear.
\end{enumerate}
So $[\Q(\alpha):\Q]=4$, and $X^4-10X^2+1$, monic of degree $4$ with root $\alpha$, is $m_\alpha$. Figure~\ref{fig:pentagon} (right) shows the three intermediate fields of degree $2$; Chapter~10 proves that there are no others.
\end{example}

The example is typical: a field generated by several algebraic numbers is often generated by a single one, a \emph{primitive element}. Over $\Q$ this is always true (Theorem~\ref{thm:primitiveelement}).

Adjoining one root may leave several others missing. For the symmetries of a polynomial to act on all its roots, we need a field containing all of them. Such a splitting field is the stage on which Galois theory will work.

\section{Splitting fields}\label{sec:splitting}

So far the big field $L$ was given. Often only $K$ and a polynomial are given, and we want a field in which the polynomial has roots. Kronecker's idea is to \emph{define} a root as the class of $X$, as $\C$ was defined as $\R[X]/(X^2+1)$ in Chapter~6.

\begin{theorem}[Kronecker]\label{thm:kronecker}
Let $P\in K[X]$ be irreducible of degree $d$. Then $L=K[X]/(P)$ is a field; $c\mapsto c+(P)$ identifies $K$ with a subfield of $L$; the class $x=X+(P)$ is a root of $P$ in $L$; and $L=K(x)$, $[L:K]=d$. Consequently every non-constant polynomial over $K$ has a root in some finite extension of $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The polynomial ring $K[X]$ is a PID.
Since $P$ is irreducible, $(P)$
is a maximal ideal, so
\[
L=K[X]/(P)
\]
is a field by Proposition~\ref{prop:primemax}.

Consider the map
\[
K\longrightarrow L,\qquad
c\longmapsto c+(P).
\]
It is a homomorphism of rings.
No non-zero constant belongs to
$(P)$, because $P$ has positive
degree. So the map is injective,
and we may identify $K$ with its
image inside $L$.

Let $x=X+(P)$ be the residue class
of the indeterminate. If
$P(X)=\sum_j a_jX^j$, then
\[
P(x)=\sum_j a_jx^j
=P(X)+(P)=0+(P).
\]
Thus $x$ is a genuine root of $P$
in the field $L$.

Every element of $L$ is a polynomial
residue class, so it is a polynomial
in $x$ with coefficients in $K$.
This shows $L=K[x]$, which is a field
and hence also $L=K(x)$.
The monic multiple of $P$ is the
minimal polynomial of $x$ over $K$,
so Theorem~\ref{thm:simple} gives
\[
[L:K]=\deg P=d.
\]

Finally, take any non-constant
polynomial $F\in K[X]$. By
factorisation in the Euclidean
domain $K[X]$, it has an
irreducible factor $P$. The field
$K[X]/(P)$ is a finite extension
and contains a root of $P$,
hence a root of $F$.
This constructs a root without
assuming an algebraically closed
field exists.
\end{proof}

If the starting field is $\mathbb F_p$ and $\deg P=d$, each class has
one representative of degree less than $d$. There are $p$ choices for
each of its $d$ coefficients, so the quotient field has exactly $p^d$
elements. This extends the four-class calculation in Chapter~6.
Chapter~9 will prove that suitable irreducible polynomials exist for
every $d\ge1$ and classify the resulting finite fields.

\begin{definition}
Let $P\in K[X]$ have degree $n\ge1$. A \emph{splitting field} of $P$ over $K$ is an extension $L/K$ such that $P=c(X-\alpha_1)\cdots(X-\alpha_n)$ in $L[X]$ and $L=K(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:splitexists}
Every $P\in K[X]$ of degree $n\ge1$ has a splitting field $L$, with $[L:K]\le n!$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We prove the claim by induction on the
degree $n$, allowing the starting field
to change at each step.

If $n=1$, the polynomial already splits
over $K$, so its splitting field is $K$
and the degree is $1=1!$.

Suppose $n\ge2$ and the result is known
for all polynomials of degree $n-1$
over any field. By Theorem~\ref{thm:kronecker},
the polynomial $P$ has a root $\alpha_1$
in a finite extension $K_1=K(\alpha_1)$.
Its minimal polynomial divides $P$,
so
\[
[K_1:K]\le n.
\]
Since $P(\alpha_1)=0$, polynomial division
in $K_1[X]$ gives
\[
P(X)=(X-\alpha_1)Q(X)
\]
with $\deg Q=n-1$.

By induction, $Q$ has a splitting
field $L$ over $K_1$ of degree at
most $(n-1)!$. Thus $L$ contains
$\alpha_1$ and all the roots of $Q$,
hence all the roots of $P$.
Moreover $L$ is generated over $K$
by these roots, so it is a
splitting field of $P$ over $K$.

Apply the tower law:
\[
[L:K]=[L:K_1][K_1:K]
\le(n-1)!\,n=n!.
\]
This finishes the induction.

The argument constructs a splitting
field one root at a time. The bound
$n!$ is often much larger than the
actual degree; it is a universal bound,
not a typical value.
\end{proof}

To compare two splitting fields we need to transport fields along isomorphisms. If $\sigma:K\to K'$ is a field isomorphism and $P=\sum a_kX^k\in K[X]$, write $\sigma P=\sum\sigma(a_k)X^k$. Then $P\mapsto\sigma P$ is a ring isomorphism $K[X]\to K'[X]$, so it preserves divisibility and irreducibility.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:extend1}
Let $\sigma:K\to K'$ be an isomorphism, $Q\in K[X]$ irreducible, $\alpha$ a root of $Q$ in an extension of $K$ and $\beta$ a root of $\sigma Q$ in an extension of $K'$. There is exactly one isomorphism $\tau:K(\alpha)\to K'(\beta)$ that extends $\sigma$ and sends $\alpha$ to $\beta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The extension is forced once we specify
where $K$ and $\alpha$ go. We will
first show that the forced formula
is well defined.

Because $Q$ is irreducible and
$Q(\alpha)=0$, its monic multiple
is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$.
Likewise $\sigma Q$ is irreducible
over $K'$ and vanishes at $\beta$.
The simple-extension theorem gives
\[
K(\alpha)\cong K[X]/(Q),
\qquad
K'(\beta)\cong K'[X]/(\sigma Q).
\]

Apply $\sigma$ to the coefficients
of every polynomial:
\[
\Sigma:K[X]\to K'[X],\qquad
\sum_j c_jX^j\mapsto\sum_j\sigma(c_j)X^j.
\]
This is a ring isomorphism and sends
$(Q)$ onto $(\sigma Q)$.
It therefore induces an isomorphism
\[
K[X]/(Q)\longrightarrow K'[X]/(\sigma Q).
\]
Composing the three maps produces
\[
\tau:K(\alpha)\to K'(\beta),
\qquad
\tau(P(\alpha))=(\sigma P)(\beta).
\]
The quotient construction proves that
this expression does not depend on
which polynomial represents an element.
In particular $\tau(c)=\sigma(c)$
for $c\in K$ and $\tau(\alpha)=\beta$.

For uniqueness, every element of
$K(\alpha)$ is a polynomial in $\alpha$
with coefficients in $K$. Any ring
homomorphism extending $\sigma$ and
sending $\alpha$ to $\beta$ must act by
exactly the displayed formula:
\[
\sum_jc_j\alpha^j\longmapsto
\sum_j\sigma(c_j)\beta^j.
\]
So no second extension is possible.
\end{proof}

The \emph{characteristic} of a field is the least positive integer $n$
with $n\cdot1=0$, if there is one, and is $0$ otherwise. A positive
characteristic must be prime: if $n=ab$ with $1<a,b<n$, then
$(a\cdot1)(b\cdot1)=0$ contradicts the absence of zero divisors and
the minimality of $n$. In characteristic zero, the integer map is injective
and $a/b\mapsto(a\cdot1)(b\cdot1)^{-1}$ embeds $\mathbb Q$.
In characteristic $p$, the same integer map embeds $\mathbb F_p$.

In characteristic $0$, irreducible polynomials have no repeated roots.
Distinct roots will make the count of isomorphisms between splitting
fields equal their degree; repeated roots can make the count smaller.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sep}
Let $K$ have characteristic $0$ and $Q\in K[X]$ be irreducible. Then $Q$ has distinct roots in every extension of $K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $d=\deg Q\ge1$ and write
\[
Q(X)=a_dX^d+\cdots+a_0,
\qquad a_d\ne0.
\]
Its \emph{formal derivative}, obtained by the coefficient rule, is
\[
Q'(X)=d\,a_dX^{d-1}+\cdots+a_1.
\]
Because $K$ has characteristic zero,
the coefficient $da_d$ is non-zero.
Thus $Q'\ne0$ and $\deg Q'=d-1$.

Since $Q$ is irreducible, any
common divisor of $Q$ and $Q'$
is either a unit or an associate
of $Q$. The latter is impossible
because $Q'$ has lower degree.
So $\gcd(Q,Q')=1$.

The polynomial ring $K[X]$ is
Euclidean, hence Bézout's identity
supplies $U,V\in K[X]$ with
\[
UQ+VQ'=1.
\]
Suppose $\alpha$ were a repeated
root of $Q$ in some extension $L$.
Then in $L[X]$ we could write
$Q=(X-\alpha)^2R$. The formal product rule follows by checking monomials and distributing.
Differentiating,
\[
Q'=2(X-\alpha)R+
(X-\alpha)^2R',
\]
so $Q(\alpha)=Q'(\alpha)=0$.
But evaluating the Bézout identity
at $\alpha$ would give
\[
1=U(\alpha)Q(\alpha)+
V(\alpha)Q'(\alpha)=0,
\]
a contradiction. Thus $Q$ has
no repeated root in any extension.

Characteristic zero is important:
in characteristic $p$, a non-constant
polynomial such as $X^p-t$ can have
zero derivative and repeated roots
in a splitting field.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In characteristic $p$ this can fail: over the field $\mathbb F_p(t)$ of rational functions, $X^p-t$ is irreducible: apply the UFD version of Eisenstein
in $(\mathbb F_p[t])[X]$ at the prime element $t$, then pass to its
fraction field by Gauss's lemma. It nevertheless equals $(X-\theta)^p$ in a splitting field, because $(a+b)^p=a^p+b^p$ in characteristic $p$. Over finite fields it does not happen (Chapter~9).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[uniqueness of splitting fields]\label{thm:splitunique}
Let $\sigma:K\to K'$ be an isomorphism, $P\in K[X]$ of degree $\ge1$, $L$ a splitting field of $P$ over $K$ and $L'$ a splitting field of $\sigma P$ over $K'$. Then $\sigma$ extends to an isomorphism $L\to L'$, and the number of such extensions is at most $[L:K]$, with equality if $\sigma P$ has distinct roots in $L'$.
In particular a splitting field is unique up to isomorphism, and if $P$ has distinct roots in $L$, then $L$ has exactly $[L:K]$ automorphisms that fix $K$ pointwise.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We prove existence and the count of extensions
together, by induction on $D=[L:K]$.
This degree is finite because $L$ is
generated by finitely many algebraic roots.

\emph{Base case.}
If $D=1$, then $L=K$ and $P$ already
splits over $K$. Applying the isomorphism
$\sigma$ to a factorisation of $P$
shows $\sigma P$ splits over $K'$.
Thus $L'=K'$, and $\sigma$ itself
is the only possible extension.
The number is $1=D$.

\emph{Choose the first new root.}
Suppose $D>1$. Some root $\alpha$
of $P$ belongs to $L\setminus K$;
otherwise $L$, which is generated by
all roots, would equal $K$.
Let $Q=m_\alpha$ be its monic minimal
polynomial of degree
\[
d=[K(\alpha):K]>1.
\]
Since $P(\alpha)=0$, we have $Q\mid P$.
Apply $\sigma$ to coefficients:
$\sigma Q\mid\sigma P$.
In $L'$, let $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_e$
be the \emph{distinct} roots of
$\sigma Q$. There is at least one,
and there are at most $d$ of them.

For each $\beta_j$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:extend1} gives a unique
isomorphism
\[
\sigma_j:K(\alpha)\to K'(\beta_j)
\]
extending $\sigma$ with
$\sigma_j(\alpha)=\beta_j$.
Every extension $\tau:L\to L'$
of $\sigma$ must send $\alpha$
to one of these $\beta_j$, because
\[
0=\tau(Q(\alpha))
=(\sigma Q)(\tau(\alpha)).
\]
So its restriction to $K(\alpha)$
must equal exactly one $\sigma_j$.

Now $L$ is a splitting field of $P$
over $K(\alpha)$, and $L'$ a splitting
field of $\sigma_jP=\sigma P$
over $K'(\beta_j)$. The tower law gives
\[
[L:K(\alpha)]=D/d<D.
\]
Apply the induction hypothesis to
each $\sigma_j$. It has at least one
extension $L\to L'$ and at most
$D/d$ such extensions. The sets
of extensions for distinct $j$
are disjoint, because their values
on $\alpha$ differ. Hence the total
number of extensions of $\sigma$
is positive and at most
\[
e(D/d)\le d(D/d)=D.
\]

\emph{When all roots are distinct.}
If $\sigma P$ has distinct roots,
then its irreducible factor $\sigma Q$
has exactly $d$ distinct roots in $L'$,
so $e=d$. The induction hypothesis
also gives exactly $D/d$ extensions
for each $\sigma_j$. The total
number is therefore exactly $D$.

Taking $K'=K$, $\sigma=\mathrm{id}$,
and $L'=L$ proves the final
automorphism assertion.
The existence of at least one
extension gives the uniqueness of
splitting fields up to isomorphism.
\end{proof}

In characteristic $0$ the hypothesis ``distinct roots'' holds as soon as $P$ is a product of distinct monic irreducibles: each has distinct roots (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}), and two of them cannot share a root, since both would be its minimal polynomial. The automorphisms of $L$ fixing $K$ form a group, the \emph{Galois group} of $P$, and it permutes the roots of $P$; the theorem says that it is as large as the degree allows. Chapter~10 is built on this.

\begin{example}\label{ex:cuberoot}
The splitting field of $X^3-2$ over $\Q$. The roots are $\sqrt[3]2$, $\omega\sqrt[3]2$, $\omega^2\sqrt[3]2$ with $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$, so the splitting field in $\C$ is $L=\Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)$ (it contains $\omega=\omega\sqrt[3]2/\sqrt[3]2$, and conversely). $X^3-2$ is irreducible (Eisenstein at $2$), so $[\Q(\sqrt[3]2):\Q]=3$. The field $\Q(\sqrt[3]2)$ is real, so it does not contain $\omega$, a root of $X^2+X+1$: $[L:\Q(\sqrt[3]2)]=2$ and $[L:\Q]=6$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:splitunique}, $L$ has $6$ automorphisms. Each permutes the three roots and is determined by what it does to them, so every one of the $3!=6$ permutations of the roots comes from an automorphism: the Galois group is $\mathfrak S_3$.
\end{example}

A quadratic extension is built by adjoining a square root, exactly the operation that appears when two lines or circles intersect. The tower law therefore turns a geometric construction question into a restriction on algebraic degrees.

\section{Ruler and compass}

Identify the plane with $\C$. Start with the points $0$ and $1$. A \emph{ruler-and-compass step} draws a line through two distinct points already constructed, or a circle whose centre is a constructed point and whose radius is the distance between two constructed points, and adds the isolated intersections of two distinct such curves. Coincident
lines or circles supply no new points; a zero-radius circle supplies only its
already known centre. A complex number is \emph{constructible} if it is obtained after finitely many steps. A real number is constructible if it is constructible as a point of $\C$; for $n\ge3$, a regular $n$-gon is constructible if $e^{2i\pi/n}$ is (then so are all its vertices, by Proposition~\ref{prop:constrfield}).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:constrfield}
The constructible numbers form a subfield $\mathcal C$ of $\C$, closed under complex conjugation and under square roots. A complex number $x+iy$ ($x,y$ real) is constructible iff $x$ and $y$ are.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Everything begins with the standard straightedge-and-compass
operations: draw a line through two known points, and draw
a circle with known centre and radius equal to a known
distance. We explain how these operations produce the
algebraic operations claimed in the statement.

\emph{Step 1: perpendiculars and parallels.}
For distinct constructed points $a,b$, draw
the circles centred at $a$ and $b$,
each passing through the other point.
Their two intersections lie on the
perpendicular bisector of the segment $[a,b]$;
joining them constructs that bisector
and its midpoint.

Given a constructed line $\ell$ and
a constructed point $p$, choose two distinct known points $a,b$ on $\ell$. At least one of
the circles centred at $p$ through $a$ or through $b$ meets $\ell$
in two distinct points: at most one of $a,b$ can be the perpendicular
foot, the tangency case. Use a circle with two intersections.
Their perpendicular bisector passes
through the foot of the perpendicular
from $p$. This constructs a perpendicular
to $\ell$ through $p$. A parallel to
$\ell$ is then obtained by constructing
a perpendicular to a perpendicular.
These constructions also work when
$p$ lies on $\ell$.

\emph{Step 2: use real and imaginary coordinates.}
The line through $0$ and $1$ is the real
axis. Construct its perpendicular at $0$
to obtain the imaginary axis, and intersect
the unit circle with it to obtain $i$.
Perpendicular projections of a constructed
point $z=x+iy$ onto the axes give the
real point $x$ and the imaginary point
$iy$. Using a circle centred at $0$,
we can transfer the length $|y|$ to
the real axis and choose the sign,
so $y$ is constructible as well.

Conversely, if $x$ and $y$ are
constructible reals, construct $iy$
by transferring $y$ to the imaginary
axis, then intersect the vertical line
through $x$ with the horizontal line
through $iy$. Their intersection is
$x+iy$. Thus $z$ is constructible
exactly when its two real coordinates are.
The same argument constructs
$\bar z=x-iy$.

\emph{Step 3: the real field operations.}
Let $F$ be the set of constructible
real numbers. Addition and subtraction
are obtained by transporting signed
lengths along the real axis with circles.
For products and quotients, use
parallels and their coordinate equations.
For example, for real $a,b$, draw
the line through the real point $1$
and the imaginary point $ib$.
Its parallel through the real point
$a$ meets the imaginary axis at
$iab$: the first line has equation $y=b(1-x)$,
and its parallel through $(a,0)$ has equation $y=b(a-x)$.
Transfer that signed coordinate to the real axis to obtain $ab$.
Similarly, for $a\ne0$,
the parallel through the real point
$b$ to the line joining the real
point $a$ to $i$ has equation $y=(b-x)/a$ and gives $ib/a$.
The equations include negative inputs; zero products give the already
known point $0$.
Thus $F$ is a subfield of $\mathbb R$.

By Step 2,
\[
\mathcal C=\{x+iy:x,y\in F\}.
\]
The identities
\[
(x+iy)(u+iv)=(xu-yv)+i(xv+yu),
\]
and, for $x+iy\ne0$,
\[
\frac1{x+iy}=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}
\]
show that $\mathcal C$ is a subfield
of $\mathbb C$ and is closed under
complex conjugation.

\emph{Step 4: square roots.}
For $a>0$ in $F$, construct the
circle with diameter from $-1$ to $a$
on the real axis. Its intersection
with the imaginary axis has
coordinates $(0,\pm\sqrt a)$:
indeed its centre is $(a-1)/2$,
its radius is $(a+1)/2$, and
substituting $x=0$ into the circle
equation gives $y^2=a$.
Consequently $\sqrt a\in F$.

Now take $z\in\mathcal C$, $z\ne0$.
Intersect the circle centred at $0$ through $z$ with the real axis:
its positive intersection is $r=|z|>0$, so $r$ is constructible,
so $\sqrt r\in F$, and
$u=z/r$ is constructible with $|u|=1$.
If $u=-1$, the number $i\sqrt r$
is a square root of $z$.
Otherwise set
\[
v=\frac{1+u}{|1+u|}.
\]
The denominator is a positive constructible
real length, and $v$ is constructible
by the field operations just proved.
Because $|u|=1$,
\[
|1+u|^2=(1+u)(1+\bar u)
=(1+u)^2/u,
\]
so $v^2=u$. Hence
$(\sqrt r\,v)^2=ru=z$.
If $z=0$, its square root is $0$.
Thus $\mathcal C$ is closed under
square roots, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Wantzel]\label{thm:wantzel}
A complex number $z$ is constructible iff there is a chain of subfields of $\C$
\[
\Q=K_0\subset K_1\subset\dots\subset K_r,\qquad[K_j:K_{j-1}]=2,
\]
with $z\in K_r$. In particular, a constructible number is algebraic over $\Q$ and its degree is a power of $2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A straightedge-and-compass construction produces new coordinates by
solving equations of degree at most two. We show that this observation
works in both directions.

\emph{From a tower to a construction.}
Suppose
\[
\mathbb Q=K_0\subset K_1\subset\cdots\subset K_r\subseteq\mathbb C,
\qquad [K_j:K_{j-1}]=2.
\]
We show by induction that every element of every $K_j$ is
constructible. The rational numbers in $K_0$ are constructible by
the usual field operations, so the first step holds.

For $j\ge1$, choose $\alpha\in K_j\setminus K_{j-1}$.
Its minimal polynomial over $K_{j-1}$ has degree $2$ by the tower law:
$K_{j-1}(\alpha)$ is a non-trivial intermediate extension of a
degree-two extension and hence equals $K_j$. Write that polynomial as
\[
X^2+bX+c,\qquad b,c\in K_{j-1}.
\]
The quadratic formula expresses $\alpha$ as
\[
\alpha=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4c}}2.
\]
By induction $b$ and $c$ are constructible. The constructible
complex numbers are closed under addition, multiplication, division
by non-zero numbers, and square roots (Proposition~\ref{prop:constrfield}).
Thus $\alpha$ is constructible. Since $K_j=K_{j-1}(\alpha)$,
all of $K_j$ consists of constructible numbers. In particular $z$
is constructible if $z\in K_r$.

\emph{From a construction to a tower.}
Start with the marked points $0$ and $1$, and label every new
intersection point in the order it is constructed:
\[
z_0=0,\quad z_1=1,\quad z_2,\dots,z_m=z,
\qquad z_j=x_j+iy_j.
\]
Let
\[
F_j=\mathbb Q(x_0,y_0,\dots,x_j,y_j)\subseteq\mathbb R.
\]
We examine how the next pair of coordinates is obtained from the
previous ones. A line through previously constructed points has
an equation $ux+vy=w$ with $u,v,w\in F_{j-1}$.
A circle with previously constructed centre and radius determined
by constructed points has an equation
\[
x^2+y^2+ux+vy+w=0
\]
with its coefficients in $F_{j-1}$.

There are only three kinds of intersections.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{Two distinct non-parallel lines.}
Their coordinates solve two linear equations. Cramer's rule places
both coordinates in $F_{j-1}$.
\item \emph{A line and a circle.}
Solve the line equation for $y$ (or for $x$ if necessary) and
substitute into the circle equation. This gives a polynomial
of degree at most $2$ with coefficients in $F_{j-1}$.
The line really meets the circle at the chosen point, so the
relevant discriminant is a non-negative real number
$\Delta\in F_{j-1}$. Both coordinates belong to
$F_{j-1}(\sqrt\Delta)$.
A tangency has $\Delta=0$ and introduces no new extension.
\item \emph{Two distinct circles.}
Subtracting their equations eliminates $x^2+y^2$ and gives a
line. If the centres were identical, distinct concentric circles
would not intersect; hence the resulting line is non-trivial.
The intersection reduces to case (2).
\end{enumerate}
Therefore $F_j/F_{j-1}$ has degree $1$ or $2$ at each stage:
every stage either changes nothing or adjoins one square root.

The complex number $z=x_m+iy_m$ lies in $F_m(i)$.
Since $i$ satisfies $X^2+1=0$, adjoining it also gives an
extension of degree at most $2$. Remove every stage of degree
$1$ from
\[
\mathbb Q=F_1\subseteq F_2\subseteq\cdots
\subseteq F_m\subseteq F_m(i).
\]
What remains is the desired chain of quadratic extensions
containing $z$. It may have no steps, as when $z$ is rational.

Finally, the tower law gives $[K_r:\mathbb Q]=2^r$.
Because $\mathbb Q(z)$ is an intermediate field,
\[
[\mathbb Q(z):\mathbb Q]\mid2^r.
\]
Thus $z$ is algebraic and its degree is a power of $2$.

Notice that the last degree condition alone is \emph{necessary},
not sufficient, for constructibility. The criterion requires the
whole chain of quadratic extensions.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Straightedge-and-compass constructions use intersections of lines and circles. Their coordinates come from linear or quadratic equations, never from a new irreducible cubic in one step. Starting with rational coordinates, each construction step therefore leaves the field unchanged or enlarges it by a square root. The tower law multiplies those degrees, so a constructible algebraic number must have degree a power of $2$. That is an \emph{obstruction}, not a complete test: an irreducible polynomial of degree $4$ may still have roots that cannot be constructed if the relevant field extension does not fit into a quadratic tower. The full theorem uses a quadratic \emph{tower}, not merely a degree bound.

\begin{corollary}[the classical problems]\label{cor:classical}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Doubling the cube) $\sqrt[3]2$ is not constructible.
\item (Trisection) The angle $60^\circ$, which is constructible, cannot be trisected: $\cos20^\circ$ is not constructible.
\item (Polygons) The regular pentagon is constructible; the regular heptagon is not. More generally, if $p\ge3$ is a prime and the regular $p$-gon is constructible, then $p-1$ is a power of $2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
We use Wantzel's criterion
(Theorem~\ref{thm:wantzel}): every
constructible complex number has
degree a power of two over $\mathbb Q$.
The examples show how that necessary
condition obstructs classical constructions.

\emph{(a) Doubling the cube.}
A cube of twice the original volume
has side length $\sqrt[3]2$ times
the original length. The polynomial
$X^3-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$
by Eisenstein's criterion at $2$.
Thus $\sqrt[3]2$ has degree $3$,
not a power of two, and cannot
be constructed by ruler and compass.

\emph{(b) Trisecting $60^\circ$.}
The angle $60^\circ$ is constructible
as an angle of an equilateral triangle.
Suppose we could trisect it. Then
the point $e^{i20^\circ}$ on the unit
circle, and in particular its real
part $\cos20^\circ$, would be constructible.

The triple-angle identity gives
\[
\cos60^\circ
=4\cos^3 20^\circ-3\cos20^\circ.
\]
Set $u=2\cos20^\circ$. After
multiplying the identity by $2$,
we obtain
\[
u^3-3u-1=0.
\]
This monic cubic has no rational root:
a rational root would be an integer
dividing $1$, so either $1$ or $-1$,
and neither satisfies the equation.
A reducible cubic over a field has
a linear factor; hence this polynomial
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.
Therefore $u$ has degree $3$,
so it is not constructible. Its
scalar multiple $\cos20^\circ=u/2$
cannot be constructible either.
This contradiction proves that
a general trisection construction
does not exist.

\emph{(c) Pentagon, heptagon and prime polygons.}
Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/5}$. The equation
\[
1+\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^3+\zeta^4=0
\]
divided by $\zeta^2$ becomes
\[
(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})^2+
(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})-1=0.
\]
Writing $t=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2\cos72^\circ$,
we obtain $t=(\sqrt5-1)/2$.
This is constructible, and so are
\[
\frac t2=\cos72^\circ,\qquad
\sqrt{1-\frac{t^2}4}=\sin72^\circ.
\]
Thus the vertex $\zeta$ can be constructed,
and successive rotations construct a
regular pentagon.

For any prime $p$, the minimal polynomial
of the primitive $p$-th root
$\zeta_p=e^{2\pi i/p}$ is
\[
\Phi_p(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}.
\]
It is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$: indeed
\[
\Phi_p(X+1)=\frac{(X+1)^p-1}{X}
=\sum_{k=1}^p\binom pk X^{k-1}.
\]
Its leading coefficient is $1$, all lower coefficients are divisible
by $p$, and its constant coefficient is $p$, not divisible by $p^2$.
Eisenstein applies, and the invertible substitution $X\mapsto X+1$
preserves irreducibility. This is also the prime cyclotomic argument
of Volume~1, Chapter~6. Thus
\[
[\mathbb Q(\zeta_p):\mathbb Q]=p-1.
\]
A constructible regular $p$-gon
would give a constructible $\zeta_p$,
so $p-1$ must be a power of $2$.
For $p=7$, this degree is $6$,
which rules out the regular heptagon.

The argument gives a necessary condition
for a prime-sided polygon; the sufficiency
of the full Gauss--Wantzel criterion is
proved later in Chapter~10.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:quartic-nonconstructible}
Every root $\alpha\in\C$ of $X^4-X-1$ has degree $4$ over $\Q$ and
is not constructible. In particular, some real numbers of degree $4$
are not constructible.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First prove irreducibility. Modulo $2$, the polynomial is $X^4+X+1$,
which has no root in $\mathbb F_2$. A factorisation would therefore
have to consist of two irreducible quadratics. The only monic
irreducible quadratic over $\mathbb F_2$ is $X^2+X+1$: a quadratic
with zero constant has root $0$, and $X^2+1$ has root $1$.
But $(X^2+X+1)^2=X^4+X^2+1$, a different polynomial.
The monic reduction test from Chapter~6 proves irreducibility over
$\Q$. Thus every root has degree $4$.

Suppose a root $\alpha$ were constructible, and let $E$ be the last
field of a quadratic tower over $\Q$ containing it. We claim that
every root $\beta$ of the same polynomial would also be constructible.
Lemma~\ref{lem:extend1} gives an isomorphism
$\Q(\alpha)\to\Q(\beta)$ fixing $\Q$ and sending $\alpha$ to $\beta$.
It extends to an isomorphism $E\to E'\subseteq\C$ as follows.
A finite $\Q$-basis of $E$ supplies finitely many field generators
over $\Q(\alpha)$. Adjoin them one at a time. At each stage the
transformed minimal polynomial has a root in $\C$, by Volume~1's
complex-root theorem, and Lemma~\ref{lem:extend1} extends the map.
The images of the original quadratic tower still have successive
degree $2$, because an isomorphism takes a basis to a basis.
Their final field contains $\beta$, so Wantzel's theorem makes
$\beta$ constructible.

Write the four roots as $r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4$. Under our assumption they
all lie in the field of constructible numbers. Comparing coefficients
in $\prod_{j=1}^4(X-r_j)=X^4-X-1$ gives
\[
\sum_jr_j=0,\qquad \sum_{i<j}r_ir_j=0,\qquad
\sum_{i<j<k}r_ir_jr_k=1,\qquad r_1r_2r_3r_4=-1.
\]
Pair the roots, and put
\[
s=r_1+r_2=-(r_3+r_4),\qquad u=r_1r_2,\qquad v=r_3r_4.
\]
The second coefficient identity says $u+v-s^2=0$; the third says
$s(v-u)=1$; and the fourth says $uv=-1$. The constructible number
$y=u+v$ therefore satisfies
\[
1=s^2(v-u)^2=y\bigl((u+v)^2-4uv\bigr)=y(y^2+4).
\]
Hence $y$ is a root of $Y^3+4Y-1$. This monic cubic has no rational
root: its only candidates are $1,-1$, giving values $4,-6$.
It is irreducible, so $y$ has degree $3$ over $\Q$. That contradicts
the power-of-two degree condition for constructible numbers.
Thus no root $\alpha$ is constructible.

Finally, $X^4-X-1$ has values $-1$ at $1$ and $13$ at $2$.
The intermediate-value theorem supplies a real root between them,
giving the promised real example.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Two classical results complete the picture; we quote them without proof here. \emph{Squaring the circle} asks to construct $\sqrt\pi$; that is impossible because $\pi$ is not even algebraic (Lindemann, 1882). And the converse of (c) holds: if $p=2^{2^k}+1$ is a (Fermat) prime, the $p$-gon is constructible. Gauss found the $17$-gon in 1796, aged 18; Chapter~10 proves the general statement with Galois theory, because ``degree a power of $2$'' alone is not enough to be constructible.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
Wantzel replaces a construction problem by a question about successive
quadratic extensions. Degree gives a quick obstruction; the quartic
example shows why the whole tower matters. Chapter~10 will attach a
group to an extension and use that structure to decide whether radicals
can supply its roots. The quotient $\Q[X]/(m_\alpha)$ already gives
exact arithmetic with one algebraic number. Over $\mathbb F_p$, the
same construction with an irreducible $P$ gives a finite field;
Chapter~9 develops the fields used in error-correcting codes and AES.
\end{beauty}

\begin{theorem}[primitive element]\label{thm:primitiveelement}
Let $K\subseteq\C$ be a field and $\alpha,\beta\in\C$ algebraic over $K$. There is $c\in\Q$ such that $K(\alpha,\beta)=K(\alpha+c\beta)$. Consequently every finite extension $L/K$ of subfields of $\C$ is of the form $K(\gamma)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The goal is to replace two generators
by one without losing either of them.
We use characteristic zero to choose
a rational coefficient that separates
all relevant conjugates.

Let $f=m_\alpha$ and $g=m_\beta$
over $K$. Since $K\subseteq\mathbb C$
has characteristic zero, their roots
in $\mathbb C$ are distinct
(Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}). List them as
\[
\alpha=\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m,
\qquad
\beta=\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n.
\]
If $n=1$, then $\beta\in K$,
so $K(\alpha,\beta)=K(\alpha)$;
choose $c=0$.

Suppose $n>1$. For a pair
$(i,j)$ with $j\ne1$, consider
\[
\alpha_i+c\beta_j
=\alpha+c\beta.
\]
Because $\beta_j\ne\beta$,
this equation determines at most
one complex value of $c$:
\[
c=\frac{\alpha-\alpha_i}
        {\beta_j-\beta}.
\]
Only finitely many pairs occur,
so only finitely many values of $c$
are forbidden. The rational numbers
form an infinite set. Choose a
rational $c$ avoiding all forbidden
values. In particular $c\ne0$,
because the pair $(i,j)=(1,2)$
forbids $c=0$.

Set $\gamma=\alpha+c\beta$.
Clearly $\gamma\in K(\alpha,\beta)$.
We will recover $\beta$ from $\gamma$.
Over the field $K(\gamma)$ consider
the two polynomials
\[
g(X)=m_\beta(X),\qquad
h(X)=f(\gamma-cX).
\]
They both vanish at $X=\beta$:
$g(\beta)=0$ by definition, and
$h(\beta)=f(\alpha)=0$.

Suppose some other complex number
$\beta_j$ were a common root.
The equation $h(\beta_j)=0$
would give
\[
\gamma-c\beta_j=\alpha_i
\]
for some root $\alpha_i$ of $f$.
This rearranges to one of the
equations excluded when choosing $c$,
unless $j=1$. Thus $\beta$ is
the \emph{only} common complex root
of $g$ and $h$.

Let $D$ be their monic gcd in
$K(\gamma)[X]$. It has positive degree: if the gcd were $1$, a polynomial
Bézout combination of $g$ and $h$ over $K(\gamma)$ would equal $1$;
evaluation at their common root $\beta$ would give $0=1$. Every root of $D$
is a common root, hence equals $\beta$.
Because $D$ divides $g$, and $g$
has distinct roots, $D$ also has
no repeated root. Therefore
\[
D(X)=X-\beta.
\]
But $D$ has coefficients in
$K(\gamma)$, so $\beta\in K(\gamma)$.
Now $\alpha=\gamma-c\beta$ also
belongs to $K(\gamma)$. Hence
\[
\boxed{K(\alpha,\beta)=K(\gamma)}.
\]

For a finite extension $L/K$,
choose a $K$-basis
$1,\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_n$.
It is also a finite generating set,
so $L=K(\alpha_2,\ldots,\alpha_n)$.
Combine the generators two at a
time by the result just proved.
If $L=K$, choose $\gamma=0$. Otherwise the finite list is nonempty,
and induction leaves one $\gamma$ with $L=K(\gamma)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:sqrt23}
Let $\alpha=\sqrt2+\sqrt[3]2$. Show that $[\Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt[3]2):\Q]=6$, that $\sqrt2=\dfrac{\alpha^3+6\alpha-2}{3\alpha^2+2}$, and deduce that $\alpha$ has degree $6$ over $\Q$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:inverse}
In $\Q(\sqrt[3]2)$, write $\dfrac1{1+\sqrt[3]2}$ and $\dfrac1{1+\sqrt[3]2+\sqrt[3]4}$ in the form $a+b\sqrt[3]2+c\sqrt[3]4$ with $a,b,c\in\Q$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:classical}
Call an angle $\theta$ constructible if $e^{i\theta}$ is.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if $\theta$ and $\varphi$ are constructible, so are $\theta\pm\varphi$ and $\theta/2$. Deduce that $3^\circ$ is constructible (use $72^\circ$ and $60^\circ$).
\item Show that the angle of $n$ degrees, $n\in\Z$, is constructible iff $3\mid n$. In particular the angle $72^\circ$ can be trisected, but the angle $1^\circ$ is not constructible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:polygons}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $u=2\cos\frac{2\pi}7$ satisfies $u^3+u^2-2u-1=0$, and that $2\cos\frac{2\pi}9$ satisfies $u^3-3u+1=0$. Deduce that the regular $7$-gon and $9$-gon are not constructible.
\item Show that $\cos\frac{2\pi}{17}$ has degree $8$ over $\Q$. (This is consistent with constructibility, but does not prove it: see Chapter~10.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:algfield}
Call a set \emph{countable} if it is finite or its elements can be listed
in a sequence. Show that the complex numbers algebraic over $\Q$ form a subfield $\overline{\Q}$ of $\C$, that $\overline{\Q}$ is countable, and that a complex root of a non-zero polynomial with coefficients in $\overline{\Q}$ lies in $\overline{\Q}$. Deduce that transcendental numbers exist.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:splitdeg}
Find the degree over $\Q$ of the splitting field of $X^4-2$, and of $X^p-1$ for a prime $p$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:oddeg}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if $[L:K]$ is prime, then $L=K(\alpha)$ for every $\alpha\in L\setminus K$.
\item Show that if $[K(\alpha):K]$ is odd, then $K(\alpha^2)=K(\alpha)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:primitive}
\emph{Finding a primitive element, and a boundary in positive characteristic.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item In Theorem~\ref{thm:primitiveelement}, let the minimal polynomials of
$\alpha,\beta$ have degrees $m,n$. Show that one of the integers
$c\in\{0,1,\ldots,m(n-1)\}$ satisfies $K(\alpha,\beta)=K(\alpha+c\beta)$.
\item Over $K=\mathbb Q$, show that every nonzero rational $c$ works for
$\alpha=\sqrt2$, $\beta=\sqrt3$. Recover both square roots explicitly from
$\gamma=\sqrt2+c\sqrt3$.
\item Let $p$ be prime, $S,T$ independent formal indeterminates,
$L=\mathbb F_p(S,T)=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb F_p[S,T])$ and
$K=\mathbb F_p(S^p,T^p)\subseteq L$. Prove that the $p^2$ monomials
$S^iT^j$, $0\le i,j<p$, form a $K$-basis of $L$, while every
$\gamma\in L$ satisfies $\gamma^p\in K$. Deduce that $L/K$ has no
primitive element.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\end{hint}

\end{hint}

\end{hint}

\end{hint}

\end{hint}

(a) Corollary~\ref{cor:towerdiv}. (b) What is $[K(\alpha):K(\alpha^2)]$ at most?
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:primitive}
(a) Count the forbidden values in the theorem's proof. (b) Multiply
$\sqrt2+c\sqrt3$ by $\sqrt2-c\sqrt3$. (c) Reduce exponents modulo $p$;
for inverses use $1/g=g^{p-1}/g^p$. To prove independence, clear denominators
and compare the two exponents modulo $p$ in the polynomial ring.
\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:sqrt23}
\idea The two radical extensions have degrees $2$ and $3$. Their compositum must have degree divisible by both, while adjoining the second radical increases degree by at most its polynomial degree. Then solve the cubic identity for $\sqrt2$ in terms of $\alpha$.

\writeup Put $u=\sqrt2$, $v=\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $L=\Q(u,v)$. Both radicals are algebraic over $\Q$: $X^2-2$ and $X^3-2$ are irreducible by Eisenstein at $2$. Thus $[\Q(u):\Q]=2$ and $[\Q(v):\Q]=3$.

The tower law shows that both $2$ and $3$ divide $[L:\Q]$, since $\Q(u)$ and $\Q(v)$ are intermediate fields. Hence $6\mid[L:\Q]$. On the other hand, adjoining $u$ to $\Q(v)$ gives an extension of degree at most $2$, so
\[
[L:\Q]=[L:\Q(v)]\,[\Q(v):\Q]\le2\cdot3=6.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{[L:\Q]=6}.
\]

Let $\alpha=u+v$. Then $v=\alpha-u$, and cubing gives
\[
2=v^3=(\alpha-u)^3
=\alpha^3-3\alpha^2u+3\alpha u^2-u^3.
\]
Use $u^2=2$ and $u^3=2u$ to obtain
\[
2=\alpha^3+6\alpha-(3\alpha^2+2)u.
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{\sqrt2=\frac{\alpha^3+6\alpha-2}{3\alpha^2+2}}.
\]
The denominator is non-zero because $\alpha$ is real and $3\alpha^2+2>0$. Consequently $u\in\Q(\alpha)$; subtracting gives $v=\alpha-u\in\Q(\alpha)$. Hence
\[
L=\Q(u,v)\subseteq\Q(\alpha)\subseteq L,
\]
so equality holds and $\boxed{[\Q(\alpha):\Q]=6}$.

As a check, eliminating $u$ and $v$ yields the polynomial
\[
X^6-6X^4-4X^3+12X^2-24X-4,
\]
which vanishes at $\alpha$. Because the degree was just proved to be $6$, this monic polynomial is its minimal polynomial over $\Q$, not merely some equation of degree six.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:inverse}
\idea Put $\alpha=\sqrt[3]{2}$, so that $\alpha^3=2$. An inverse in the degree-three field $\Q(\alpha)$ can be found by multiplying a denominator by a suitable quadratic expression. Here the two classical factorizations $1+x^3$ and $x^3-1$ do the work immediately.

\writeup The minimal polynomial $X^3-2$ is irreducible over $\Q$ (Eisenstein at $2$, or the rational-root test). Thus $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$ form a basis of $\Q(\alpha)$ over $\Q$. In particular, any answer has a unique expression
\[
a+b\alpha+c\alpha^2,\qquad a,b,c\in\Q.
\]

For the first denominator, use
\[
(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha+\alpha^2)=1+\alpha^3=3.
\]
Since $3\ne0$, division gives
\[
\boxed{\frac1{1+\sqrt[3]{2}}
=\frac13-\frac13\sqrt[3]{2}+\frac13\sqrt[3]{4}}.
\]
The identity can be checked by multiplying the right side by $1+\alpha$, after which all non-constant terms cancel and $1+\alpha^3=3$ remains.

For the second denominator, notice the exact factorisation
\[
(\alpha-1)(1+\alpha+\alpha^2)=\alpha^3-1=1.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{\frac1{1+\sqrt[3]{2}+\sqrt[3]{4}}
=\sqrt[3]{2}-1}.
\]
This is also in the required form: $a=-1$, $b=1$, and $c=0$. Again the product with the given denominator is $1$, so no approximation of cube roots is involved.

Both computations illustrate the quotient-field viewpoint: an inverse is a polynomial in $\alpha$ whose product with the denominator is congruent to $1$ modulo the minimal polynomial $X^3-2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:classical}
\idea Constructible points are closed under field arithmetic and square roots. Apply those facts to unit complex numbers to build new angles. For the converse, reduce the assumed angle to $1^\circ$ using Bézout and derive a contradiction from the non-constructibility of $20^\circ$.

\writeup \emph{(a) Closure and a $3^\circ$ construction.} Let $\mathcal C$ denote the field of constructible complex numbers, and suppose $e^{i\theta},e^{i\varphi}\in\mathcal C$. Their product
\[
e^{i\theta}e^{i\varphi}=e^{i(\theta+\varphi)}
\]
is constructible. Their quotient
\[
e^{i\theta}/e^{i\varphi}=e^{i(\theta-\varphi)}
\]
is constructible too, since the denominator is non-zero. Thus constructible angles are closed under addition and subtraction.

The number $e^{i\theta/2}$ is one of the two square roots of $e^{i\theta}$. Since $\mathcal C$ is closed under square roots and contains $-1$, both choices are constructible. So $\theta/2$ is constructible as well.

A regular pentagon can be constructed with straightedge and compass, so $72^\circ=360^\circ/5$ is constructible; $60^\circ$ is the angle of an equilateral triangle, so it is constructible too. By subtraction $12^\circ=72^\circ-60^\circ$ is constructible. Halve twice to obtain $6^\circ$ and then $3^\circ$.

\emph{(b) Integer angles.} If $3\mid n$, write $n=3m$ with $m\in\Z$. Repeating the construction of $3^\circ$ $|m|$ times and reversing orientation when $m<0$ constructs $n^\circ$.

For the converse, suppose that $n^\circ$ is constructible and $3\nmid n$.
Then $\gcd(n,3)=1$, so Bézout gives integers $a,b$ with $an+3b=1$.
Integer multiples and addition of the two constructed angles would give
\[
a(n^\circ)+b(3^\circ)=1^\circ.
\]
Taking twenty copies would construct $20^\circ$.

But Corollary~\ref{cor:classical}(b) proves that $20^\circ$ is not constructible: a construction would trisect the constructible angle $60^\circ$, contradicting the irreducible cubic obstruction. This contradiction shows that our assumption $3\nmid n$ was impossible.

We have proved the exact criterion
\[
\boxed{n^\circ\text{ is constructible for }n\in\Z
\iff 3\mid n}.
\]
In particular $72^\circ$ \emph{can} be trisected in this specific case, because $72^\circ/3=24^\circ$ and $24$ is a multiple of $3$. By contrast $1^\circ$ cannot be constructed. The famous impossibility of arbitrary angle trisection does not mean that no particular angle is trisectable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:polygons}
\idea A regular $n$-gon gives the complex number $\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}$. The sum $\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1}=2\cos(2\pi/n)$ is real and often has a much smaller minimal polynomial. Find that polynomial first; only then use the degree obstruction to constructibility.

\writeup \emph{(a) The heptagon.} Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$ and $u=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$. Since
\[
1+\zeta+\zeta^2+\cdots+\zeta^6=0,
\]
division by $\zeta^3$ gives
\[
(\zeta^3+\zeta^{-3})+(\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2})
+(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})+1=0.
\]
We can express everything in $u$:
\[
\zeta^2+\zeta^{-2}=u^2-2,\qquad
\zeta^3+\zeta^{-3}=u^3-3u.
\]
Substituting and simplifying gives
\[
u^3+u^2-2u-1=0.
\]
The only possible rational roots of this monic integer cubic are $1$ and $-1$, and neither is a root. A reducible cubic over $\Q$ has a rational root, so this polynomial is irreducible. Hence $[\Q(u):\Q]=3$.

\emph{The nonagon.} Set $v=2\cos(2\pi/9)$ and $\theta=2\pi/9$. The triple-angle identity
\[
2\cos3\theta=(2\cos\theta)^3-3(2\cos\theta)
\]
gives $-1=v^3-3v$, since $3\theta=2\pi/3$. Thus
\[
v^3-3v+1=0.
\]
Again neither $1$ nor $-1$ is a root, so the cubic is irreducible and $[\Q(v):\Q]=3$.

The real part of a constructible complex point is constructible (Proposition~\ref{prop:constrfield}). If the regular heptagon or nonagon were constructible, its corresponding $u$ or $v$ would be constructible. Wantzel's theorem would then force its degree over $\Q$ to be a power of $2$. Degree $3$ is not, so neither polygon is constructible.

\emph{(b) The regular $17$-gon.} Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/17}$ and $c=\cos(2\pi/17)$. The cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_{17}=1+X+\cdots+X^{16}$ is irreducible over $\Q$ by Eisenstein applied after substituting $X+1$ (as proved in Volume~1). Hence
\[
[\Q(\zeta):\Q]=16.
\]
Because $2c=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$, the field $\Q(c)$ lies inside $\Q(\zeta)$. And $\zeta$ satisfies
\[
X^2-2cX+1=0
\]
over $\Q(c)$. It cannot belong to $\Q(c)$, which is a real field, because $\zeta$ is non-real. Therefore $[\Q(\zeta):\Q(c)]=2$. The tower law yields
\[
[\Q(c):\Q]=\frac{16}{2}=\boxed{8}.
\]
This degree passes the \emph{necessary} power-of-two test. By itself it is not a construction of the $17$-gon: that needs a suitable tower of quadratic extensions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:algfield}
\idea Replace an algebraic element by the finite field extension it generates. A finite collection of algebraic elements still generates a finite extension, which proves closure under field operations. Counting polynomials and their roots then gives countability.

\writeup Let
\[
\overline{\Q}=\{\alpha\in\C:\alpha\text{ is algebraic over }\Q\}.
\]
Clearly $0,1\in\overline{\Q}$. Suppose $\alpha,\beta\in\overline{\Q}$. Their simple extensions $\Q(\alpha)/\Q$ and $\Q(\beta)/\Q$ are finite. The tower law shows
\[
[\Q(\alpha,\beta):\Q]
=[\Q(\alpha,\beta):\Q(\alpha)]\,[\Q(\alpha):\Q]
<\infty,
\]
because $\beta$ satisfies its original polynomial over $\Q$, hence also over the larger coefficient field $\Q(\alpha)$.

The field $\Q(\alpha,\beta)$ contains $\alpha+\beta$, $\alpha-\beta$, $\alpha\beta$, and $\alpha/\beta$ when $\beta\ne0$. Every element of a finite extension is algebraic over the base field (Corollary~\ref{cor:finitealg}). These four expressions therefore lie in $\overline{\Q}$. This proves that $\overline{\Q}$ is a subfield of $\C$.

Now let $P(X)=a_dX^d+\cdots+a_0$ be a non-zero polynomial with coefficients in $\overline{\Q}$, and suppose $P(\gamma)=0$ for some $\gamma\in\C$. Put $M=\Q(a_0,\ldots,a_d)$. Repeated application of the preceding finite-extension argument makes $M/\Q$ finite. The equation $P(\gamma)=0$ says that $\gamma$ is algebraic over $M$, so $[M(\gamma):M]<\infty$. The tower law gives
\[
[M(\gamma):\Q]<\infty.
\]
Thus $\gamma$ is algebraic over $\Q$ and belongs to $\overline{\Q}$.
Every nonconstant polynomial over $\overline{\Q}$ has a complex root
by Volume~1's complex-root theorem, and every such root lies in
$\overline{\Q}$ by the argument just given. Repeated division by
linear factors therefore splits it \emph{within} $\overline{\Q}$.
So $\overline{\Q}$ is algebraically closed.

For countability, clear denominators: every algebraic number is a root
of a nonzero integer polynomial. List those polynomials in order of the bound
$\deg P+\sum_j|a_j|$, with any fixed ordering inside each bound. Each bound
contains only finitely many coefficient lists, and every integer polynomial
appears. List the finitely many roots of each polynomial, ordering them by
real part and then imaginary part. This lists every algebraic number,
possibly repeatedly, and proves that $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ is countable.

Here is the missing connection from Cantor's set argument in Volume~1 to real
numbers. For $A\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots\}$, set
\[
r_A=\sum_{n\in A}\frac2{3^n}.
\]
The series converges by comparison with the geometric series. If $A$ and $B$
first differ at $k$, their values differ by at least
$2/3^k-\sum_{j>k}2/3^j=1/3^k>0$. Thus different subsets give different reals.
If all reals could be listed, assign to each listed real its subset $A$ when
it has this form, and the empty set otherwise. This would list all subsets of
the positive integers, impossible: the subset whose $n$th membership is the
opposite of the $n$th listed subset's $n$th membership is missed. Hence
$\mathbb R$, and therefore $\mathbb C$, is uncountable. Hence
\[
\C\setminus\overline{\Q}\ne\varnothing,
\]
indeed it is uncountable: otherwise interleaving lists of it and of
$\overline{\mathbb Q}$ would list $\mathbb C$. Every element of this complement is transcendental over $\Q$. This counting proof establishes their existence without needing to exhibit a specific transcendental number.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:splitdeg}
\idea A splitting field is obtained by adjoining \emph{all} roots, but often one root and one additional element already generate them. Identify those generators and use the tower law to multiply their extension degrees.

\writeup First consider
\[
f(X)=X^4-2.
\]
Let $\alpha=\sqrt[4]{2}$ be the positive real fourth root of $2$. The four roots of $f$ in $\C$ are
\[
\alpha,\quad-\alpha,\quad i\alpha,\quad-i\alpha.
\]
Adjoining $\alpha$ and $i$ therefore supplies all four roots, and conversely the quotient $(i\alpha)/\alpha=i$ shows that a field containing all roots must contain $i$. Thus the splitting field is exactly
\[
L=\Q(\alpha,i).
\]

The polynomial $X^4-2$ is irreducible over $\Q$ by Eisenstein's criterion at $2$: each non-leading coefficient is divisible by $2$, but the constant coefficient $-2$ is not divisible by $4$. Hence
\[
[\Q(\alpha):\Q]=4.
\]
The field $\Q(\alpha)$ is contained in $\R$, because $\alpha$ is real and its elements are rational functions of $\alpha$. So $i\notin\Q(\alpha)$. The polynomial $X^2+1$ has degree $2$ and has no root in this real field, giving
\[
[L:\Q(\alpha)]=2.
\]
By the tower law,
\[
\boxed{[L:\Q]=2\cdot4=8}.
\]
The Galois group therefore also has order $8$. Chapter~10 identifies it more precisely with the dihedral group of order $8$.

Now let $p$ be any prime and take $g(X)=X^p-1$. Its roots are
\[
1,\zeta,\zeta^2,\ldots,\zeta^{p-1},
\qquad \zeta=e^{2\pi i/p}.
\]
Thus the splitting field is $\Q(\zeta)$. The minimal polynomial of $\zeta$ is the $p$-th cyclotomic polynomial
\[
\Phi_p(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}.
\]
For prime $p$ this polynomial is irreducible over $\Q$ (apply Eisenstein at $p$ to $\Phi_p(X+1)$). Its degree is $p-1$, so
\[
\boxed{[\Q(\zeta):\Q]=p-1}.
\]
For $p=2$ this reads $1$, as expected: $X^2-1$ already splits over $\Q$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:oddeg}
\idea The tower law says that intermediate-field degrees must multiply to the total extension degree. A prime total degree leaves no non-trivial factorisation. For an odd degree, a possible quadratic step is ruled out by parity.

\writeup (a) Suppose $[L:K]=p$ is prime and choose $\alpha\in L\setminus K$. The generated field $K(\alpha)$ satisfies
\[
K\subsetneq K(\alpha)\subseteq L.
\]
Apply the tower law:
\[
p=[L:K(\alpha)]\,[K(\alpha):K].
\]
Both factors are positive integers. Since $[K(\alpha):K]>1$ and $p$ is prime, the only possibility is $[K(\alpha):K]=p$ and $[L:K(\alpha)]=1$. Therefore $L=K(\alpha)$. This proves that \emph{every} element outside $K$, not just some specially chosen element, generates a prime-degree extension.

(b) Assume $\alpha$ is algebraic over $K$ and $d=[K(\alpha):K]$ is odd. Let $M=K(\alpha^2)$. Certainly
\[
K\subseteq M\subseteq K(\alpha).
\]
Over $M$, the element $\alpha$ satisfies $X^2-\alpha^2$, because $\alpha^2\in M$. Thus the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $M$ has degree $1$ or $2$, and
\[
[K(\alpha):M]\in\{1,2\}.
\]
By the tower law this degree divides $[K(\alpha):K]=d$, which is odd. It cannot equal $2$, so it equals $1$. Hence $M=K(\alpha)$:
\[
\boxed{K(\alpha^2)=K(\alpha)}.
\]
The argument also works in characteristic $2$; it relies on odd extension degree, not on a formula involving $\pm\alpha$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:primitive}
\idea The primitive-element proof already describes the bad coefficients:
count them instead of merely saying there are finitely many. For an explicit
sum, multiplication by its sign-changed partner recovers the generators.
In positive characteristic, compare polynomial exponents before passing to
fractions; this keeps the dimension argument concrete.

\writeup \emph{(a) A finite search.}
If $n=1$, then $\beta\in K$, and $c=0$ works. Otherwise the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:primitiveelement} excludes at most one value of $c$ for each
pair $(i,j)$ with $1\le i\le m$, $2\le j\le n$. There are $m(n-1)$ pairs.
The $m(n-1)+1$ distinct integers in the proposed list cannot all be forbidden.
For any remaining value, the same gcd argument recovers $\beta$ and then
$\alpha$ from $\alpha+c\beta$. These integers are distinct in $K$ because
$K\subseteq\mathbb C$ has characteristic zero.

\emph{(b) Recovering the two square roots.}
Take $c\in\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\}$ and put $D=2-3c^2$. Neither $D$ nor
$\gamma=\sqrt2+c\sqrt3$ is zero: either equality would make $2/3$ a rational
square, impossible by the odd exponent of $3$ in its prime factorisation.
Since
\[
(\sqrt2+c\sqrt3)(\sqrt2-c\sqrt3)=D,
\]
we have
\[
\boxed{\sqrt2=\frac{\gamma+D/\gamma}{2},\qquad
\sqrt3=\frac{\gamma-\sqrt2}{c}.}
\]
Thus both generators belong to $\mathbb Q(\gamma)$, proving equality of the
fields. Their degree is $4$ by Example~\ref{ex:sqrt2sqrt3}. At $c=0$ we get
only $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, so excluding zero is necessary.

\emph{(c) A finite extension without one generator.}
The field $K$ consists of rational functions in $S^p,T^p$ with coefficients
in $\mathbb F_p$. Every monomial $S^aT^b$ with nonnegative exponents is a
$K$-multiple of one with $0\le i,j<p$, by division of both exponents by $p$.
Hence these $p^2$ monomials span all polynomials over $K$.

For a nonzero $g\in\mathbb F_p[S,T]$, the characteristic-$p$ binomial identity
and $a^p=a$ for $a\in\mathbb F_p$ give $g^p\in\mathbb F_p[S^p,T^p]$.
Here $a^p=a$ follows from Lagrange applied to the multiplicative group
$\mathbb F_p^*$ of order $p-1$, with $a=0$ handled directly. Consequently
\[
\frac1g=\frac{g^{p-1}}{g^p}
\]
is in the same $K$-span. Multiplying a polynomial numerator by this expression
shows that the proposed monomials span every rational function in $L$.

For independence, suppose
\[
\sum_{0\le i,j<p}r_{ij}(S^p,T^p)S^iT^j=0,\qquad r_{ij}\in\mathbb F_p(S^p,T^p).
\]
Multiply by a common nonzero polynomial denominator in $S^p,T^p$. We obtain
an identity of polynomials in $S,T$. Terms from the $(i,j)$ summand have
exponents congruent to $(i,j)$ modulo $p$. Different pairs cannot contribute
to the same monomial, so every coefficient polynomial is zero. All $r_{ij}$
are zero. This proves independence and $[L:K]=p^2$.

Finally every $\gamma=f/g\in L$ has $\gamma^p=f^p/g^p\in K$. Its minimal
polynomial over $K$ therefore divides $X^p-\gamma^p$, and
\[
[K(\gamma):K]\le p<p^2=[L:K].
\]
No $\gamma$ can generate $L$. The characteristic-zero hypothesis in the theorem
cannot simply be dropped. This example does not say that all positive-
characteristic extensions lack a primitive element; the finite fields of the
next chapter will provide a different behaviour.
\end{solution}
