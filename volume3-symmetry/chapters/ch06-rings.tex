\chapter{Rings and Ideals}

\begin{center}\itshape
In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, and none of these four factors can be split into two non-units.\\
Unique factorisation, which we took for granted in $\Z$, simply fails here. What property of $\Z$ was doing the work?
\end{center}

In the integers, $6=2\cdot3$ is a prime factorisation, and every other prime factorisation agrees with it up to signs and order. But in the larger ring
\[
\Z[\sqrt{-5}]=\{a+b\sqrt{-5}:a,b\in\Z\}
\]
we also have
\[
6=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).
\]
These two expressions are not the same factorisation in disguise. We will prove that their four factors are irreducible, yet the two lists do not agree up to units and order.

That failure is a reason to study \emph{rings}. A ring has addition, subtraction and multiplication, but division may be impossible. Polynomials form rings. So do residue classes modulo $n$, the Gaussian integers, and many sets of algebraic numbers. Their common rules give us tools that work in all these examples.

An ideal is to a ring what a normal subgroup is to a group: the right kind of subset to collapse when building a quotient. There is an extra demand. Multiplying an element of an ideal by \emph{any} element of the ring must keep it inside the ideal. That makes multiplication well defined in the quotient.

Some ideals behave like prime numbers; others give a field when we divide by them. Those two properties become the tests for prime and maximal ideals. Finally, Gauss's lemma tells us when familiar polynomial factorisation over a field can be brought back to the original ring.

The failed factorisation of $6$ will return in arithmetic. Here it teaches us what algebra can still say when ordinary prime factorisation is no longer guaranteed.

\section{Rings}

\begin{definition}
A (commutative) \emph{ring} is a set $R$ with two operations $+$ and $\times$ such that $(R,+)$ is an abelian group with neutral element $0$, multiplication is associative and commutative with a neutral element $1$, and $a(b+c)=ab+ac$. A \emph{ring homomorphism} $f:R\to S$ satisfies $f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)$, $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$ and $f(1)=1$. The \emph{units} of $R$ are the elements with a multiplicative inverse; they form a group $R^*$.

$R$ is an \emph{integral domain} (or \emph{domain}) if $1\ne0$ and $ab=0$ implies $a=0$ or $b=0$. It is a \emph{field} if $1\ne0$ and $R^*=R\setminus\{0\}$.
\end{definition}

We allow the \emph{zero ring}, whose single element satisfies $0=1$.
It is neither a domain nor a field. If $0=1$ in any ring, then
$a=a1=a0=0$ for every $a$, so there is only one element. Here
$a0=0$ follows from $a(0+0)=a0+a0$ by additive cancellation;
the same laws give $a(-b)=-(ab)$. Products and inverses of units
are units, by $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$, so $R^*$ is indeed a group.

All rings in this chapter are commutative. Matrices and the quaternions of Chapter~12 also have associative multiplication, but it is generally non-commutative. Our ideal and factorisation theorems here use commutativity; their hypotheses do not extend automatically to those examples.

\begin{example}\label{ex:rings}
$\Z$, $\Q$, $\R$, $\C$; $\Z/n\Z$ for $n\ge1$, a field iff $n$ is prime (Volume~2); $K[X]$ for a field $K$, and $R[X]$ for any ring $R$; $\Z[i]$, $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]=\{a+b\sqrt{-5}:a,b\in\Z\}$; the product $R\times S$ with componentwise operations, which is not a domain when both factors are nonzero rings, since $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are then nonzero with product zero. If one factor is the zero ring, the product is isomorphic to the other factor. Another example is the set of all functions $\R\to\R$, with pointwise addition and multiplication: every ring law holds at each input separately. A field is a domain: if $ab=0$ and $a\ne0$, then $b=a^{-1}ab=0$. A domain may be far from a field: $\Z$, $K[X]$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:finitedomain}
A finite integral domain is a field.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $R$ be a finite integral domain, and
take any non-zero element $a\in R$.
We must show that $a$ has a multiplicative inverse.

Consider multiplication by $a$:
\[
L_a:R\longrightarrow R,\qquad x\longmapsto ax.
\]
Suppose $L_a(x)=L_a(y)$. Then
$a(x-y)=0$. An integral domain has no
zero divisors, and $a\ne0$, so $x-y=0$
and hence $x=y$. Thus $L_a$ is injective.

Here finiteness does the rest. An injective
map from a finite set to itself must be
surjective. In particular, $1\in R$
is in its image, so there exists $b\in R$
with
\[
ab=1.
\]
Since $R$ is commutative, also $ba=1$.
Therefore every non-zero element has an
inverse, which is exactly the definition
of a field.

The hypothesis that $R$ is an integral
domain is essential: the finite ring
$\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ has zero divisors
($2\cdot3=0$), and $2$ has no inverse.
\end{proof}

In a domain one may cancel: $ab=ac$ with $a\ne0$ gives $b=c$. If $R$ is a domain, so is $R[X]$: the leading coefficient of $fg$ is the product of the leading coefficients, so $\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g$ for $f,g\ne0$. In particular $R[X]^*=R^*$.

Over any field $K$, polynomial division works just as it does over
$\Q$. To divide $P$ by $0\ne Q$, start with quotient $0$ and
remainder $P$. If the current nonzero remainder has leading term
$aX^r$ and $Q$ has leading term $bX^s$ with $r\ge s$, subtract
$(a/b)X^{r-s}Q$ from the remainder and add $(a/b)X^{r-s}$ to
the quotient. The division by $b$ is valid in $K$, and the leading
term cancels, so the remainder's degree strictly decreases. Nonnegative
integer degrees cannot decrease indefinitely. We obtain
$P=QU+R$, where $R=0$ or $\deg R<\deg Q$; this also covers
$P=0$ and constant nonzero $Q$. The answer is unique: two such
expressions give $Q(U-U')=R'-R$. If $U-U'\ne0$, the left
side has degree at least $\deg Q$, whereas the right side is zero
or has smaller degree, a contradiction. Thus $U=U'$ and $R=R'$.
The field hypothesis matters: in $\Z[X]$, the leading term of $X$
cannot be cancelled by an integer multiple of $2X$.

A ring homomorphism may send several elements to the same output. As with groups, the kernel measures what the map forgets. But multiplication puts a new demand on the kernel: it must absorb products by arbitrary ring elements.

\section{Ideals and quotient rings}

\begin{definition}
An \emph{ideal} of $R$ is an additive subgroup $I$ such that $ra\in I$ for all $r\in R$, $a\in I$. The ideal \emph{generated} by $a_1,\dots,a_n$ is $(a_1,\dots,a_n)=\{r_1a_1+\dots+r_na_n:r_i\in R\}$; an ideal $(a)=aR$ is \emph{principal}.
\end{definition}

More generally, $(S)$ for any subset $S\subseteq R$ consists of all
\emph{finite} sums $r_1s_1+\cdots+r_ks_k$, with $s_j\in S$ and
$r_j\in R$. The empty sum is $0$, so $(\varnothing)=(0)$.
Adding two such sums, taking negatives and multiplying by an element of
$R$ leave this set unchanged. It is therefore an ideal; it contains $S$
and lies in every ideal containing $S$. A finite list of generators is
a special case, not an assumption that every ideal has such a list.

The kernel of a ring homomorphism $f$ is an ideal: if $f(a)=0$ then $f(ra)=f(r)f(a)=0$. An ideal containing a unit $u$ contains $u^{-1}u=1$ and then everything: $I=R$. So a field $K$ has only the ideals $\{0\}$ and $K$, and conversely a ring $R$ with $1\ne0$ whose only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $R$ is a field, since for $a\ne0$ the ideal $(a)$ must be $R$, so $ab=1$ for some $b$.

\begin{proposition}[quotient rings]\label{prop:quotring}
Let $I$ be any ideal of $R$, including $I=R$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The quotient group $R/I$ is a ring with $(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I$, and $\pi:a\mapsto a+I$ is a ring homomorphism with kernel $I$.
\item (First isomorphism theorem) If $f:R\to S$ is a ring homomorphism, $R/\ker f\cong f(R)$.
\item (Correspondence) $J\mapsto J/I$ is a bijection from the ideals of $R$ containing $I$ to the ideals of $R/I$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(a) The quotient multiplication.}
The additive group $(R,+)$ is abelian,
so every additive subgroup, including
an ideal $I$, is normal. Its cosets
form the additive quotient group $R/I$.

We must check that
\[
(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I
\]
does not depend on the representatives.
If $a'=a+i$ and $b'=b+j$ with $i,j\in I$,
then
\[
a'b'-ab=aj+ib+ij\in I,
\]
because an ideal is closed under sums and
multiplication by arbitrary ring elements.
Therefore $a'b'+I=ab+I$.

Associativity, commutativity, distributivity,
and the class $1+I$ as the multiplicative
identity follow by taking representatives
and applying the corresponding laws in $R$.
The projection $\pi(a)=a+I$ preserves both
operations and $1$. Its kernel is
\[
\ker\pi=\{a:a+I=I\}=I.
\]

\emph{(b) The first isomorphism theorem.}
Let $f:R\to S$ be a ring homomorphism
and put $J=\ker f$. From (a) the
quotient $R/J$ is a ring. Define
\[
\bar f:R/J\to f(R),\qquad
a+J\longmapsto f(a).
\]
It is well defined because
$a+J=b+J$ means $a-b\in J$,
hence $f(a)=f(b)$. It respects sums,
products and the identity because $f$
does. It is surjective by definition
of $f(R)$, and injective because
$\bar f(a+J)=0$ implies $a\in J$.
Thus $\bar f$ is a ring isomorphism. If $\ker f=R$, then $f(R)$
and $R/\ker f$ are both the zero ring; this is why part (a) includes
the unit ideal.

\emph{(c) Ideals above and below.}
Let $J$ be an ideal of $R$ containing $I$.
Its image $J/I$ under $\pi$ is closed
under addition and negatives. For
$r+I\in R/I$ and $j+I\in J/I$,
\[
(r+I)(j+I)=rj+I\in J/I,
\]
so $J/I$ is an ideal.

Conversely, if $L$ is an ideal of $R/I$,
its inverse image $\pi^{-1}(L)$
is an ideal of $R$ containing $I$:
closure under addition and absorption
follow by applying $\pi$.
Because $\pi$ is onto, taking the image
and then the inverse image (or the reverse)
returns the original ideal. The two
constructions are inverse bijections.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:quotients}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item The ideals of $\Z$ are the $n\Z$ (they are subgroups), and $\Z/n\Z$ is the ring of Volume~2.
\item Evaluation at $a$, $K[X]\to K$, $P\mapsto P(a)$, is onto with kernel $(X-a)$ (a polynomial vanishes at $a$ iff $X-a$ divides it): $K[X]/(X-a)\cong K$.
\item Evaluation at $i$, $\R[X]\to\C$, is onto ($a+bX\mapsto a+bi$). Divide a real polynomial by $X^2+1$; its remainder is $a+bX$. If the polynomial vanishes at $i$, then $a+bi=0$ forces $a=b=0$. Thus its kernel is exactly $(X^2+1)$ and $\R[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\C$. The same division works over $\Z$ because the divisor is monic: no coefficient division is needed. Remainders $a+bX$ vanish at $i$ or $\sqrt{-5}$ only when $a=b=0$, giving $\Z[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\Z[i]$ and $\Z[X]/(X^2+5)\cong\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

So $\C$ can be \emph{defined} as $\R[X]/(X^2+1)$: adjoin a symbol $X$ and decree $X^2+1=0$. Chapter~8 will use this construction to adjoin roots of irreducible polynomials.

The quotient by an ideal is always a ring. Sometimes it has no zero divisors; sometimes every non-zero element has an inverse. Those two outcomes tell us exactly which ideals deserve the names prime and maximal.

\section{Prime and maximal ideals}

\begin{definition}
An ideal $P\ne R$ is \emph{prime} if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. An ideal $M\ne R$ is \emph{maximal} if the only ideals containing $M$ are $M$ and $R$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:primemax}
Let $I\ne R$ be an ideal.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $I$ is prime iff $R/I$ is an integral domain.
\item $I$ is maximal iff $R/I$ is a field.
\item Every maximal ideal is prime.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In each statement, translate an ideal
condition into a property of the quotient.

\emph{(a) Prime ideals.}
For $a,b\in R$,
\[
(a+I)(b+I)=0+I
\iff ab\in I.
\]
Also $a+I=0+I$ iff $a\in I$.
Therefore the condition
\[
ab\in I\ \Longrightarrow\ a\in I
\text{ or }b\in I
\]
is exactly the condition that the quotient
has no zero divisors. Because $I\ne R$,
the quotient satisfies $1+I\ne0+I$.
It is therefore an integral domain
precisely when $I$ is prime.

\emph{(b) Maximal ideals.}
First observe that a commutative ring
$T$ with $1\ne0$ is a field exactly
when its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $T$.
If $T$ is a field and $J\ne\{0\}$
is an ideal, take $0\ne x\in J$.
Multiplying $x$ by its inverse gives
$1\in J$, so $J=T$.

Conversely, suppose $T$ has no
non-zero proper ideals. For
$0\ne x\in T$, the principal ideal
$(x)$ is non-zero and hence equals $T$.
Thus $1=xy$ for some $y\in T$,
giving an inverse of $x$.
So $T$ is a field.

Apply this observation to $T=R/I$.
By the ideal correspondence
(Proposition~\ref{prop:quotring}(c)),
ideals of $R/I$ correspond precisely
to ideals of $R$ containing $I$.
The zero ideal corresponds to $I$,
and the whole quotient to $R$.
Hence $R/I$ is a field exactly when
there is no ideal strictly between
$I$ and $R$, which is maximality.

\emph{(c) Maximal implies prime.}
Every field is an integral domain.
So if $I$ is maximal, part (b) makes
$R/I$ a field, and part (a) makes
$I$ prime.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
There are two different questions about a quotient ring. Can two non-zero classes multiply to zero? That happens precisely when $ab\in I$ but neither $a$ nor $b$ is in $I$, which is what a prime ideal forbids. Can we divide by every non-zero class? That happens precisely when the quotient is a field, or equivalently when it has no non-trivial ideals. For a concrete contrast, $(6)$ is not prime in $\Z$, since $2\cdot3\in(6)$ but neither factor lies in $(6)$. The ideal $(5)$ is maximal: $\Z/(5)$ is a field. In $\Z$, a non-zero prime ideal is maximal; in $\Z[X]$ this need not hold.

In $\Z$, the prime ideals are $\{0\}$ and the $p\Z$, and the non-zero ones are maximal. In $\Z[X]$ the picture is richer:

\begin{think}
Is $(X)$ a maximal ideal of $\Z[X]$? Is $(2,X)$ principal? Compute $\Z[X]/(X)$ and $\Z[X]/(2,X)$ before reading on.
\end{think}

$\Z[X]/(X)\cong\Z$ (evaluate at $0$) is a domain but not a field: $(X)$ is prime, not maximal. $\Z[X]/(2,X)\cong\Z/2\Z$ (evaluate at $0$, then reduce mod $2$): $(2,X)$ is maximal. And $(2,X)$ is not principal (Exercise~\ref{ex:zxnotpid}).

\begin{theorem}[Chinese remainder theorem for rings]\label{thm:crtring}
Write $I+J=\{i+j:i\in I,j\in J\}$, an ideal by addition and absorption.
Let $I,J$ be ideals with $I+J=R$ (they are \emph{coprime}). Then $I\cap J=IJ$, where $IJ$ is the ideal generated by the products $ab$, $a\in I$, $b\in J$, and
\[
\begin{gathered}R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J,\qq x+I\cap J\mapsto(x+I,\,x+J).\end{gathered}
\]
If $n\ge1$ and $I_1,\dots,I_n$ are pairwise coprime, $R/(I_1\cap\dots\cap I_n)\cong\prod_kR/I_k$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The condition $I+J=R$ means that we can write
\[
1=a+b,\qquad a\in I,\quad b\in J.
\]
This equation is the tool for both parts of the theorem: it turns an intersection into a product and lets us prescribe residues independently.

\emph{First, identify the intersection.}
Every product $uv$ with $u\in I$, $v\in J$ belongs to both ideals, and so does every finite sum of such products. Therefore $IJ\subseteq I\cap J$.

For the reverse inclusion take $x\in I\cap J$. Multiply $1=a+b$ by $x$:
\[
x=xa+xb.
\]
The term $xa$ belongs to $JI$ because $x\in J$ and $a\in I$. The term $xb$ belongs to $IJ$ because $x\in I$ and $b\in J$. Since our ring is commutative, $JI=IJ$, and hence $x\in IJ$. We have proved $I\cap J=IJ$.

\emph{Next, construct the isomorphism.}
Consider
\[
\varphi:R\to R/I\times R/J,
\qquad x\mapsto(x+I,x+J).
\]
It is a ring homomorphism. Its kernel is exactly $I\cap J$, since both residue classes vanish precisely when $x$ lies in both ideals.

We also need surjectivity. Notice that $a\equiv0\pmod I$ and $a\equiv1\pmod J$, whereas $b\equiv1\pmod I$ and $b\equiv0\pmod J$. Given arbitrary residues $u+I$ and $v+J$, the element
\[
x=ub+va
\]
has $x+I=u+I$ and $x+J=v+J$. Thus $\varphi$ is onto. The first isomorphism theorem for rings now gives
\[
\boxed{R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J.}
\]

\emph{Finally, more than two ideals.}
For $n=1$, the asserted map is the identity. For $n\ge2$, suppose
$I_1,\ldots,I_n$ are pairwise coprime. By induction it is enough to show that $I_1$ is coprime to the intersection $K=I_2\cap\cdots\cap I_n$. For each $j\ge2$ choose
\[
1=a_j+b_j,\qquad a_j\in I_1,\quad b_j\in I_j.
\]
Multiply all these equations:
\[
1=\prod_{j=2}^n(a_j+b_j).
\]
Expand the product. Every term involving any $a_j$ lies in $I_1$. The only term with no $a_j$ is $b_2\cdots b_n$, which belongs to every $I_j$ for $j\ge2$, and hence to $K$. Thus $1\in I_1+K$.

The two-ideal result applies to $I_1$ and $K$, and induction applies to $I_2,\ldots,I_n$. Combining the resulting isomorphisms gives
\[
\boxed{R/(I_1\cap\cdots\cap I_n)\cong\prod_{j=1}^nR/I_j.}
\]
The proof also shows why pairwise coprimality is the right assumption: it is exactly what lets us choose each residue independently.
\end{proof}

For $R=\Z$ this is the theorem of Volume~2. For $R=K[X]$ and $I_k=(X-x_k)$ with distinct $x_k$, it says that a polynomial is determined modulo $\prod(X-x_k)$ by its values at the $x_k$, and that any values can be prescribed: this is Lagrange interpolation (Volume~1, Chapter~6), and the element $b$ of the proof is a Lagrange basis polynomial.

When a quotient ring is a field, every nonzero class can be divided by. Inside a domain, however, we may not be able to divide, and factorisation can behave very differently. We need to separate the properties that integers happen to enjoy from those that hold in every domain.

\section{Factorisation in domains}

From now on $R$ is an integral domain. We write $a\mid b$ if $b=ac$ for some $c\in R$; this means $(b)\subseteq(a)$. Elements $a,b$ are \emph{associates} if $a=ub$ with $u\in R^*$, equivalently $(a)=(b)$.

Indeed, multiplying by a unit does not change a principal ideal.
Conversely, if $(a)=(b)$ and $a\ne0$, write $a=bc$, $b=ad$.
Then $a=adc$ and cancellation gives $dc=1$, so $c$ is a unit.
If $a=0$, equality of the ideals also forces $b=0$, and the unit $1$
relates them.

\begin{definition}
A non-zero non-unit $p$ is \emph{irreducible} if $p=ab$ implies that $a$ or $b$ is a unit. It is \emph{prime} if $p\mid ab$ implies $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$, i.e.\ if $(p)$ is a prime ideal. $R$ is a \emph{unique factorisation domain} (UFD) if every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and associates.
\end{definition}

A prime $p$ is irreducible: if $p=ab$, then $p\mid a$ say, $a=pc$, so $p=pcb$ and $cb=1$: $b$ is a unit. In $\Z$, ``prime'' and ``irreducible'' coincide, which is Euclid's lemma. The opening example shows how they can differ:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:zm5}
In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, with the norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2=|a+b\sqrt{-5}|^2$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $N(xy)=N(x)N(y)$, the units are $\pm1$, and no element has norm $2$ or $3$;
\item $2$, $3$, $1+\sqrt{-5}$ and $1-\sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible, and no two of them are associates;
\item $2$ is irreducible but not prime. So $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $R=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$.
Its elements are exactly $a+b\sqrt{-5}$
with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$.

\emph{(a) Norm and units.}
Complex conjugation sends
$a+b\sqrt{-5}$ to $a-b\sqrt{-5}$.
Their product is
\[
N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2.
\]
Because complex modulus is multiplicative,
\[
N(xy)=|xy|^2=|x|^2|y|^2=N(x)N(y).
\]
If $x$ is a unit, say $xy=1$, the
positive integers $N(x),N(y)$
multiply to $1$, so $N(x)=1$.
The equation $a^2+5b^2=1$
forces $b=0$ and $a=\pm1$.
Conversely, $\pm1$ are units.
Thus $R^*=\{\pm1\}$.

If $N(x)=2$ or $3$, then $5b^2$
must vanish, so $b=0$, but no
integer square is $2$ or $3$.
Hence these norms never occur.

\emph{(b) Four irreducibles.}
The elements
\[
2,\quad3,\quad1+\sqrt{-5},\quad
1-\sqrt{-5}
\]
have norms $4,9,6,6$ respectively.
Suppose one factors as $xy$ with
both factors non-units. Their norms
would be integers greater than $1$
whose product is $4$, $9$, or $6$.
Such a factorisation requires one of
the norms to be $2$ or $3$,
which part (a) ruled out.
So all four elements are irreducible.

Two associates differ by multiplication
by a unit $\pm1$, so have the same norm.
The elements $2$ and $3$ cannot be
associates of one another or of either
element of norm $6$. The two elements
$1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ are not negatives or
equals of one another, so they are
not associates either.

\emph{(c) An irreducible that is not prime.}
We have two decompositions of the same element:
\[
6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).
\]
Thus $2$ divides the product of the two
factors on the right. But it divides
neither one in $R$, because
\[
\frac{1\pm\sqrt{-5}}2
=\frac12\pm\frac12\sqrt{-5}
\notin R.
\]
So $2$ is not prime, despite being
irreducible. The two displayed
factorisations also consist of
non-associate irreducibles, showing
that $R$ is not a UFD.
\end{proof}

The ideal $I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$ records a relation that elements alone
miss. Put $w=\sqrt{-5}$. By multiplying generators,
\[
I^2=(4,2+2w,-4+2w)\subseteq(2).
\]
Conversely, $(2+2w)-(-4+2w)-4=2$ belongs to $I^2$, so
$I^2=(2)$. Exercise~\ref{ex:zsqrtm5} will show that $I$ itself has
no single generator. An ideal can therefore act like a factor of $(2)$
without being generated by a factor of the element $2$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:primeufd}
In a domain, if $a=p_1\cdots p_r=q_1\cdots q_s$ with the $p_i$ prime and the $q_j$ irreducible, then $r=s$ and, after reordering, $q_i$ is an associate of $p_i$. Hence a domain in which every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles is a UFD iff every irreducible is prime.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First compare two factorisations
\[
p_1\cdots p_r=q_1\cdots q_s
\]
in an integral domain, where each $p_i$
is prime and each $q_j$ is irreducible.

We argue by induction on $r$.
If $r=0$, the left side is the unit $1$.
A product of non-units cannot be a unit
(in a commutative ring with identity),
so $s=0$ as well.

If $r>0$, then $s>0$, and the prime
$p_1$ divides $q_1\cdots q_s$.
The defining property of a prime element,
applied repeatedly, says that $p_1$
divides one factor; relabel to make it
$q_1$. Thus $q_1=p_1u$ for some $u$.
Since $q_1$ is irreducible and $p_1$
is not a unit, $u$ must be a unit.
Cancel the non-zero element $p_1$:
\[
p_2\cdots p_r=u\,q_2\cdots q_s.
\]
The factor $uq_2$ is irreducible when
$s>1$; when $s=1$, the right side is
the unit $u$. In either case induction,
after absorbing the unit into a factor
or comparing the remaining units,
shows $r-1=s-1$ and matches the
remaining factors up to associates.
Together with $q_1=up_1$, this gives
$r=s$ and uniqueness up to reordering
and associates.

Now assume that every non-zero non-unit
of a domain $R$ has an irreducible
factorisation. If all irreducibles are
prime, the argument above makes every
factorisation unique, so $R$ is a UFD.

Conversely suppose $R$ is a UFD and
let the irreducible $p$ divide $ab$.
If $a=0$ or $b=0$, the conclusion
is immediate. Otherwise factor $a$,
$b$ and $ab$ into irreducibles, omitting
unit factors. Write $ab=pc$. Since $a,b$ are nonzero in a domain, $c$ is nonzero too.
Factor $c$ into irreducibles, taking an empty product if it is a unit.
Thus one irreducible factorisation of $ab$ contains $p$.
Uniqueness means that an associate of
$p$ occurs among the factors of $a$
or of $b$. Thus $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$.
Every irreducible is prime.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
The domain $R$ is a \emph{principal ideal domain} (PID) if every ideal is principal. $R$ is \emph{Euclidean} if there is a map $N:R\setminus\{0\}\to\N$ such that for all $a$ and all $b\ne0$ there are $q,r$ with $a=bq+r$ and $r=0$ or $N(r)<N(b)$.
\end{definition}

$\Z$ ($N=|\cdot|$), $K[X]$ ($N=\deg$), $\Z[i]$ and $\Z[\omega]$ ($N=|\cdot|^2$, Volume~2, Chapter~10) are Euclidean.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:edpidufd}
Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We prove the two implications separately.
The first uses division with remainder;
the second uses the structure of ideals.

\emph{Euclidean domain implies PID.}
Let $R$ be Euclidean with Euclidean
function $N$ and take an ideal $I$.
If $I=\{0\}$ it is generated by $0$.
Otherwise choose $0\ne b\in I$
whose value $N(b)$ is minimal among
non-zero elements of $I$.

For any $a\in I$, Euclidean division
produces $a=bq+r$ with $r=0$ or
$N(r)<N(b)$. But $r=a-bq\in I$,
so the minimality of $N(b)$ rules
out a non-zero remainder.
Thus $a\in(b)$ for every $a\in I$,
while $b\in I$ implies $(b)\subseteq I$.
Hence $I=(b)$, and every ideal is principal.

\emph{PID implies UFD: first, primality.}
Let $R$ be a PID and let $p$ be irreducible.
Suppose an ideal $(d)$ contains $(p)$.
Then $p=dc$ for some $c\in R$.
Irreducibility says $d$ or $c$ is a
unit. In the first case $(d)=R$;
in the second $(d)=(p)$.
Thus the ideal $(p)$ is maximal.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:primemax},
it is prime, meaning that $p$
is a prime element.

\emph{Next, existence of factorisations.}
Suppose some non-zero non-unit of $R$
cannot be written as a product of
irreducibles. Call it $a_0$.
It is not irreducible itself, so
$a_0=a_1b_1$ with both factors non-units.
At least one, say $a_1$, still has no
irreducible factorisation; otherwise
their product would factor $a_0$.
Continue in this way to obtain
\[
(a_0)\subsetneq(a_1)
\subsetneq(a_2)\subsetneq\cdots.
\]
Each inclusion is proper: if
$(a_i)=(a_{i+1})$, then the equation
$a_i=a_{i+1}b_{i+1}$ in a domain
would make $b_{i+1}$ a unit.

Let $J=\bigcup_{i\ge0}(a_i)$.
Because the ideals form an increasing
chain, their union is an ideal.
As $R$ is a PID, $J=(d)$ for some $d$.
The generator $d$ belongs to the union,
so $d\in(a_M)$ for some $M$.
Then
\[
(a_M)\subsetneq(a_{M+1})
\subseteq J=(d)\subseteq(a_M),
\]
a contradiction.

Every non-zero non-unit therefore factors
into irreducibles. We already proved
that every irreducible is prime, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:primeufd} supplies
uniqueness. Hence $R$ is a UFD.
\end{proof}

In a PID, $\gcd$s exist and Bézout holds: $(a,b)=(d)$ means that $d$ divides $a$ and $b$ and $d=ua+vb$. Every common divisor of $a,b$ therefore divides $d$. This includes $a=b=0$, with $d=0$.

Does having one generator for every ideal give a division algorithm?
The next example separates the two properties. Its proof uses the same
least-norm idea as the Euclidean argument, but permits a multiple of the
numerator before taking a remainder.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:pid-not-euclidean}
The ring $S=\Z[\alpha]$, where
$\alpha=(1+\sqrt{-19})/2$, is a PID but is not Euclidean for any function.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\alpha^2=\alpha-5$, the elements of $S$ are uniquely
$a+b\alpha$, with $a,b\in\Z$, and these expressions are closed under
addition, subtraction and multiplication. As a subring of $\C$, $S$ is
a domain. Its norm is
\[
N(a+b\alpha)=|a+b\alpha|^2
=a^2+ab+5b^2
=\left(a+\frac b2\right)^2+\frac{19b^2}{4}.
\]
It is a positive integer on nonzero elements, and $N(xy)=N(x)N(y)$.
If $x$ is a unit, then $N(x)=1$. The displayed formula forces
$b=0$, $a=\pm1$, so the units are exactly $\pm1$.

We first compute a small quotient that will repair a rounding exception.
Modulo $2S$, each element has one of the four distinct representatives
$0,1,\alpha,1+\alpha$. The relation becomes
$\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, and
$\alpha(1+\alpha)=1$. Thus all three nonzero classes are invertible.

\emph{A small nonzero remainder after multiplication.}
We claim that for every $z\in\C\setminus S$ there are $c,d\in S$ with
\[
0<|cz-d|^2<1.
\]
Subtract an integer multiple of $\alpha$ to bring the imaginary
coordinate between $-\sqrt{19}/4$ and $\sqrt{19}/4$, then subtract
an integer to bring the real coordinate between $-1/2$ and $1/2$.
In this way $z-q=u+i\sqrt{19}\,v/2$ for some $q\in S$, where
$|u|,|v|\le1/2$. If $|v|\le1/3$, then
\[
0<|z-q|^2\le\frac14+\frac{19}{36}=\frac79<1,
\]
so $c=1,d=q$ work. The strict lower bound follows from $z\notin S$.

If $|v|>1/3$, double $z-q$. When $v>0$, subtract $\alpha$;
when $v<0$, add $\alpha$. The new imaginary coefficient is respectively
$2v-1$ or $2v+1$, of absolute value less than $1/3$.
Subtract an integer to make the real part have absolute value at most
$1/2$. This gives $d\in S$ with $|2z-d|^2\le7/9$.
If this difference is nonzero, take $c=2$.

There remains the case $2z=A\in S$. Here $A\notin2S$, since
$z\notin S$. The nonzero class of $A$ has an inverse in the
four-element quotient just computed. Choose $c,d\in S$ with
$cA=1+2d$. Then $cz-d=1/2$, whose squared absolute value is $1/4$.
This proves the claim in every case, including the half-lattice points.

\emph{Every ideal is principal.}
For a nonzero ideal $I$, choose $0\ne b\in I$ of least norm.
For $a\in I$, suppose $z=a/b\notin S$, where this division takes
place in $\C$. The claim gives $c,d\in S$ such that
$r=ca-db\in I$ and
\[
0<N(r)=N(b)|cz-d|^2<N(b),
\]
contradicting minimality. Therefore $a/b\in S$ for every $a\in I$,
so $I=(b)$. The zero ideal is $(0)$. Hence $S$ is a PID.

\emph{No Euclidean function can exist.}
Suppose $\delta:S\setminus\{0\}\to\N$ were a Euclidean function.
There are nonzero non-units, for example $2$. Choose one, $b$, whose
$\delta$-value is least among them. Division of any $x\in S$ by $b$
leaves remainder $0$ or a nonzero remainder with smaller $\delta$-value.
In the latter case minimality forces the remainder to be a unit,
hence $1$ or $-1$. Thus $S/(b)$ has at most three elements.
Since $b$ is not a unit, this quotient is nonzero, so it has two or
three elements.

A unital ring of prime cardinality $p$ is $\Z/p\Z$: its nonzero
element $1$ generates the additive group by Lagrange's theorem, and
distributivity then fixes multiplication. The image of $\alpha$
would therefore solve $X^2-X+5=0$ modulo $2$ or modulo $3$.
But its values modulo $2$ are $1,1$, and its values modulo $3$ are
$2,2,1$. Neither polynomial has a root. This contradiction rules out
every Euclidean function, not merely the norm used above.
\end{proof}

So PID need not imply Euclidean. The other converse also fails:
$\Z[X]$ is a UFD (proved next in Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussufd}) but is
not a PID (Exercise~\ref{ex:zxnotpid}).

The integers have unique factorisation even though division is not always possible. We now ask whether that property survives when we adjoin a variable. The key is to distinguish the factors that come from coefficients from those carried by the polynomial itself.

\section{Polynomials over a UFD}

For a domain $R$, division can be supplied by a construction rather
than assumed. Take pairs $(a,b)$ with $b\ne0$, and declare
$(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ when $ad=bc$. Reflexivity and symmetry follow
from equality. For transitivity, $ad=bc$ and $cf=de$ imply
$adf=bde$; cancel the nonzero $d$ to obtain $af=be$.
Write $a/b$ for the equivalence class, and define
\[
\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad
\frac ab\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}.
\]
The denominators are nonzero because $R$ is a domain.
If $ab'=a'b$ and $cd'=c'd$, then
$(ad+bc)b'd'=(a'd'+b'c')bd$ and
$acb'd'=a'c'bd$, by expanding and using those two equalities.
Thus both operations are independent of representatives.

Addition of three fractions has numerator $adf+bcf+bde$ over
$ bdf$; multiplication has numerator $ace$ over $bdf$.
These expressions prove associativity, and the same expansions give
distributivity and commutativity. The zero and identity are $0/1$ and
$1/1$, with additive inverse $-a/b$. The class $a/b$ is zero exactly
when $a=0$; otherwise $b/a$ is its multiplicative inverse.
Hence these classes form a field, the \emph{field of fractions}
$K=\operatorname{Frac}R$. The map $a\mapsto a/1$ is an injective
ring homomorphism, so we regard $R$ as a subring of $K$.
For example $\operatorname{Frac}\mathbb Z=\mathbb Q$;
$\operatorname{Frac}\mathbb Z[i]=\mathbb Q(i)=\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb Q\}$,
since a Gaussian denominator can be multiplied by its conjugate.
For a field $K$, the fractions of $K[X]$ form $K(X)$, the rational functions.

In a UFD, a gcd of finitely many nonzero elements is obtained by taking
the minimum exponent of each irreducible in their factorizations; zero
entries may be omitted. This also proves that multiplying every entry
by $a\ne0$ multiplies the gcd by $a$, up to a unit.

Let $R$ be a UFD. The \emph{content} $c(f)$ of a non-zero $f\in R[X]$ is a gcd of its coefficients (defined up to a unit), and $f$ is \emph{primitive} if $c(f)$ is a unit. Every non-zero $f\in K[X]$ can be written $f=\lambda f_0$ with $\lambda\in K^*$ and $f_0\in R[X]$ primitive: multiply by a common denominator, then divide by the content. This writing is unique up to a unit of $R$: if $\lambda f_0=\mu g_0$, write $\mu/\lambda=a/b$ in lowest terms (possible in a UFD); then $bf_0=ag_0$, comparing contents $b\sim a$ up to units, so $a/b\in R^*$.

\begin{lemma}[Gauss]\label{lem:gauss}
Let $R$ be a UFD and let $f,g\in R[X]$ be nonzero. If $f,g$ are primitive, so is $fg$; in general $c(fg)=c(f)c(g)$ up to units.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First suppose that $f,g\in R[X]$ are primitive. We want to show that $fg$ is primitive as well.

Assume the contrary. Then the coefficients of $fg$ have a common non-unit divisor. Since $R$ is a UFD, that divisor has an irreducible factor $p$. Thus $p$ divides \emph{every} coefficient of $fg$. Reduce each coefficient modulo the ideal $(p)$. In $(R/(p))[X]$ this says
\[
\bar f\,\bar g=\overline{fg}=0.
\]
Neither $\bar f$ nor $\bar g$ is zero: otherwise $p$ would divide every coefficient of $f$ or of $g$, contradicting primitivity.

In a UFD every irreducible element is prime (Lemma~\ref{lem:primeufd}). Hence $(p)$ is a prime ideal, $R/(p)$ is an integral domain, and so is its polynomial ring. Two non-zero polynomials over a domain cannot have zero product. This contradiction proves that $fg$ is primitive.

For arbitrary non-zero polynomials write $f=c(f)f_0$ and $g=c(g)g_0$, where $f_0$ and $g_0$ are primitive. Then
\[
fg=c(f)c(g)\,(f_0g_0).
\]
We just proved that $f_0g_0$ is primitive, so the content of $fg$ is associated to $c(f)c(g)$. This is the stated identity up to multiplication by a unit of $R$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
The word \emph{primitive} means that no non-unit of the coefficient ring divides every coefficient. To test whether a product has gained a common prime factor $p$, reduce the coefficients modulo $p$. If $p$ divides every coefficient of $fg$, the product becomes the zero polynomial. But the reductions of primitive $f$ and $g$ are both non-zero, and a polynomial ring over a domain has no zero divisors. This contradiction explains the lemma. The UFD hypothesis matters: it lets us choose an irreducible $p$ dividing a non-unit common factor, and it makes that $p$ prime.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gaussirr}
Let $R$ be a UFD with fraction field $K$, and $f\in R[X]$ primitive of degree $\ge1$. Then $f$ is irreducible in $R[X]$ iff it is irreducible in $K[X]$. If $f$ divides $g\in R[X]$ in $K[X]$, it divides $g$ in $R[X]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A polynomial is \emph{primitive} if the
gcd of its coefficients is a unit.
Gauss's lemma says that a product of
primitive polynomials remains primitive.
We use it to move factorisations between
$R[X]$ and its fraction-field ring $K[X]$.

\emph{Irreducibility over the two rings.}
Suppose first that the primitive
$f\in R[X]$ factors in $K[X]$ as
\[
f=GH,\qquad \deg G,\deg H\ge1.
\]
Clear denominators and divide out
contents to write $G=\lambda g$ and
$H=\mu h$, where $g,h\in R[X]$
are primitive and $\lambda,\mu\in K^*$.
Thus
\[
f=(\lambda\mu)gh.
\]
Both $f$ and $gh$ are primitive.
Uniqueness of content up to a unit
forces $\lambda\mu$ to be a unit of
$R$. Therefore
$f=(\lambda\mu g)h$ is a factorisation
in $R[X]$ into two positive-degree
polynomials, and $f$ is reducible there.

Conversely, suppose $f=gh$ in
$R[X]$ with both factors non-units.
Neither factor can be a non-unit
constant: such a constant would divide
all coefficients of $f$, contradicting
primitivity. Hence $\deg g,\deg h\ge1$.
The same factorisation then proves
reducibility in $K[X]$.

\emph{Divisibility descends to $R[X]$.}
Suppose $g=fQ$ for some $Q\in K[X]$,
where $g\in R[X]$. If $g=0$, use $Q=0$ in $R[X]$ and we are done.
Otherwise $Q\ne0$. Write $Q=\lambda Q_0$ with
$Q_0\in R[X]$ primitive and
$\lambda\in K^*$. Since $fQ_0$
is primitive, the content of
$g=\lambda fQ_0$ is $\lambda$
up to multiplication by a unit of $R$.
But the content of $g$ lies in $R$.
Therefore $\lambda\in R$ and
$Q=\lambda Q_0\in R[X]$.
This proves that $f$ divides $g$
already in $R[X]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Gauss]\label{thm:gaussufd}
If $R$ is a UFD, so is $R[X]$. In particular $\Z[X]$ and $K[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are UFDs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The proof has two tasks: produce an
irreducible factorisation in $R[X]$,
then show that the irreducibles there
are prime.

\emph{Existence.}
Take a non-zero non-unit $f\in R[X]$.
Separate its content:
\[
f=c(f)f_0,
\]
where $f_0$ is primitive and
$c(f)\in R$, up to a unit.
Factor the non-unit part of $c(f)$
into irreducibles of the UFD $R$.
They remain irreducible as constant
polynomials in $R[X]$.

If $\deg f_0\ge1$, factor it in
the polynomial ring $K[X]$ over the
fraction field:
\[
f_0=F_1\cdots F_s
\]
up to a non-zero scalar, with every
$F_i$ irreducible of positive degree.
Write each $F_i$ as a scalar times
a primitive polynomial $f_i\in R[X]$.
Gauss's lemma says that
$f_1\cdots f_s$ is primitive.
Comparing contents with $f_0$
shows that the leftover scalar
is a unit of $R$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussirr}
makes each $f_i$ irreducible in
$R[X]$. We have therefore built
an irreducible factorisation of $f$.

\emph{Every irreducible is prime.}
An irreducible polynomial $P\in R[X]$
is either a non-unit constant $p$
or a positive-degree primitive polynomial.
Indeed a positive-degree irreducible
cannot have a non-unit content,
since that would give a factorisation.

If $P=p$ is constant, then $p$
is irreducible in $R$, hence prime
because $R$ is a UFD. The quotient
\[
R[X]/(p)\cong(R/(p))[X]
\]
is therefore an integral domain.
Thus $(p)$ is a prime ideal,
and $p$ is prime in $R[X]$.

If $P$ has positive degree,
Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussirr}
makes it irreducible in $K[X]$.
Since $K[X]$ is Euclidean,
$P$ is prime there. If
$P\mid AB$ in $R[X]$, it divides
$A$ or $B$ in $K[X]$. The divisibility
part of the same proposition brings
that conclusion back to $R[X]$.
Thus $P$ is prime in either case.

Every non-zero non-unit factors
into irreducibles, and every
irreducible is prime. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:primeufd}, $R[X]$
is a UFD.

Finally, $\mathbb Z$ and every field
are UFDs. The first gives
$\mathbb Z[X]$; applying the theorem
repeatedly to
\[
K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]
=K[X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}][X_n]
\]
proves the assertion for all $n$.
\end{proof}

Volume~1, Chapter~6, proved Gauss' lemma and Eisenstein's criterion for $\Z[X]$; the same arguments now work over any UFD. For Eisenstein, take a prime
element $p$ not dividing the leading coefficient, dividing all lower
coefficients, with $p^2$ not dividing the constant coefficient. In a
positive-degree factorisation, $p$ divides exactly one constant factor.
Choose in that factor the least index whose coefficient is not divisible
by $p$. It is below the total degree; its product with the other constant
factor makes that coefficient of the product nonzero modulo $p$, while
all other terms there vanish. This contradicts the coefficient hypothesis.
Gauss's lemma transfers the exclusion to the fraction field. Another tool is reduction: if $f\in\Z[X]$ is monic and its reduction mod $p$ is irreducible over $\Z/p\Z$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\Q$ (a factorisation into monic polynomials of lower degree would reduce to one mod $p$). The converse fails badly, see Exercise~\ref{ex:x4p1}.

\begin{beauty}
Nonunique factorisation obstructed nineteenth-century attempts on Fermat's Last Theorem. Kummer developed ideal numbers, and Dedekind later made ideals into sets with arithmetic of their own. Volume~13, Chapters~1 and~2, will prove the resulting theorem: in the ring of integers of a number field every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals, even when numbers do not. That later theorem is an outlook here, not a premise for our ring arguments.

The Chinese remainder theorem also gives a concrete computational tool.
Suppose an integer coefficient $a$ is known to satisfy $|a|\le B$.
Its residues modulo pairwise coprime integers determine it uniquely as
soon as their product $M$ exceeds $2B$: two candidates would differ by
a multiple of $M$ of absolute value at most $2B$, hence by zero.
\emph{Multi-modular} polynomial algorithms use this principle to
reconstruct integer coefficients after calculations modulo several
primes. Bounds and checks are part of those algorithms; the CRT supplies
the uniqueness step just proved.
\end{beauty}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:idealsZn}
Let $n\ge1$ be an integer. Describe the ideals of $\Z/n\Z$, and say which are prime and which are maximal. Find the units and the zero divisors of $\Z/12\Z$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:quot}
Show that $\R[X]/(X^2-1)\cong\R\times\R$, and that $\R[X]/(X^2)$ is not isomorphic to it, nor to $\C$. (Look for elements with $x^2=0$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:zsqrtm5}
In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, show that the ideal $I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal, that $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]/I\cong\Z/2\Z$, so $I$ is maximal, and that $3$ is irreducible but not prime.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:zi}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that every complex number $z$ is at distance at most $\frac1{\sqrt2}$ from some Gaussian integer, and deduce again that $\Z[i]$ is Euclidean for $N(z)=|z|^2$.
\item Do the same for $\Z[\sqrt{-2}]$.
\item Why does the argument fail for $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$? Find $a,b$ for which no $q\in\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ satisfies $N(a-bq)<N(b)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:zxnotpid}
Show that $(2,X)$ is not a principal ideal of $\Z[X]$, so $\Z[X]$ is not a PID. More generally show that $R[X]$ is a PID iff $R$ is a field.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:boolean}
A ring is \emph{Boolean} if $x^2=x$ for every $x$. Show that in a Boolean ring $2x=0$ for all $x$, that the only Boolean domain is $\Z/2\Z$, and that every prime ideal of a Boolean ring is maximal. (Example: the subsets of a set, with symmetric difference as sum and intersection as product.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:crtinterp}
Use Theorem~\ref{thm:crtring} in $\Q[X]$ to find the polynomial $P$ of degree at most $2$ with $P(0)=1$, $P(1)=0$ and $P'(1)=2$. (Note that $P(1)=a$, $P'(1)=b$ is a condition modulo $(X-1)^2$.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:x4p1}
Show that $X^4+1$ is irreducible in $\Q[X]$, but that its reduction modulo $p$ is reducible for every prime $p$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:idealsZn}
\idea An ideal of a quotient ring comes from an ideal of the original ring containing the quotient's kernel. Every ideal of $\Z$ is generated by one integer. This reduces the entire classification to the divisors of $n$.

\writeup Let $\pi:\Z\to\Z/n\Z$ be reduction modulo $n$. The inverse image of any ideal $I$ of $\Z/n\Z$ is an ideal of $\Z$ containing $\ker\pi=n\Z$. Thus $\pi^{-1}(I)=d\Z$ for some positive divisor $d$ of $n$. Conversely each such divisor gives an ideal
\[
I_d=d\Z/n\Z=\{[dk]_n:k\in\Z\}.
\]
These are all the ideals, and $I_d=I_{d'}$ only if $d=d'$.

The homomorphism $[a]_n\mapsto[a]_d$ has kernel $I_d$ and is onto, so
\[
(\Z/n\Z)/I_d\cong\Z/d\Z.
\]
A proper ideal is prime exactly when its quotient is an integral domain, and maximal exactly when its quotient is a field. For $d>1$, the ring $\Z/d\Z$ is a domain if and only if $d$ is prime, in which case it is already a field. Therefore the prime ideals and the maximal ideals coincide:
\[
\boxed{I_p=p\Z/n\Z\quad\text{for every prime }p\mid n.}
\]
(The zero ring obtained from $d=1$ does not define a proper prime or maximal ideal.)

In $\Z/12\Z$, Bézout's identity tells us that $[a]$ is invertible precisely when $\gcd(a,12)=1$. The units are
\[
\boxed{\{[1],[5],[7],[11]\}}.
\]
Every other non-zero class is a zero divisor: if $g=\gcd(a,12)>1$, then
\[
a\cdot\frac{12}{g}\equiv0\pmod{12},
\]
while $[12/g]\ne[0]$ because $1<g\le12$ and $a\not\equiv0$. Thus the non-zero zero divisors are
\[
\boxed{\{[2],[3],[4],[6],[8],[9],[10]\}}.
\]
The class $[0]$ is also a zero divisor under definitions allowing zero itself, so we have explicitly separated it here.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:quot}
\idea The two linear factors $X-1$ and $X+1$ have no common root, so the Chinese remainder theorem should split the first quotient. The quotient by $X^2$ is different: it contains a non-zero class whose square is zero. Isomorphisms cannot erase such an element.

\writeup Work in $\R[X]$. The ideals $(X-1)$ and $(X+1)$ are comaximal, because
\[
\frac12(X+1)-\frac12(X-1)=1.
\]
Their intersection therefore equals their product:
\[
(X-1)\cap(X+1)=((X-1)(X+1))=(X^2-1).
\]
By the Chinese remainder theorem for rings,
\[
\R[X]/(X^2-1)
\cong\R[X]/(X-1)\times\R[X]/(X+1).
\]
Evaluation at $1$ and $-1$ identifies the two factors with $\R$. Explicitly, the isomorphism sends
\[
[P]\longmapsto(P(1),P(-1)).
\]
It is onto: for any $a,b\in\R$, the linear polynomial
\[
P(X)=\frac{a+b}2+\frac{a-b}2X
\]
has $P(1)=a$ and $P(-1)=b$. Its kernel is $(X^2-1)$, as the factor theorem shows. Hence
\[
\boxed{\R[X]/(X^2-1)\cong\R\times\R}.
\]

Now put $A=\R[X]/(X^2)$ and write $x=[X]$. The element $x$ is non-zero: $X$ is not a multiple of $X^2$. But
\[
x^2=[X^2]=0.
\]
So $A$ has a \emph{non-zero nilpotent}. This property is preserved by ring isomorphisms.

Neither $\R\times\R$ nor $\C$ has a non-zero element whose square is zero. Indeed, $(a,b)^2=(0,0)$ implies $a^2=b^2=0$, hence $a=b=0$; and in the field $\C$, $z^2=0$ implies $z=0$. Therefore
\[
A\not\cong\R\times\R,\qquad A\not\cong\C.
\]
The contrast is useful: the two \emph{distinct} roots $\pm1$ split a quotient into two fields, while the \emph{repeated} root $0$ leaves behind a non-zero infinitesimal-like class $x$ with $x^2=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:zsqrtm5}
\idea Three tools handle the three requests. Use the integer norm to test whether the ideal could have one generator; reduce coefficients modulo $2$ to recognise the quotient; use the factorisation of $6$ to show that an irreducible element need not be prime.

\writeup Put $R=\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ and $I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$. Every element of $R$ is uniquely $a+b\sqrt{-5}$ with $a,b\in\Z$. Its norm
\[
N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2
\]
is a non-negative integer and is multiplicative.

\emph{The ideal is not principal.} Suppose $I=(d)$. Then $d$ divides both $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$, so the positive integer $N(d)$ divides
\[
N(2)=4,\qquad N(1+\sqrt{-5})=6.
\]
Thus $N(d)=1$ or $2$. There is no element of norm $2$: the equation $a^2+5b^2=2$ has no integer solution. Hence $N(d)=1$ and $d$ is a unit, forcing $I=R$.

But $I$ is proper. Indeed, the map
\[
\psi:R\to\mathbb F_2,\qquad
\psi(a+b\sqrt{-5})=[a+b]
\]
respects multiplication: modulo $2$, the relation $(\sqrt{-5})^2=-5$ becomes $1$, so sending $\sqrt{-5}$ to $1$ is compatible with multiplication. Both generators of $I$ map to zero, while $\psi(1)=1$. Consequently $I\subsetneq R$, a contradiction. So $I$ is not principal.

\emph{The quotient is a field.} The map $\psi$ is onto because $\psi(1)=1$. Its kernel consists of elements with $a+b$ even. Such an element can be written
\[
a+b\sqrt{-5}=(a-b)+b(1+\sqrt{-5}),
\]
and $a-b$ is even precisely when $a+b$ is. Thus $\ker\psi=I$. The first isomorphism theorem for rings gives
\[
R/I\cong\mathbb F_2.
\]
Since this quotient is a field, $I$ is maximal.

\emph{Irreducible is not prime.} An element of norm $3$ does not exist: $a^2+5b^2=3$ has no integer solution. If $3=uv$ with both factors non-units, then $N(u)N(v)=N(3)=9$ and both norms are integers greater than $1$. The only possibility is $N(u)=N(v)=3$, which is impossible. Thus $3$ is irreducible.

On the other hand,
\[
(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})=6=2\cdot3,
\]
so $3$ divides the product. It divides neither factor: an equality $1\pm\sqrt{-5}=3(a+b\sqrt{-5})$ would require $3a=1$. Hence $3$ is \emph{not} prime. This is the precise failure of unique factorisation that ideals will help us understand later.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:zi}
\idea Division in an imaginary quadratic integer ring is a lattice-rounding problem: divide $a$ by $b$ in $\C$, choose a nearby lattice point $q$, and put $r=a-bq$. The norm inequality follows when the squared distance from $a/b$ to $q$ is strictly less than $1$.

\writeup \emph{(a) Gaussian integers.} Write $z=x+iy\in\C$ with $x,y\in\R$. Choose ordinary integers $m,n$ nearest to $x$ and $y$, so
\[
|x-m|\le\frac12,\qquad |y-n|\le\frac12.
\]
For $q=m+in\in\Z[i]$,
\[
|z-q|^2=(x-m)^2+(y-n)^2\le\frac14+\frac14=\frac12.
\]
Thus every complex number is within distance $1/\sqrt2$ of a Gaussian integer.

Now take $a,b\in\Z[i]$ with $b\ne0$ and apply this rounding to $z=a/b$. Choose $q\in\Z[i]$ with $|a/b-q|^2\le1/2$ and define $r=a-bq\in\Z[i]$. Then
\[
a=bq+r,\qquad
N(r)=|b|^2|a/b-q|^2\le\frac12N(b)<N(b).
\]
This is Euclidean division for $N(z)=|z|^2$.

\emph{(b) Integers in $\Q(\sqrt{-2})$.} The lattice $\Z[\sqrt{-2}]$ consists of points $m+n\sqrt2\,i$. Given $z=x+iy$, round $x$ to an integer $m$ and $y/\sqrt2$ to an integer $n$. Then
\[
|z-(m+n\sqrt2\,i)|^2
\le\frac14+\frac{2}{4}=\frac34<1.
\]
Given $a,b\in\Z[\sqrt{-2}]$ with $b\ne0$, take such a $q$ near $a/b$ and put $r=a-bq$. The same calculation yields
\[
N(r)\le\frac34 N(b)<N(b).
\]
Hence $\Z[\sqrt{-2}]$ is Euclidean for the norm.

\emph{(c) Why this fails for $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$.} Here the lattice has points $m+n\sqrt5\,i$. The elementary rounding argument gives only
\[
|z-q|^2\le\frac14+\frac54=\frac32,
\]
which is \emph{not} enough for Euclidean division.

This is not just a weak bound: take
\[
z=\frac{1+\sqrt{-5}}2=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt5}{2}i.
\]
For every lattice point $q=m+n\sqrt{-5}$, with $m,n\in\Z$,
\[
|z-q|^2
=\left(\frac12-m\right)^2
 +5\left(\frac12-n\right)^2
\ge\frac14+\frac54=\frac32.
\]
Equality is attained at the four nearest lattice points with $m,n\in\{0,1\}$, so the lower bound is sharp.

Take $a=1+\sqrt{-5}$ and $b=2$, both in the ring and with $b\ne0$. Since $a/b=z$, every possible quotient $q\in\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ leaves remainder $r=a-bq$ satisfying
\[
N(r)=N(b)\,|a/b-q|^2
\ge4\cdot\frac32=6>4=N(b).
\]
No $q$ gives the required strict Euclidean remainder inequality.

This proves that the \emph{norm} fails as a Euclidean function. In fact the ring is not Euclidean for \emph{any} function, because every Euclidean domain is a PID whereas Exercise~\ref{ex:zsqrtm5} constructed a non-principal ideal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:zxnotpid}
\idea A single generator for $(a,X)$ would have to divide both a non-zero constant $a$ and the polynomial $X$. Over a domain, the first condition forces the generator to be constant and the second forces it to be a unit.

\writeup Begin with the general claim. Suppose $R$ is an integral domain that is \emph{not} a field. Choose a non-zero non-unit $a\in R$; such an element exists, or else every non-zero element would be invertible and $R$ would be a field. Consider the ideal
\[
I=(a,X)\subseteq R[X].
\]

Assume, for a contradiction, that $I$ is principal: $I=(d)$ for some $d\in R[X]$. Since $a\in I$, we have $a=du$ for some $u\in R[X]$. Because $a\ne0$ and $R$ is a domain,
\[
0=\deg a=\deg d+\deg u.
\]
Thus $\deg d=0$ and $d$ is a non-zero constant from $R$.

But $X\in I$, so $X=dv(X)$. Comparing coefficients of $X$ gives
\[
1=d\cdot(\text{coefficient of }X\text{ in }v),
\]
which makes $d$ a unit of $R$. Therefore $I=(d)=R[X]$ and in particular $1\in I$.

There would then be polynomials $f,g\in R[X]$ such that
\[
1=af(X)+Xg(X).
\]
Evaluate at $X=0$:
\[
1=af(0),
\]
contradicting the fact that $a$ is a non-unit. So $I$ is not principal and $R[X]$ is not a PID.

For $R=\Z$, take $a=2$. This proves in particular that $(2,X)$ is not principal in $\Z[X]$.

Conversely, if $R=K$ is a field, division with remainder by any non-zero polynomial makes $K[X]$ a Euclidean domain with degree as the Euclidean function. Every Euclidean domain is a PID (Theorem~\ref{thm:edpidufd}). We have proved the exact equivalence
\[
\boxed{R[X]\text{ is a PID}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R\text{ is a field}}
\]
for integral domains $R$. If $R$ has zero divisors, then $R[X]$ is not a domain and therefore is not a PID under our definition.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:boolean}
\idea Idempotence of every element puts a strong constraint on addition. Expand $(x+y)^2=x+y$ and compare it with $x^2=x$ and $y^2=y$. Once the ring has characteristic $2$, examine the equation $x(x-1)=0$ in any domain quotient.

\writeup Let $R$ be a Boolean ring, so $x^2=x$ for all $x\in R$. Apply the defining condition to $2x=x+x$:
\[
(2x)^2=2x.
\]
Since the ring is commutative, the left side is $4x^2=4x$. Hence $4x=2x$, and subtracting $2x$ in the additive group gives
\[
\boxed{2x=0\quad\text{for every }x\in R}.
\]
Thus $R$ has characteristic $2$ if $1\ne0$. The identity also implies $-x=x$.

Now suppose $R$ is both Boolean and an integral domain. Each $x$ satisfies
\[
x^2-x=x(x-1)=0.
\]
The absence of zero divisors forces $x=0$ or $x=1$. Since $0\ne1$ in a domain, the ring consists of exactly these two elements; its addition and multiplication are uniquely those of $\mathbb F_2$. Thus
\[
\boxed{R\cong\Z/2\Z}.
\]

If $P$ is a prime ideal of a Boolean ring $R$, the quotient $R/P$ is an integral domain. It is still Boolean, because
\[
(x+P)^2=x^2+P=x+P.
\]
By the preceding argument, $R/P\cong\mathbb F_2$, which is a field. Consequently $P$ is maximal. Every maximal ideal is prime in a commutative unital ring, so in a Boolean ring prime and maximal ideals coincide.

For the proposed example, let $R$ be the power set of a set $E$, with symmetric difference $A\triangle B$ as addition and intersection $A\cap B$ as multiplication. Distributivity follows from the membership truth tables, the additive identity is $\varnothing$, and the multiplicative identity is $E$. Since $A\cap A=A$, every element is idempotent. For any fixed $e\in E$, the map $A\mapsto 1$ if $e\in A$, and $0$ otherwise, is a surjective ring homomorphism to $\mathbb F_2$; its kernel is therefore a maximal ideal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:crtinterp}
\idea A value at $0$ is a congruence modulo $X$. A value and derivative at $1$ form a congruence modulo $(X-1)^2$. The two ideals are comaximal, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives one polynomial class modulo their product; then choose its representative of degree at most $2$.

\writeup We seek $P\in\Q[X]$ with
\[
\deg P\le2,\qquad P(0)=1,\qquad P(1)=0,\qquad P'(1)=2.
\]
The first condition on values means
\[
P\equiv1\pmod X.
\]
Division by $(X-1)^2$ gives a remainder $a+b(X-1)$. Evaluating at $1$
identifies $a=P(1)$; differentiating and evaluating there identifies
$b=P'(1)$, since the derivative of a multiple of $(X-1)^2$ vanishes
at $1$. Thus, using only polynomial division and differentiation,
\[
P(X)\equiv P(1)+P'(1)(X-1)
=2(X-1)\pmod{(X-1)^2}.
\]

The ideals $(X)$ and $((X-1)^2)$ are comaximal. An explicit Bézout identity is
\[
1=(X-1)^2-X(X-2).
\]
It gives two complementary CRT idempotents:
\[
e_0=(X-1)^2,\qquad e_1=-X(X-2)=1-e_0.
\]
Indeed, $e_0\equiv1\pmod X$ and $e_0\equiv0\pmod{(X-1)^2}$, while $e_1$ has the opposite residues.

Therefore
\[
P_0=e_0+2(X-1)e_1
\]
satisfies both required congruences. Expand it:
\[
\begin{aligned}
P_0
&=(X-1)^2-2X(X-1)(X-2)\\
&=-2X^3+7X^2-6X+1.
\end{aligned}
\]
The modulus is
\[
X(X-1)^2=X^3-2X^2+X.
\]
Subtracting $-2$ times that modulus removes the cubic term and leaves
\[
\boxed{P(X)=3X^2-4X+1}.
\]

Check all three conditions directly:
\[
P(0)=1,\qquad P(1)=3-4+1=0,\qquad
P'(1)=6-4=2.
\]
Finally, two solutions differ by a multiple of both $X$ and $(X-1)^2$, hence by a multiple of their degree-three product. A non-zero polynomial of degree at most $2$ cannot have that factor. Therefore the answer is unique.

This is a small instance of \emph{Hermite interpolation}: a derivative requirement is just a congruence modulo a repeated linear factor.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:x4p1}
\idea For irreducibility over $\Q$, shift the variable so Eisenstein's criterion becomes visible. For reducibility modulo every prime, find three different quadratic factorizations. One of them works for each odd prime because $(-1)\cdot2\cdot(-2)$ is a square.

\writeup Over $\Q$, consider the translate
\[
f(X+1)=(X+1)^4+1=X^4+4X^3+6X^2+4X+2.
\]
All non-leading coefficients are divisible by $2$, while the constant coefficient $2$ is not divisible by $4$. Eisenstein's criterion at $2$ makes this polynomial irreducible in $\Q[X]$. The substitution $X\mapsto X+1$ is an automorphism of $\Q[X]$ with inverse $X\mapsto X-1$. It preserves reducibility, so
\[
\boxed{X^4+1\text{ is irreducible over }\Q.}
\]

Modulo $2$, the binomial identity in characteristic $2$ gives
\[
X^4+1=(X+1)^4,
\]
so the reduction is reducible.

Let $p$ be odd. Write $\left(\frac ap\right)$ for the Legendre symbol, taking values $\pm1$ for non-zero residues. The numbers $-1,2,-2$ are all non-zero modulo $p$. Their product is $4$, a square, hence
\[
\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac2p\right)
\left(\frac{-2}{p}\right)=\left(\frac4p\right)=1.
\]
They cannot all be non-squares, for then the product of the three symbols would be $(-1)^3=-1$. At least one has a square root modulo $p$.

If $a^2=-1$, then
\[
X^4+1=(X^2-a)(X^2+a).
\]
If $b^2=2$, then
\[
X^4+1=(X^2+1)^2-(bX)^2
=(X^2+bX+1)(X^2-bX+1).
\]
If $c^2=-2$, then
\[
X^4+1=(X^2-1)^2-(cX)^2
=(X^2+cX-1)(X^2-cX-1).
\]
Each is a genuine factorisation into monic quadratics. Thus $X^4+1$ is reducible modulo every prime $p$ even though it is irreducible over $\Q$.

This shows why a reduction-based irreducibility test only works in one direction. Irreducibility after reduction \emph{can} certify irreducibility over $\Q$, but failing that test at every prime need not mean reducibility over $\Q$.
\end{solution}
