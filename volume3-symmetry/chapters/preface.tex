\chapter*{Preface}
Algebra is the study of structure: what is preserved when an object is transformed, and what can be said about
it from that alone. This volume of \emph{Order and Chance} builds groups, rings, fields and their representations
from the idea of symmetry, and climbs to two summits.

The first is Galois' answer to a question that had stood for three centuries. There is a formula in radicals for
the roots of every equation of degree $4$, but there is none for $x^5-4x+2=0$, and the reason is that a certain
group of permutations of its roots is too large to be built from abelian pieces. The second summit is the
character table of a finite group, a small square of numbers that holds most of what there is to know about the
group, and that Chapter~11 computes by hand for the first interesting cases.

The chapters follow two tracks that meet at Galois theory. Chapters~1--5 are about groups: symmetries, morphisms
and quotients, group actions and the art of counting orbits, the symmetric group, and the Sylow theorems that
classify the small finite groups. Chapters~6--10 are about rings and fields: ideals, modules over a principal ideal
domain (which classify finite abelian groups and normal forms of matrices at one stroke), field extensions,
finite fields and Galois theory. Chapter~11 is about representations, and Chapter~12 about quaternions and the
rotations of space. Three appendices close the book, and each of them counts by symmetry: the Perron root of a positive matrix, the circulants that a symmetry diagonalises, and the linear algebra method in combinatorics. The spectral theorem for matrices, singular values, graph spectra, matrix inequalities and linear dynamics are in Volume~8, where they come before the spectral theorem for compact operators. The cost of multiplying matrices is in Volume~10, before the tensor product of modules.

Every theorem, proposition and lemma is proved; results quoted only for their interest appear as remarks, with a
reference to where they are proved. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a good
afternoon and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} for gems, and every one has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its
chapter, beginning with \emph{How to find it} before the \emph{Write-up}. ``Pause and think'' boxes ask you to guess
before you read on.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random yet obey laws. This floor is the symmetries of the plan: what is preserved by a transformation, up to a polynomial whose Galois group is $S_5$, and a character table. The next floor takes up the load of uncertainty. Those characters are not yet aimed at the primes.
