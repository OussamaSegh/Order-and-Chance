\chapter{Group Actions and Counting}

\begin{center}\itshape
How many necklaces can be made with $6$ beads in $3$ colours, if two necklaces that differ by a rotation are the same?\\
Not $3^6/6$, which is not even an integer. The right answer, $130$, comes from averaging over the group.
\end{center}

String six beads together, each either dark or light, and fasten the ends. If the necklace can turn freely, rotating the beads does not make a new necklace. How many different necklaces are there?

There are $2^6=64$ colour strings if we choose a starting bead and a direction. Dividing $64$ by $6$ seems tempting. It is wrong. The all-dark necklace remains unchanged under every rotation, while a necklace with just one dark bead has six distinct positions. We cannot pretend that every necklace comes in a group of six strings.

A \emph{group action} is a way for a group to move objects. Here the group consists of six rotations, and the objects are the $64$ colour strings. Strings connected by rotations form an \emph{orbit}. To count necklaces, we must count orbits, not strings.

There are two ideas to uncover. First, the number of moves that change a particular object is controlled by the number that leave it fixed: the orbit--stabiliser theorem. Second, if we count the fixed strings for each rotation and average those counts, we get the number of orbits. That is Burnside's lemma. For our necklace, the answer will be $14$.

The same fixed-point count works on a group acting on itself by conjugation. It divides the group into conjugacy classes and begins to explain why groups of prime-power order have special subgroups. Counting necklaces and understanding finite groups turn out to use the same move.

\section{Actions, orbits and stabilisers}

\begin{definition}
An \emph{action} of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a map $G\times X\to X$, $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$, such that $e\cdot x=x$ and $g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x$ for all $g,h\in G$, $x\in X$.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, each $g$ gives a map $\rho_g:x\mapsto g\cdot x$, and the axioms say $\rho_e=\mathrm{id}$ and $\rho_g\circ\rho_h=\rho_{gh}$. So $\rho_g$ is a bijection with inverse $\rho_{g^{-1}}$, and an action is the same thing as a homomorphism $\rho:G\to\mathfrak S(X)$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:actions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item $\mathfrak S_n$ acts on $\{1,\dots,n\}$, $D_n$ on the vertices of the $n$-gon, the rotations of the cube on its faces, edges, vertices or long diagonals, $GL_n(K)$ on $K^n$.
\item $G$ acts on itself by left multiplication, $g\cdot x=gx$, and by \emph{conjugation}, $g\cdot x=gxg^{-1}$.
\item For $H\le G$, $G$ acts on the set $G/H$ of left cosets by $g\cdot xH=gxH$.
\item If $G$ acts on $X$, it acts on the colourings $f:X\to C$ by $(g\cdot f)(x)=f(g^{-1}\cdot x)$ (the inverse is needed for the second axiom).
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Cayley]\label{thm:cayley}
Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\mathfrak S(G)$. In particular every group of order $n$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\mathfrak S_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The action by left multiplication gives a homomorphism $\rho:G\to\mathfrak S(G)$, and $\rho_g=\mathrm{id}$ forces $g=ge=e$, so $\rho$ is injective. By the first isomorphism theorem, $G\cong\rho(G)$. Numbering the elements of $G$ identifies $\mathfrak S(G)$ with $\mathfrak S_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $G$ act on $X$. The \emph{orbit} of $x$ is $G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$, its \emph{stabiliser} is $G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}$, and the \emph{fixed points} of $g$ form $X^g=\{x:g\cdot x=x\}$. The action is \emph{transitive} if there is only one orbit.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[orbit--stabiliser]\label{thm:orbstab}
Let $G$ act on $X$ and $x\in X$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The orbits partition $X$.
\item $G_x$ is a subgroup of $G$, and $gG_x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection $G/G_x\to G\cdot x$. Hence, for $G$ finite,
\[
|G\cdot x|=[G:G_x]=\frac{|G|}{|G_x|}.
\]
\item $G_{g\cdot x}=gG_xg^{-1}$: points in the same orbit have conjugate stabilisers.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Write $x\sim y$ if $y=g\cdot x$ for some $g$. This is an equivalence relation: $x=e\cdot x$; $y=g\cdot x$ gives $x=g^{-1}\cdot y$; $y=g\cdot x$ and $z=h\cdot y$ give $z=(hg)\cdot x$. Its classes are the orbits.
(b) $e\in G_x$, and if $g,h\in G_x$ then $gh^{-1}\cdot x=g\cdot(h^{-1}\cdot(h\cdot x))=g\cdot x=x$. Next, $g\cdot x=h\cdot x$ iff $h^{-1}g\cdot x=x$ iff $h^{-1}g\in G_x$ iff $gG_x=hG_x$: read in one direction the map is well defined, in the other it is injective; it is onto the orbit by definition. Then use Lagrange.
(c) $k\cdot(g\cdot x)=g\cdot x$ iff $g^{-1}kg\cdot x=x$ iff $g^{-1}kg\in G_x$ iff $k\in gG_xg^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Some moves look different but take $x$ to the same place. Which ones? Exactly those that differ by a move fixing $x$. That is why the right objects to count are the cosets of $G_x$. For the six-bead necklace with one dark bead, only the identity rotation fixes the colouring, so the orbit contains $6/1=6$ strings. For the all-dark colouring every rotation fixes it, so the orbit contains $6/6=1$ string. Both results come from the same formula; no special exception is needed for symmetric objects.

This gives a second proof that the cube has $24$ rotations: they act transitively on the $6$ faces, and the stabiliser of the top face is the group of $4$ rotations about the vertical axis, so there are $6\times4=24$ of them.

Orbit--stabiliser counts how many positions one object can reach. To count all distinct objects up to symmetry, we need to add those orbit counts without listing the orbits first. Counting pairs of a symmetry and an object it fixes gives the needed average.

\section{Counting orbits}

\begin{think}
Colour each of the $6$ beads of a necklace black or white: $2^6=64$ colourings. Two colourings give the same necklace if a rotation takes one to the other. Most necklaces come from $6$ colourings, but not all. Can you find the ones that come from fewer? Then guess the number of necklaces.
\end{think}

\begin{theorem}[Burnside's lemma]\label{thm:burnside}
Let a finite group $G$ act on a finite set $X$. The number of orbits is the average number of fixed points:
\[
|X/G|=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Consider the set
\[
\mathcal P=\{(g,x)\in G\times X:g\cdot x=x\}.
\]
An element of $\mathcal P$ consists of a group element together with a point that it fixes. There are two ways to count these pairs.

First fix $g$. It appears in one pair for each element of $X^g$, the set of points fixed by $g$. Therefore
\[
|\mathcal P|=\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|.
\]
Now fix $x$. It appears once for each element of its stabiliser $G_x$, so also
\[
|\mathcal P|=\sum_{x\in X}|G_x|.
\]

To simplify the second sum, group the points into orbits. If $O$ is one orbit and $x\in O$, orbit--stabiliser gives $|G_x|=|G|/|O|$. Every point of $O$ has a stabiliser of the same size. Thus the entire orbit contributes
\[
\sum_{y\in O}|G_y|=|O|\frac{|G|}{|O|}=|G|.
\]
Each orbit contributes exactly one copy of $|G|$, however symmetric its points happen to be. We conclude that
\[
\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|=|\mathcal P|=|G|\cdot|X/G|.
\]
Divide by $|G|$ to obtain Burnside's formula.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Try the six-bead example before memorising the average. The identity fixes all $64$ binary strings. Rotating one or five places fixes only the two constant strings. A rotation by two or four places has two bead cycles, so it fixes $2^2=4$ strings. The half-turn has three cycles, so it fixes $2^3=8$. The answer is
\[
\frac{64+2+4+8+4+2}{6}=14.
\]
Each colouring contributes as many pairs $(g,x)$ as the size of its stabiliser. Orbit--stabiliser makes the total contribution of \emph{every orbit} exactly $|G|$. That is the reason the average counts necklaces rather than simply estimating them.

For the necklaces of the opening with $2$ colours, the rotation by $k$ steps of a $6$-bead necklace fixes a colouring iff the colouring is constant on the cycles of the rotation, which has $\gcd(k,6)$ cycles. The numbers of fixed colourings for $k=0,\dots,5$ are $64,2,4,8,4,2$, whose average is $\frac{84}6=14$. The $14$ necklaces are drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:necklaces}.

The general formula takes one more idea: a colouring fixed by $g$ is the same thing as a colouring constant on the cycles of $g$.

\begin{theorem}[P\'olya's counting theorem]\label{thm:polya}
Let a finite group $G$ act on a finite set $X$, and for $g\in G$ let $c(g)$ be the number of cycles of the permutation $\rho_g$ of $X$. The number of colourings of $X$ with $k$ colours, up to the action of $G$, is
\[
\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}k^{c(g)}.
\]
More precisely, give each colour $c$ a weight $w_c$ (a variable), and a colouring $f$ the weight $w(f)=\prod_{x\in X}w_{f(x)}$. If $g$ has $c_j(g)$ cycles of length $j$, the sum of the weights of one colouring per orbit is
\[
\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\ \prod_{j\ge1}\Big(\sum_{c}w_c^{\,j}\Big)^{c_j(g)}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A colouring $f$ is fixed by $g$ iff $f(g^{-1}\cdot x)=f(x)$ for all $x$, iff $f$ is constant on each cycle of $\rho_g$. Choosing one colour per cycle gives the $k^{c(g)}$ fixed colourings, and Burnside's lemma gives the first formula.
For the weighted version, $w(g\cdot f)=w(f)$ (the same colours are used, permuted), so all colourings in an orbit have the same weight $w(O)$. Burnside's double count, with each pair $(g,f)$, $g\cdot f=f$, counted with weight $w(f)$, gives $\sum_g\sum_{f\in X^g}w(f)=|G|\sum_Ow(O)$ in the same way. For a fixed $g$, choosing a colour $c$ on a cycle of length $j$ contributes $w_c^{\,j}$, and the cycles are chosen independently: $\sum_{f\in X^g}w(f)=\prod_j\left(\sum_cw_c^{\,j}\right)^{c_j(g)}$.
\end{proof}

The polynomial $Z_G=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\prod_jt_j^{c_j(g)}$ is the \emph{cycle index} of the action; P\'olya's theorem says to substitute $t_j=\sum_cw_c^{\,j}$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:necklaceformula}
For the cyclic group of rotations of an $n$-bead necklace, the rotation by $k$ steps has $\gcd(k,n)$ cycles, all of length $\frac n{\gcd(k,n)}$. Grouping the $k$ by $d=\frac n{\gcd(k,n)}$ (there are $\varphi(d)$ of them, Theorem~\ref{thm:cyclic}), the number of necklaces with $k$ colours is
\[
N(n,k)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)\,k^{n/d}.
\]
For $n=6$, $k=3$: $\frac16(729+27+2\cdot9+2\cdot3)=130$.
\end{example}

Rotations act on coloured necklaces. A group can also act on its own elements, by conjugation. The resulting orbits are conjugacy classes, and the same counting idea produces strong information about groups whose order is a power of a prime.

\section{Conjugacy classes and \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-groups}

When $G$ acts on itself by conjugation, the orbits are the \emph{conjugacy classes} $\{gxg^{-1}:g\in G\}$, and the stabiliser of $x$ is its \emph{centraliser} $C(x)=\{g:gx=xg\}$. An element is alone in its class iff it lies in the centre $Z(G)$.

\begin{theorem}[class equation]\label{thm:classeq}
Let $G$ be finite, and let $x_1,\dots,x_r$ be representatives of the conjugacy classes with more than one element. Then
\[
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C(x_i)],
\]
and each $[G:C(x_i)]$ is a divisor of $|G|$ greater than $1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The classes partition $G$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:orbstab}(a)); the one-element classes make up $Z(G)$, and the class of $x_i$ has $[G:C(x_i)]$ elements by orbit--stabiliser, which divides $|G|$ by Lagrange.
\end{proof}

A \emph{$p$-group} is a group of order $p^k$, $k\ge1$, for a prime $p$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:pgroup}
The centre of a $p$-group is not trivial.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
In the class equation, $|G|$ and each $[G:C(x_i)]$ (a divisor of $p^k$ greater than $1$) are divisible by $p$. So $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$, which is at least $1$, hence at least $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy]\label{thm:cauchy}
If a prime $p$ divides the order of a finite group $G$, then $G$ has an element of order $p$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(J.~McKay's proof.) Let $X$ be the set of $p$-tuples $(g_1,\dots,g_p)$ of elements of $G$ with $g_1g_2\cdots g_p=e$. The first $p-1$ entries can be chosen freely and determine $g_p$, so $|X|=|G|^{p-1}$, a multiple of $p$. The cyclic group $\Z/p\Z$ acts on $X$ by rotating the tuples: if $g_1\cdots g_p=e$ then $g_2\cdots g_pg_1=g_1^{-1}(g_1\cdots g_p)g_1=e$. Orbits have size $1$ or $p$ (divisors of $p$). The orbits of size $1$ are the constant tuples $(g,\dots,g)$ with $g^p=e$; there is at least one, $(e,\dots,e)$. Since $|X|$ and the sizes of the large orbits are multiples of $p$, so is the number of fixed tuples. So there are at least $p$ of them, and some $g\ne e$ has $g^p=e$: its order is $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Burnside's lemma turns a question about counting objects up to symmetry, which looks hopeless, into a sum over the group, which is usually short: $24$ terms for the cube, grouped into $5$ kinds of rotations. P\'olya used it in 1937 to count chemical compounds (isomers), and it counts graphs, crystals and musical scales as well. The same double counting, in Cauchy's theorem, proves that a group must contain elements of every prime order dividing its size. In Chapter~5 a cleverer action, on subsets, will produce whole subgroups of prime power order: the Sylow subgroups.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:cubefaces}
In how many ways can the $6$ faces of a cube be painted with $3$ colours, two colourings being the same if a rotation takes one to the other? (Use the list of rotations of Proposition~\ref{prop:cube}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:fermatnecklace}
Using Example~\ref{ex:necklaceformula} for a prime number $p$ of beads, prove Fermat's little theorem $k^p\equiv k\pmod p$. Can you also see it directly on the necklaces?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:classesD}
Find the conjugacy classes of $D_4$ and of $Q_8$, and check the class equation for both.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:p2}
Show that every group of order $p^2$ ($p$ prime) is abelian, hence isomorphic to $\Z/p^2\Z$ or $\Z/p\Z\times\Z/p\Z$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:smallestprime}
Let $H\le G$ with $[G:H]=p$, where $p$ is the smallest prime dividing $|G|$. Show that $H$ is normal. (For $p=2$ this is Proposition~\ref{prop:normal}.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:bracelets}
A \emph{bracelet} is a necklace that may also be turned over: the dihedral group $D_n$ acts. Count the bracelets with $6$ beads in $2$ colours, and find in Figure~\ref{fig:necklaces} the two necklaces that become the same bracelet.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:graphs4}
Show, with P\'olya's theorem for $\mathfrak S_4$ acting on the $6$ pairs of vertices, that there are $11$ graphs on $4$ vertices up to isomorphism, and use the weighted version to find how many have $3$ edges.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:commuting}
Let $G$ be finite with $k(G)$ conjugacy classes. Show that the probability that two elements of $G$, chosen independently and uniformly, commute is $\frac{k(G)}{|G|}$. Show that it is at most $\frac58$ when $G$ is not abelian, and that $D_4$ attains $\frac58$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:sieving}
\emph{Cyclic sieving.} Let $[m]_q=1+q+\dots+q^{m-1}$, $[m]_q!=[1]_q[2]_q\cdots[m]_q$, and for $0\le k\le n$
\[
\qbinom nk=\frac{[n]_q!}{[k]_q!\,[n-k]_q!}.
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\qbinom nk=\qbinom{n-1}{k-1}+q^k\qbinom{n-1}k$, and deduce that $\qbinom nk$ is a polynomial in $q$ with
non-negative integer coefficients.
\item Let $m\mid n$ and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $m$-th root of unity. Show that the value of this polynomial at $\zeta$
is $\binom{n/m}{k/m}$ if $m\mid k$, and $0$ otherwise.
\item The rotation $g:x\mapsto x+1$ of $\Z/n\Z$ acts on the $k$-element subsets of $\Z/n\Z$. Show that for every $j$, the
number of subsets fixed by $g^j$ is the value of $\qbinom nk$ at $q=e^{2i\pi j/n}$.
\item Deduce the number of necklaces with $n$ beads, $k$ of them black, up to rotation, and check that there are $4$ for
$n=6$, $k=3$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:patterns}
Let $G$ act on a finite set $X$, and let $K$ be a set of $k$ colours. Two colourings $f:X\to K$ are \emph{equivalent} if
$f'=s\circ f\circ g^{-1}$ for some $g\in G$ and some permutation $s$ of $K$: the symmetry moves the points and the colours may
be renamed.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that for $g\in G$ and $s\in\mathfrak S(K)$, the number of colourings $f$ with $s\circ f\circ g^{-1}=f$ is
\[
\prod_{\text{cycles of }g}\ \Bigl(\sum_{j\mid\ell}j\,c_j(s)\Bigr),
\]
where the product is over the cycles of $g$ on $X$, $\ell$ is the length of the cycle, and $c_j(s)$ is the number of $j$-cycles
of $s$.
\item Count the necklaces of $n$ beads in two colours, up to rotation and up to exchanging the two colours. Check that there
are $8$ for $n=6$.
\item In how many essentially different ways can one paint the faces of a cube with at most three colours, if the colours
themselves do not matter (only which faces share a colour)?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:landau}
Let $G$ be a finite group with exactly $k$ conjugacy classes.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $|G|\le k^{2^{k-1}}$. So, for each $k$, only finitely many groups have $k$ classes.
\item Find all groups with at most $3$ conjugacy classes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:nilpotent}
\emph{Counting nilpotent matrices.} Let $N_n$ be the number of nilpotent matrices in $M_n(\mathbb F_q)$, with $N_0=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Fitting.) For $A\in M_n(\mathbb F_q)$ acting on $V=\mathbb F_q^n$, show that $V=\ker A^n\oplus\operatorname{im}A^n$,
that both are $A$-stable, that $A$ is nilpotent on the first and invertible on the second, and that this is the only
decomposition of $V$ into two $A$-stable subspaces with these properties.
\item Deduce that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{N_k}{|GL_k(\mathbb F_q)|}=\frac{q^{n^2}}{|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|}$ for every $n\ge0$.
\item (Fine and Herstein, 1958.) Prove that $N_n=q^{n^2-n}$. So a random matrix is nilpotent with probability exactly $q^{-n}$.
\item (Steinberg.) How many unipotent matrices ($A-I$ nilpotent) does $GL_n(\mathbb F_q)$ contain?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:flags}
\emph{Flags and the field with one element.} Let $V=\mathbb F_q^n$ and $[m]_q=1+q+\dots+q^{m-1}$, $[n]_q!=[1]_q[2]_q\cdots[n]_q$.
A \emph{complete flag} is a chain $0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\dots\subset V_n=V$ with $\dim V_i=i$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $V$ has $[n]_q!$ complete flags.
\item Let $U$ be a subspace of dimension $k$. Show that $U$ has exactly $q^{k(n-k)}$ complements $W$ (subspaces with $U\oplus W=V$).
\item Show that $|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|=q^{\binom n2}(q-1)^n\,[n]_q!$, by letting $GL_n$ act on flags.
\item Set $q=1$ in the formulas of (a) and (b). What do flags and complements become, and what does (c) say about the
symmetric group?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:touchard}
\emph{Touchard's congruence.} The Bell number $B_n$ is the number of partitions of the set $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into non-empty blocks.
Let $p$ be prime. By letting the cyclic group of order $p$ rotate the last $p$ elements of $\{1,\dots,n+p\}$, show that
\[
B_{n+p}\equiv B_n+B_{n+1}\pmod p.
\]
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:cubefaces}
\idea A colouring is fixed by a rotation exactly when every face in the same cycle has the same colour. A rotation with $c$ cycles on the six faces fixes $3^c$ colourings. Sort the $24$ rotations by their cycle structure, then apply Burnside's lemma.

\writeup We must count each rotation, not just each rotation axis. The five types are:
\[
\begin{array}{l|r|l|r}
\text{rotation}&\text{number}&\text{cycles on the six faces}&\text{fixed colourings}\\\hline
\text{identity}&1&1^6&3^6\\
90^\circ,\ 270^\circ\text{ about opposite faces}&6&1^2\,4&3^3\\
180^\circ\text{ about opposite faces}&3&1^2\,2^2&3^4\\
120^\circ,\ 240^\circ\text{ about opposite vertices}&8&3^2&3^2\\
180^\circ\text{ about opposite edges}&6&2^3&3^3
\end{array}
\]
For example, a quarter-turn fixes the two faces on its axis and cycles the other four. Thus we freely choose one colour for each of the two fixed faces and one for the four moving faces: $3^3=27$ fixed colourings. A rotation about opposite vertices has two three-cycles, hence only $3^2=9$ fixed colourings.

The counts of rotations sum to $1+6+3+8+6=24$, as required. Burnside's formula now gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
|X/G|
&=\frac{1}{24}\left(3^6+6\cdot3^3+3\cdot3^4+8\cdot3^2+6\cdot3^3\right)\\
&=\frac{729+162+243+72+162}{24}
=\frac{1368}{24}
=\boxed{57}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The division by $24$ comes \emph{after} counting fixed colourings. Dividing the total $3^6$ directly by $24$ would be wrong because some colourings have non-trivial rotational symmetries.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:fermatnecklace}
\idea A necklace is a rotation orbit. When the number of beads is prime, an orbit can have only one bead-pattern or $p$ distinct rotations. The one-element orbits are precisely the constant colourings.

\writeup Fix an integer $k\ge1$ of available colours and a prime $p$. A linear arrangement of $p$ beads has $k^p$ possible colourings. The cyclic group of $p$ rotations acts on these arrangements.

By the necklace formula (Example~\ref{ex:necklaceformula}), the number of rotation orbits is
\[
N(p,k)=\frac1p\bigl(k^p+(p-1)k\bigr).
\]
This is an integer because it counts necklaces, so
\[
p\mid k^p+(p-1)k
  =(k^p-k)+pk.
\]
Subtract the multiple $pk$ to obtain
\[
\boxed{k^p\equiv k\pmod p}.
\]
This also holds for $k=0$, for which there is no colouring when $p>0$; the congruence is immediate.

Here is the direct argument, which explains the arithmetic cancellation. A colouring fixed by a rotation through a non-zero number of positions must be constant: when $p$ is prime, repeatedly applying such a rotation visits all $p$ bead positions. There are exactly $k$ constant colourings.

Every other colouring has a rotation orbit larger than $1$. Orbit--stabiliser says its orbit size divides the prime $p$, so the size must be $p$. Thus the $k^p-k$ non-constant colourings split into disjoint sets of $p$, giving $p\mid(k^p-k)$.

Finally, the case $p\mid k$ is included automatically; no assumption that the number of colours is invertible modulo $p$ was used. This is the full congruence $k^p\equiv k$, not merely the unit-group form $k^{p-1}\equiv1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:classesD}
\idea First identify central elements, which form singleton conjugacy classes. Then conjugate each remaining generator by $r$ and $s$, and count the resulting classes. The class equation must record the individual sizes $1,1,2,2,2$, not just sum conveniently to $8$.

\writeup In $D_4=\langle r,s:r^4=s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle$, the centre is $\{e,r^2\}$ by Exercise~\ref{ex:dihedralcentre}. These give two singleton classes. Also
\[
srs^{-1}=r^{-1}=r^3,
\]
so $\{r,r^3\}$ is one conjugacy class. The reflections split into two classes. Indeed, using $rs=sr^{-1}$,
\[
rsr^{-1}=sr^{-2}=sr^2.
\]
Thus $s$ is conjugate to $sr^2$, and similarly $sr$ is conjugate to $sr^3$. Conjugating any reflection $sr^k$ by either generator changes $k$ by an even integer (or sends $k$ to its negative), so an even exponent cannot become an odd one. The five classes are exactly
\[
\boxed{\{e\},\quad\{r^2\},\quad\{r,r^3\},
       \quad\{s,sr^2\},\quad\{sr,sr^3\}}.
\]
Their class equation is $8=1+1+2+2+2$.

For $Q_8=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$, the elements $\pm1$ are central. Conjugation preserves order, and the multiplication rules give
\[
jij^{-1}=j\,i\,(-j)=-i.
\]
So $i$ and $-i$ are conjugate. The same calculation with the roles of $i,j,k$ rotated gives the three two-element classes $\{\pm i\}$, $\{\pm j\}$ and $\{\pm k\}$. No two of these classes can merge: the centraliser of $i$ is $\{\pm1,\pm i\}$, giving a class of size $8/4=2$, and similarly for $j,k$.

Therefore
\[
\boxed{\{1\},\quad\{-1\},\quad\{\pm i\},
       \quad\{\pm j\},\quad\{\pm k\}}
\]
is the complete class list for $Q_8$, and once again the class equation is $8=1+1+2+2+2$. The equality of class sizes does not mean that the two groups are isomorphic.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:p2}
\idea A finite $p$-group has a non-trivial centre. In a group of order $p^2$, this almost determines the whole group: if the centre had only $p$ elements, the quotient by it would be cyclic and force the group itself to be abelian.

\writeup Let $G$ have order $p^2$. By the class equation for finite $p$-groups (Corollary~\ref{cor:pgroup}), its centre $Z(G)$ has more than one element. Lagrange gives
\[
|Z(G)|\in\{p,p^2\}.
\]

Suppose $|Z(G)|=p$. Then the quotient $G/Z(G)$ has order $p$ and is cyclic, because every group of prime order is cyclic. But Exercise~\ref{ex:centre} proved that a group with cyclic quotient by its centre is abelian. Therefore $G$ would be abelian, making its centre all of $G$, of order $p^2$. This contradicts $|Z(G)|=p$.

Thus $|Z(G)|=p^2$, so $Z(G)=G$: every group of order $p^2$ is abelian.

It remains to classify it. If $G$ contains an element $g$ of order $p^2$, the subgroup $\langle g\rangle$ has all $p^2$ elements and $G\cong\Z/p^2\Z$.

Otherwise every non-identity element has order $p$, by Lagrange. Choose $a\ne e$, so $\langle a\rangle$ has order $p$; then choose $b\notin\langle a\rangle$. The subgroup $\langle b\rangle$ also has order $p$. Their intersection cannot have order $p$, for then the two groups would coincide and $b$ would belong to $\langle a\rangle$. Thus
\[
\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle=\{e\}.
\]
Since $G$ is abelian, both subgroups are normal and commute. Their product contains
\[
\frac{|\langle a\rangle|\,|\langle b\rangle|}
{|\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle|}
=p^2
\]
elements, so it equals $G$. The internal direct product theorem gives
\[
G\cong\langle a\rangle\times\langle b\rangle
\cong\Z/p\Z\times\Z/p\Z.
\]

The two possibilities are not isomorphic: one has an element of order $p^2$, the other does not. Hence they are exactly the two isomorphism types of groups of order $p^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:smallestprime}
\idea Turn the cosets of $H$ into a permutation representation on $p$ points. Its kernel is contained in $H$. Since the image has order dividing both $|G|$ and $p!$, the smallest-prime hypothesis leaves only one possible image size.

\writeup Let $[G:H]=p$, where $p$ is the smallest prime divisor of $|G|$. The left action of $G$ on the set of $p$ left cosets gives
\[
\rho:G\longrightarrow\mathfrak S_p,\qquad
g\cdot(xH)=(gx)H.
\]
Let $K=\ker\rho$. Every element of $K$ fixes every coset, in particular the coset $H$. So if $g\in K$, then $gH=H$ and $g\in H$. This gives $K\subseteq H$.

The first isomorphism theorem says
\[
[G:K]=|\rho(G)|.
\]
This order divides $p!$ by Lagrange in $\mathfrak S_p$, and it divides $|G|$ because $K$ is a subgroup of $G$. Every prime factor of $|G|$ is at least $p$, whereas every prime factor of $p!$ is at most $p$. Therefore the only common prime factor can be $p$. Further, $p!$ contains exactly one factor $p$, since $p$ occurs only in the last factor $1\cdot2\cdots p$. Hence
\[
[G:K]\in\{1,p\}.
\]

But the inclusion $K\subseteq H$ and the index formula give
\[
[G:K]=[G:H][H:K]=p[H:K]\ge p.
\]
Thus $[G:K]=p$, forcing $[H:K]=1$ and therefore $K=H$. As a kernel $K$ is normal in $G$, so
\[
\boxed{H\trianglelefteq G}.
\]

For $p=2$, this recovers the familiar fact that every index-two subgroup is normal. For larger $p$, the index alone is not sufficient without the smallest-prime assumption.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:bracelets}
\idea A bracelet identifies rotations \emph{and} reflections of a colouring. Burnside's lemma therefore averages over the twelve symmetries of a regular hexagon. A symmetry with $c$ cycles on the six beads fixes exactly $2^c$ binary colourings.

\writeup The group $D_6$ has six rotations and six reflections. A rotation through $k$ bead positions has $\gcd(6,k)$ cycles, so the rotation contributions to the fixed-colouring count, for $k=0,\ldots,5$, are
\[
2^6,\;2^1,\;2^2,\;2^3,\;2^2,\;2^1,
\]
whose sum is
\[
64+2+4+8+4+2=84.
\]

There are two kinds of reflection because the number of beads is even. Three reflection axes pass through pairs of opposite beads. Such a reflection fixes two individual beads and swaps the other four in two pairs: its cycle type is $(1,1,2,2)$, and it fixes $2^4=16$ colourings.

The other three axes pass through the midpoints of opposite gaps. Each swaps the beads in three pairs, cycle type $(2,2,2)$, and fixes $2^3=8$ colourings. The total reflection contribution is
\[
3\cdot16+3\cdot8=48+24=72.
\]

Burnside's lemma now gives
\[
\boxed{\#\text{bracelets}=\frac{84+72}{12}=13}.
\]
For comparison, applying Burnside to the rotations alone gives $84/6=14$ necklaces. Thus precisely one pair of rotational orbits becomes identified by reflections.

One representative of this pair, read cyclically, is
\[
\bullet\,\bullet\,\circ\,\bullet\,\circ\,\circ.
\]
Its mirror image is represented by
\[
\bullet\,\circ\,\bullet\,\bullet\,\circ\,\circ.
\]
A cyclic shift cannot turn the first six-symbol word into the second (check all six starting positions), but a reversal followed by a suitable rotation does. Those are the two chiral necklaces marked in Figure~\ref{fig:necklaces}. All other necklaces are equivalent to their own reflections.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:graphs4}
\idea A simple graph on four labelled vertices assigns one of two colours, absent or present, to each of the six unordered vertex pairs. Graph isomorphism is exactly the action of $\mathfrak S_4$ on these pairs. Find the induced cycle type for each conjugacy class, then use P\'olya's average.

\writeup Label the vertices $1,2,3,4$. The six potential edges are the two-element subsets. Each vertex permutation induces a permutation of that six-element edge set. The five possible vertex cycle types give:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{vertex type}&\text{number}&\text{induced edge-cycle type}\\\hline
1^4&1&1^6\\
2\,1^2&6&1^2 2^2\\
2^2&3&1^2 2^2\\
3\,1&8&3^2\\
4&6&2\,4
\end{array}
\]
For example, $(1\,2)$ fixes edges $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ and exchanges $\{1,3\}$ with $\{2,3\}$ and $\{1,4\}$ with $\{2,4\}$. A four-cycle rotates the four polygon sides and swaps the diagonals.

The cycle-index polynomial of this action is therefore
\[
Z=\frac1{24}\bigl(t_1^6+9t_1^2t_2^2+8t_3^2+6t_2t_4\bigr).
\]
Each edge-cycle must have one colour, so substituting $t_j=2$ counts unlabelled graphs:
\[
\boxed{\frac{2^6+9\cdot2^4+8\cdot2^2+6\cdot2^2}{24}
=\frac{64+144+32+24}{24}=11}.
\]

To count graphs with exactly three edges, weight an absent edge by $1$ and a present edge by $y$. A cycle of $j$ edges can be all absent or all present, giving weight $1+y^j$. Substitute
\[
t_j=1+y^j
\]
in the same cycle index:
\[
\frac1{24}\left((1+y)^6+9(1+y)^2(1+y^2)^2
 +8(1+y^3)^2+6(1+y^2)(1+y^4)\right).
\]
The coefficient of $y^3$ is
\[
\frac1{24}\left(\binom63+9\cdot4+8\cdot2+0\right)
=\frac{20+36+16}{24}=\boxed{3}.
\]
The three isomorphism types are a triangle plus an isolated vertex; a path of three edges; and a star with three leaves. They can be distinguished by their vertex-degree sequences, respectively $(2,2,2,0)$, $(2,2,1,1)$ and $(3,1,1,1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:commuting}
\idea Commuting means being fixed under conjugation. Burnside's lemma therefore counts commuting ordered pairs from the number of conjugacy classes. To bound that number, separate the centre from the non-central classes.

\writeup Let $n=|G|$ and let $k(G)$ be the number of conjugacy classes of $G$. For each $g\in G$, the elements that commute with $g$ form its centraliser
\[
C_G(g)=\{x\in G:gx=xg\}.
\]
The total number of commuting ordered pairs is
\[
\sum_{g\in G}|C_G(g)|.
\]
Conjugation defines an action of $G$ on its own underlying set. Its fixed-point set under $g$ is exactly $C_G(g)$. Burnside's lemma says
\[
k(G)=\frac1n\sum_{g\in G}|C_G(g)|.
\]
Thus there are $nk(G)$ commuting pairs out of $n^2$ equally likely ordered pairs, and
\[
\boxed{\mathbb P(XY=YX)=\frac{k(G)}n}.
\]

Now assume $G$ is non-abelian and put $z=|Z(G)|$. The quotient $G/Z(G)$ cannot be cyclic by Exercise~\ref{ex:centre}, because a cyclic quotient by the centre would make $G$ abelian. Its order, which is the index $n/z$, is therefore neither $1$ nor a prime, since groups of prime order are cyclic. In particular $n/z\ge4$, so $z\le n/4$.

Every central element forms a singleton conjugacy class. Every non-central element has a conjugacy class of size at least $2$. Therefore the number of classes satisfies
\[
k(G)\le z+\frac{n-z}2
=\frac{n+z}2
\le\frac{n+n/4}2=\frac{5n}8.
\]
Divide by $n$:
\[
\boxed{\mathbb P(XY=YX)\le\frac58}
\]
for every non-abelian finite group.

For the dihedral group $D_4$ of order $8$, Exercise~\ref{ex:classesD} lists exactly five conjugacy classes, with sizes $1,1,2,2,2$. Hence its commuting probability is $5/8$. The bound is sharp.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:sieving}
\idea At $q=1$ the $q$-binomial is the ordinary binomial, which counts all subsets. At a root of unity of order $m$, the
factors $1-q^i$ with $m\mid i$ vanish, and only they matter: they behave like $i$, divided by $m$. So the value at $\zeta$
looks like a binomial coefficient ``with everything divided by $m$''. On the other side, a subset fixed by a rotation of
order $m$ is a union of whole orbits of size $m$, and choosing $k/m$ orbits out of $n/m$ is the same binomial.

\writeup (a) $[n]_q=[k]_q+q^k[n-k]_q$. Divide by $[k]_q![n-k]_q!$ and multiply by $[n-1]_q!$:
\[
\qbinom nk=\frac{[n-1]_q!\,[k]_q}{[k]_q![n-k]_q!}+q^k\frac{[n-1]_q!\,[n-k]_q}{[k]_q![n-k]_q!}=\qbinom{n-1}{k-1}+q^k\qbinom{n-1}k.
\]
With $\qbinom n0=\qbinom nn=1$, induction on $n$ shows that $\qbinom nk$ is a polynomial with non-negative integer
coefficients.

(b) Cancel $[n-k]_q!$ and multiply each $[i]_q$ by $1-q$:
$\qbinom nk=\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{1-q^{n-k+i}}{1-q^i}$. The value of the polynomial at $\zeta$ is the limit of this product as
$q\to\zeta$. A factor $1-q^e$ vanishes at $\zeta$ if and only if $m\mid e$.

Suppose $m\mid k$. Then $m\mid n-k$, so $\zeta^{n-k+i}=\zeta^i$: the factors with $m\nmid i$ have the same non-zero limit above
and below, and cancel. The others are $i=mb$, $1\le b\le\frac km$; with $u=q^m\to1$,
\[
\frac{1-q^{n-k+mb}}{1-q^{mb}}=\frac{1-u^{(n-k)/m+b}}{1-u^b}\longrightarrow\frac{(n-k)/m+b}b,
\]
and $\prod_{b=1}^{k/m}\frac{(n-k)/m+b}b=\binom{n/m}{k/m}$.

Suppose $m\nmid k$. In the denominator, $m\mid i$ for $\lfloor k/m\rfloor$ values of $i\in\{1,\dots,k\}$. In the numerator,
$n-k+i$ runs over $\{n-k+1,\dots,n\}$, which contains $\lfloor\frac{k-1}m\rfloor+1$ multiples of $m$ (since $m\mid n$, they are
$n,n-m,\dots$ down to $n-k+1$). As $m\nmid k$, $\lfloor\frac{k-1}m\rfloor=\lfloor\frac km\rfloor$, so the numerator has one
more vanishing factor, of order one each. The limit is $0$.

(c) Let $d=\gcd(j,n)$. The subgroup generated by $g^j$ is generated by $g^d$, the rotation by $d$, whose orbits on
$\Z/n\Z$ are the $d$ classes modulo $d$, each of size $m=n/d$. A subset is fixed by $g^j$ if and only if it is fixed by
$g^d$, that is, it is a union of orbits. There are $\binom d{k/m}$ such subsets if $m\mid k$, and none otherwise. Since
$e^{2i\pi j/n}$ is a primitive $m$-th root of unity, this is the value given by (b).

(d) By Burnside's lemma (Volume~3, Chapter~3), the number of orbits is the average number of fixed points. With $F$ the polynomial $\qbinom nk$, it is
$\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}F(e^{2i\pi j/n})=\frac1n\sum_{m\mid\gcd(n,k)}\varphi(m)\binom{n/m}{k/m}$. For $n=6$, $k=3$:
$\frac16\bigl(20+2\binom21\bigr)=4$.
\tools Volume~3, Chapter~3 (Burnside's lemma); Volume~1, Chapter~6 (roots of unity); Volume~5, Chapter~1 (finite sums).
\leads Reiner, Stanton and White (2004) named this the \emph{cyclic sieving phenomenon}: a polynomial that counts a set at
$q=1$ and counts fixed points of a cyclic action at roots of unity. Dozens of instances are known, for triangulations of
polygons, plane partitions and Young tableaux, and each one comes from representation theory.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:patterns}
\idea Treat moving points and renaming colours as one action of $G\times\mathfrak S(K)$. The fixed-colouring count becomes easy once we look separately at each cycle of the point symmetry.

\writeup \emph{(a) Follow one point around a cycle.}
The pair $(g,s)$ acts on a colouring $f:X\to K$ by
\[
(g,s)\cdot f=s\circ f\circ g^{-1}.
\]
The two actions commute, so this is a genuine action of the direct product. A colouring is fixed exactly when
\[
f(gx)=s(f(x))\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.
\]
Now choose a cycle $x,gx,\ldots,g^{\ell-1}x$ of length $\ell$. Once $c=f(x)$ is chosen, the equation forces $f(g^tx)=s^t(c)$. After one full turn, consistency requires $s^\ell(c)=c$.

If $c$ belongs to a cycle of $s$ of length $j$, this holds exactly when $j\mid\ell$. There are $jc_j(s)$ colours in the $j$-cycles of $s$. Thus one cycle of $g$ permits
\[
\sum_{j\mid\ell}j\,c_j(s)
\]
choices. Choices for distinct cycles of $g$ are independent. Multiplying gives the required product over its cycles.

\emph{(b) Two colours on a necklace.}
For a rotation by $r$ positions of an $n$-bead necklace, let $d=\gcd(n,r)$ and $\ell=n/d$. The rotation has $d$ cycles, each of length $\ell$. If colours cannot be exchanged, every cycle gets either colour, so there are $2^d$ fixed strings.

If the two colours are exchanged as well, travelling once around a cycle alternates them. The assignment is consistent only if $\ell$ is even, when there are two choices of starting colour for each cycle; the count is then $2^d$. If $\ell$ is odd, the count is zero. Burnside's lemma for the $2n$ combined transformations therefore gives
\[
\boxed{\frac1{2n}\left(
\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}2^{\gcd(n,r)}+
\sum_{\substack{0\le r<n\\n/\gcd(n,r)\text{ even}}}
2^{\gcd(n,r)}\right).}
\]
For $n=6$, the fixed strings under the six rotations number
$64,2,4,8,4,2$, a total of $84$. The colour exchange can accompany just the rotations $r=1,3,5$, contributing $2,8,2$, a total of $12$. The number of equivalence classes is
\[
\frac{84+12}{12}=\boxed8.
\]

\emph{(c) Paint the six faces of a cube with unnamed colours.}
We may use a palette $K$ of exactly three named colours and allow some colours not to occur. Identifying palettes by $\mathfrak S_3$ gives precisely the partitions of the six faces into at most three colour classes. The rotation group has $24$ elements and the combined group has $24\cdot6=144$.

The rotations have these cycle types on faces: one identity $1^6$, six quarter-turns $1^24^1$, three face-axis half-turns $1^22^2$, eight vertex-axis turns $3^2$, and six edge-axis half-turns $2^3$.

For the identity renaming of colours, a rotation with $c$ face-cycles permits $3^c$ colourings. Summing over the five types gives
\[
3^6+6\cdot3^3+3\cdot3^4+8\cdot3^2+6\cdot3^3=1368.
\]
For any of the three transpositions of colours, a face-cycle of odd length has one available colour (the fixed colour); a face-cycle of even length has three. The corresponding sum is
\[
1+6\cdot3+3\cdot3^2+8\cdot1+6\cdot3^3=216.
\]
For a three-cycle of colours, a face-cycle has three choices if its length is divisible by $3$, and none otherwise. Only the eight vertex rotations, each of type $3^2$, contribute, giving $8\cdot3^2=72$ for each of the two three-cycles.

Burnside's average is
\[
\boxed{\frac{1368+3\cdot216+2\cdot72}{144}=15}.
\]
Both calculations count palettes with unused colours correctly, since the action renames the whole three-colour palette.

\tools Burnside's lemma and the rotational symmetry classes of the cube (Chapter~3).

\leads The same cycle-by-cycle method works whenever labels on a set can be renamed. It explains why symmetry of the positions and symmetry of the palette have to be counted together.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:landau}
\idea The class equation divided by $|G|$ is an Egyptian fraction: $1=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac1{c_i}$, $c_i$ the order of a
centraliser. The largest $c_i$ is $|G|$ itself (the centraliser of $e$). With $k$ terms one cannot reach far: the smallest
denominator is at most $k$, and each next one is bounded by the previous ones.

\writeup (a) Let $x_1,\dots,x_k$ represent the classes. The class of $x_i$ has $\frac{|G|}{c_i}$ elements with
$c_i=|C_G(x_i)|$ (orbit--stabiliser for conjugation), so $1=\sum_i\frac1{c_i}$, and $c_i=|G|$ for the class of $e$. Order
$c_1\le c_2\le\dots\le c_k$; then $c_k=|G|$. Let $r_i=1-\sum_{j\le i}\frac1{c_j}$. For $0\le i<k$, $r_i=\sum_{j>i}\frac1{c_j}\le\frac{k-i}{c_{i+1}}$,
and $r_i>0$ is a fraction with denominator dividing $c_1\cdots c_i$, so $r_i\ge\frac1{c_1\cdots c_i}$. Hence
$c_{i+1}\le(k-i)c_1\cdots c_i\le k\,c_1\cdots c_i$. By induction $c_i\le k^{2^{i-1}}$: true for $i=1$ ($c_1\le k$), and then
$c_{i+1}\le k\cdot k^{1+2+\dots+2^{i-1}}=k^{2^i}$. So $|G|=c_k\le k^{2^{k-1}}$. There are finitely many groups of each order,
hence finitely many with $k$ classes.

(b) $k=1$: $G=\{e\}$. $k=2$: $1=\frac1{c_1}+\frac1{c_2}$ with $c_1\le c_2$ forces $c_1=c_2=2$, so $|G|=2$ and $G=\Z/2\Z$. $k=3$: the
solutions of $1=\frac1{c_1}+\frac1{c_2}+\frac1{c_3}$, $c_1\le c_2\le c_3$, are $(3,3,3)$, $(2,4,4)$, $(2,3,6)$, so $|G|\in\{3,4,6\}$.
Order $3$: $\Z/3\Z$, which has $3$ classes. Order $4$: abelian, so $4$ classes, impossible. Order $6$: $\Z/6\Z$ has $6$ classes,
and $\mathfrak S_3$ has $3$. So the answer is $\{e\}$, $\Z/2\Z$, $\Z/3\Z$ and $\mathfrak S_3$.
\tools Volume~3, Chapter~3 (class equation); Volume~2, Chapter~1 (fractions).
\leads This is Landau's theorem (1903). The sharp bound for the largest denominator of an Egyptian fraction with $k$ terms is
$s_{k-1}-1$, where $s_0=2$, $s_1=3$, $7$, $43$, \dots{} is Sylvester's sequence of Problem~\ref{pb:sylvester} (Curtiss, 1922); it gives
$|G|\le s_{k-1}-1$, which is doubly exponential too. The groups with few classes have been classified, with the help of computers, up
to about a dozen classes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:nilpotent}
\idea Every matrix splits uniquely into a nilpotent part and an invertible part on complementary subspaces. Counting matrices
this way gives one identity per $n$, and these identities determine all the $N_k$, so it is enough to check that $q^{k^2-k}$
satisfies them.

\writeup (a) The kernels $\ker A^k$ increase and the images $\operatorname{im}A^k$ decrease, and both stabilise by $k=n$ (the
dimension cannot change more than $n$ times). If $x\in\ker A^n\cap\operatorname{im}A^n$, write $x=A^ny$; then $A^{2n}y=0$, so
$y\in\ker A^{2n}=\ker A^n$ and $x=0$. By rank--nullity the dimensions add up to $n$, so $V=\ker A^n\oplus\operatorname{im}A^n$.
Both are $A$-stable. $A$ is nilpotent on $\ker A^n$, and on $\operatorname{im}A^n$ it is onto
($A(\operatorname{im}A^n)=\operatorname{im}A^{n+1}=\operatorname{im}A^n$), hence invertible. If $V=U\oplus W$ is another such
decomposition, then $A^n=0$ on $U$, so $U\subset\ker A^n$, and $W=A^nW\subset\operatorname{im}A^n$; the dimensions force equality.

(b) By (a), $A\mapsto(U,W,A|_U,A|_W)$ is a bijection from $M_n(\mathbb F_q)$ to the set of quadruples where $V=U\oplus W$, $A|_U$
is nilpotent on $U$ and $A|_W$ is invertible on $W$. $GL_n$ acts transitively on the ordered pairs $(U,W)$ with
$\dim U=k$, $V=U\oplus W$ (send a basis adapted to one pair to a basis adapted to another), with stabiliser $GL(U)\times GL(W)$.
So there are $\frac{|GL_n|}{|GL_k||GL_{n-k}|}$ such pairs, and
\[
q^{n^2}=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{|GL_n|}{|GL_k|\,|GL_{n-k}|}\,N_k\,|GL_{n-k}|=|GL_n|\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{N_k}{|GL_k|}.
\]

(c) The identity of (b) for $n$ determines $N_n$ from $N_0,\dots,N_{n-1}$, so it suffices to show that $N_k=q^{k^2-k}$ satisfies
it. Write $|GL_k|=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(q^k-q^i)=q^{\binom k2}D_k$ with $D_k=\prod_{i=1}^k(q^i-1)$. Then
$\frac{q^{k^2-k}}{|GL_k|}=\frac{q^{\binom k2}}{D_k}$ and $\frac{q^{n^2}}{|GL_n|}=\frac{q^{\binom{n+1}2}}{D_n}$, so we need
\[
\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{q^{\binom k2}}{D_k}=\frac{q^{\binom{n+1}2}}{D_n}.
\]
For $n=0$ both sides are $1$. If it holds for $n$, adding the term $k=n+1$ gives
$\frac{q^{\binom{n+1}2}}{D_n}+\frac{q^{\binom{n+1}2}}{D_{n+1}}=\frac{q^{\binom{n+1}2}(q^{n+1}-1+1)}{D_{n+1}}=\frac{q^{\binom{n+2}2}}{D_{n+1}}$.

(d) $A\mapsto I+A$ is a bijection from nilpotent to unipotent matrices, so there are $q^{n^2-n}$ unipotent matrices.

\tools Volume~1, Chapter~8 (rank--nullity, invariant subspaces); Volume~3, Chapter~3 (orbit--stabiliser); counting independent vectors for the
order of $GL_n(\mathbb F_q)$.
\leads $q^{n^2-n}=q^{2\binom n2}$ is the square of the order of a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $GL_n$ (the unitriangular matrices).
Steinberg proved that in every finite group of Lie type the number of unipotent elements is the square of the order of a Sylow
$p$-subgroup. Over a general field the nilpotent matrices form the \emph{nilpotent cone}, of dimension $n^2-n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:flags}
\idea A flag chooses a line, then a flag in the quotient by that line. Complements look different: after choosing one of them, every other complement is the graph of a linear map.

\writeup \emph{(a) Count the flags.} There are $q^n-1$ non-zero vectors in $V=\mathbb F_q^n$. Every line has $q-1$ non-zero vectors, and different lines meet only at $0$. Thus there are $(q^n-1)/(q-1)=[n]_q$ choices for $V_1$. Once $V_1$ is chosen, subspaces containing it correspond to subspaces of the $(n-1)$-dimensional quotient $V/V_1$. If $F_n$ is the number of complete flags, then
\[
F_n=[n]_q F_{n-1},\qquad F_0=1.
\]
Induction gives $\boxed{F_n=[n]_q!}$.

\emph{(b) Count the complements.} Let $\dim U=k$ and choose a complement $W_0$ by extending a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$. Write $\pi:V\to W_0$ for projection along $U$.

Suppose $W$ is another complement. The restriction $\pi|_W:W\to W_0$ is injective, since its kernel is $W\cap U=\{0\}$, and both spaces have dimension $n-k$. So it is an isomorphism. Each $w\in W_0$ has a unique lift in $W$, of the form $w+f(w)$ for a linear map $f:W_0\to U$. Conversely, every such map gives a complement
\[
W_f=\{w+f(w):w\in W_0\}.
\]
Distinct maps give distinct graphs. A linear map is determined by its values on $n-k$ basis vectors, each with $q^k$ possibilities. Hence
\[
\boxed{\#\{\text{complements of }U\}=q^{k(n-k)}.}
\]

\emph{(c) Apply orbit--stabiliser.} The group $GL_n(\mathbb F_q)$ acts transitively on complete flags: choose a basis adapted to each flag and send one basis to the other. A matrix preserving the standard flag must be upper triangular, with $q-1$ choices for each of the $n$ diagonal entries and $q$ choices for each of the $\binom n2$ entries above the diagonal. This stabiliser has size $(q-1)^nq^{\binom n2}$. Since there are $[n]_q!$ flags,
\[
\boxed{|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|=[n]_q!\,(q-1)^nq^{\binom n2}.}
\]
For $n=2$, the formula becomes $(q+1)(q-1)^2q=(q^2-1)(q^2-q)$, agreeing with Chapter~1.

\emph{(d) The formal substitution $q=1$.} The expressions $[j]_q=1+\cdots+q^{j-1}$ and $q^{k(n-k)}$ are polynomials in $q$, and at $q=1$ they become $j$ and $1$. A complete flag of subsets of an $n$-element set is an ordering of its elements, so there are $n!$ such flags. Each subset has exactly one set-theoretic complement. Those are the specialisations of (a) and (b).

There is a subtlety in (c). Direct substitution into $|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|$ gives zero because of the factor $(q-1)^n$. Remove that factor first:
\[
\frac{|GL_n(\mathbb F_q)|}{(q-1)^n}=q^{\binom n2}[n]_q!
\quad\longrightarrow\quad n!\quad(q\to1).
\]
This is why the symmetric group is sometimes compared to a general linear group over a hypothetical field with one element. The analogy concerns counting and geometry; it does not assert that an ordinary one-element field exists.

\tools Linear algebra (Volume~1); quotients and orbit--stabiliser (this volume).

\leads The powers of $q$ count choices of vectors and linear maps. Evaluating their counting polynomials at $1$ reveals the simpler problem of choosing elements of a set.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:touchard}
\idea Rotate the last $p$ labels of a partition. Most partitions then travel in groups of $p$. Only the unchanged partitions matter modulo $p$, and those have a simple description.

\writeup Let $A=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $T=\{n+1,\ldots,n+p\}$. Write $\sigma$ for the $p$-cycle on $T$ that fixes every element of $A$. It acts on set partitions of $A\cup T$ by moving every element in every block.

Because $p$ is prime, every orbit of the cyclic group $\langle\sigma\rangle$ on partitions has size either $1$ or $p$. Orbits of size $p$ contribute zero to the count modulo $p$. Thus
\[
B_{n+p}\equiv
\#\{\text{partitions fixed by }\sigma\}\pmod p.
\]

\emph{What does an unchanged partition look like?}
Suppose a block $S$ of such a partition meets $T$. If $\sigma(S)=S$, the transitivity of the $p$-cycle on $T$ means that $S$ contains all of $T$.

Otherwise $S,\sigma(S),\ldots,\sigma^{p-1}(S)$ are $p$ distinct blocks, so they are pairwise disjoint. All their intersections with $A$ are equal, because $\sigma$ fixes $A$ pointwise. Those intersections must therefore be empty. Each of the $p$ disjoint blocks contains at least one member of the $p$-element set $T$, and so each is a singleton. In particular every element of $T$ is a singleton.

This leaves exactly two possibilities:
\begin{itemize}
\item All members of $T$ occur together in one block, possibly with some old labels. Replacing $T$ by one new symbol $\ast$ gives a bijection with partitions of $A\cup\{\ast\}$: there are $B_{n+1}$ choices.
\item Every member of $T$ is a singleton. Removing those singleton blocks gives a partition of $A$: there are $B_n$ choices.
\end{itemize}
The cases are disjoint because $p\ge2$, and every partition described is indeed unchanged by $\sigma$. Therefore
\[
\boxed{B_{n+p}\equiv B_{n+1}+B_n\pmod p.}
\]

\emph{A small check.} For $p=2$ and $n=1$, the formula reads
$B_3\equiv B_2+B_1\pmod2$. Indeed $5\equiv2+1\equiv1\pmod2$.

We can also iterate the result carefully. Replacing $n$ by $n+p^j-p$ and applying the congruence repeatedly yields, for $m\ge1$,
\[
B_{n+p^m}\equiv mB_n+B_{n+1}\pmod p.
\]
One way to see this is to apply the shift operator $E$ defined by $E(B_n)=B_{n+1}$ to the congruence $E^p=E+1$ modulo $p$. In characteristic $p$, $(E+1)^{p}=E^p+1$, and induction gives $E^{p^m}=E+m$. The displayed identity follows.

\tools Group actions and orbit sizes, the Bell numbers, and the binomial identity in characteristic $p$.

\leads Here the group does not count every partition directly. It separates a difficult set into orbits whose sizes vanish modulo $p$, leaving only two families of fixed partitions to count.
\end{solution}
