\chapter{A Glimpse Beyond: Quaternions and Rotations}

\begin{center}\itshape
Hold a plate flat on your palm and turn it one full turn, keeping it horizontal: your arm is twisted.\\
Keep turning in the same direction: after a second full turn, your arm is straight again.\\
Why two turns, and not one?
\end{center}

Take two quarter-turns of an object in space, one about the horizontal axis and one about the vertical axis. Reverse their order. The final orientation may change. Three-dimensional rotation is not commutative, and any algebra meant to describe it must remember that.

Complex multiplication describes rotations in a plane. To describe rotations in space, we need four real coordinates rather than two. The \emph{quaternions} are numbers of the form
\[
q=a+bi+cj+dk,\qquad i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1,
\]
with multiplication that satisfies $ij=k$ but $ji=-k$. Their lack of commutativity is not a defect. It is what makes the algebra fit the geometry.

A vector in $\R^3$ can be written as the imaginary quaternion $v=xi+yj+zk$. If $q$ is a unit quaternion, the map $v\mapsto qvq^{-1}$ is a rotation. The same rotation comes from $-q$. This is the double cover of the rotation group by the unit quaternions.

The groups we studied earlier return in a visible form. The rotations of the tetrahedron form $A_4$; those of the cube form $\mathfrak S_4$; and those of the icosahedron form $A_5$. In fact every finite subgroup of the three-dimensional rotation group belongs to one of five families.

Before closing, we will also multiply quaternion norms and recover the four-square identity. It is a good final example of how a useful algebraic rule can make a numerical identity seem inevitable.

\section{Quaternions}

Instead of postulating Hamilton's rules and checking associativity by hand, we realise quaternions as complex matrices, for which associativity is automatic.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathbb H$ be the set of complex matrices
\[
\begin{pmatrix}z&w\\-\bar w&\bar z\end{pmatrix},\qquad z,w\in\C.
\]
It is a real vector space of dimension $4$, with basis $1=I$ and
\[
i=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},\quad j=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad k=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix};
\]
so $q=a+bi+cj+dk$ corresponds to $z=a+bi$, $w=c+di$ (here $i$ on the right is the complex number). Its elements are \emph{quaternions}. The \emph{conjugate} of $q$ is $\bar q=a-bi-cj-dk$, its \emph{norm} is $N(q)=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2$, its \emph{real part} is $a$, and $q$ is \emph{pure} if $a=0$; the pure quaternions form a copy of $\R^3$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:quat}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\mathbb H$ is closed under matrix multiplication; it is an associative ring, and
\[
\begin{gathered}i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,\qq ij=-ji=k,\quad jk=-kj=i,\qq ki=-ik=j.\end{gathered}
\]
\item $\bar q$ is the conjugate transpose of the matrix $q$, so $\overline{pq}=\bar q\,\bar p$; and $q\bar q=\bar qq=N(q)=\det q$. Hence $N(pq)=N(p)N(q)$.
\item Every $q\ne0$ has an inverse $q^{-1}=\bar q/N(q)$: $\mathbb H$ is a \emph{division ring} (a non-commutative field).
\item For pure quaternions $u,v$, identified with vectors of $\R^3$, $uv=-u\cdot v+u\times v$. In particular $u^2=-|u|^2$, and $u,v$ anticommute iff they are orthogonal.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
We use the concrete matrix model
\[
q=\begin{pmatrix}z&w\\-\bar w&\bar z\end{pmatrix},
\qquad z=a+bi,\quad w=c+di.
\]
It makes associativity and the norm
visible without assuming quaternion
multiplication is commutative.

\emph{(a) Closure and multiplication rules.}
Multiply two such matrices:
\[
\begin{pmatrix}z&w\\-\bar w&\bar z\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}z'&w'\\-\bar w'&\bar z'\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
zz'-w\bar w'&zw'+w\bar z'\\
-\bar wz'-\bar z\bar w'&
-\bar ww'+\bar z\bar z'
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Writing the upper entries as $Z,W$,
the lower entries are precisely
$-\bar W,\bar Z$; hence the product
belongs to $\mathbb H$.
Matrix multiplication is associative
and distributive, so these properties
pass to the set of quaternion matrices.
Direct multiplication of the
three indicated basis matrices gives
\[
i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,\quad
ij=k=-ji,\quad jk=i=-kj,\quad
ki=j=-ik.
\]
For example $ij=k$ while $ji=-k$:
the ring is genuinely non-commutative.

\emph{(b) Conjugation and norm.}
The conjugate transpose of $q$ is
\[
q^*=\begin{pmatrix}
\bar z&-w\\\bar w&z
\end{pmatrix},
\]
which represents $a-bi-cj-dk=\bar q$.
The identity $(pq)^*=q^*p^*$
for matrices therefore gives
$\overline{pq}=\bar q\,\bar p$.
Multiplication shows that
\[
q\bar q=\bar qq=
(|z|^2+|w|^2)I
=(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)I.
\]
Also $\det q=|z|^2+|w|^2=N(q)$.
Taking determinants of a product
then yields
\[
N(pq)=\det(pq)=\det p\,\det q=N(p)N(q).
\]

\emph{(c) Inverses.}
If $q\ne0$, at least one of its
four real coordinates is non-zero,
so $N(q)>0$. The identities in
(b) imply
\[
q\frac{\bar q}{N(q)}
=\frac{\bar q}{N(q)}q=1.
\]
Thus every non-zero quaternion
is invertible, with
$\boxed{q^{-1}=\bar q/N(q)}$.

\emph{(d) Pure quaternions as vectors.}
Both the quaternion product and
the expressions $-u\cdot v$ and
$u\times v$ are bilinear in the
coordinates of $u,v\in\R^3$.
It is therefore enough to check
the identity on pairs from the
basis $i,j,k$.
For equal basis vectors,
$i^2=j^2=k^2=-1$ agrees with
minus the dot product and
zero cross product.
For different basis vectors,
$ij=k=i\times j$ and
$ji=-k=j\times i$, with the
same cyclic identities for
$j,k$ and $k,i$.
Bilinearity gives
\[
\boxed{uv=-u\cdot v+u\times v}.
\]
Putting $u=v$ gives $u^2=-|u|^2$.
Also
\[
uv+vu=-2u\cdot v
\]
because the cross product is
antisymmetric. Thus $uv=-vu$
if and only if $u\cdot v=0$,
which is orthogonality.
\end{proof}

Taking norms in $(a+bi+cj+dk)(a'+b'i+c'j+d'k)$ gives Euler's four-square identity (Exercise~\ref{ex:euler4}), used in Volume~2 to reduce the four-square theorem to primes. Frobenius proved in 1878 that $\R$, $\C$ and $\mathbb H$ are the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over $\R$; dropping associativity adds Cayley's octonions, of dimension $8$, and nothing else.

A non-zero quaternion has an inverse, and its norm behaves multiplicatively. For a quaternion of norm one, conjugation preserves the lengths of imaginary quaternions. We can now ask which rotation of space this conjugation describes.

\section{Unit quaternions and rotations}

The unit quaternions form a group $S^3=\{q:N(q)=1\}$ under multiplication (by Proposition~\ref{prop:quat}(b)), geometrically the unit sphere of $\R^4$. As matrices they are exactly $SU(2)$, the unitary $2\times2$ matrices of determinant $1$: such a matrix $\begin{psmallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\gamma&\delta\end{psmallmatrix}$ has inverse $\begin{psmallmatrix}\delta&-\beta\\-\gamma&\alpha\end{psmallmatrix}$ (determinant $1$) equal to its conjugate transpose, so $\delta=\bar\alpha$ and $\gamma=-\bar\beta$.

\begin{lemma}[Euler]\label{lem:euler}
Every $A\in SO(3)$ is a rotation about some axis: there are a unit vector $u$ and an angle $\theta$ such that $Au=u$ and $A$ turns the plane $u^\perp$ by $\theta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We first show that a three-dimensional orientation-preserving
orthogonal map fixes some non-zero vector. Then we examine what it does
to the perpendicular plane.

\emph{Find a fixed axis.}
Since $A$ is orthogonal, $AA^{\mathsf T}=I$; since
$A\in SO(3)$, $\det A=1$. Calculate
\[
\begin{aligned}
\det(A-I)
 &=\det(A-AA^{\mathsf T})\\
 &=\det(A)\det(I-A^{\mathsf T})\\
 &=\det(I-A^{\mathsf T})\\
 &=\det(I-A)\\
 &=-\det(A-I).
\end{aligned}
\]
The fourth equality uses invariance of the determinant under
transpose. The last uses that $I-A=-(A-I)$ and a $3\times3$
determinant changes sign when we negate all three rows.
Thus $\det(A-I)=0$, so $A-I$ has a non-zero kernel.
Choose a vector in that kernel and divide by its length to obtain
a unit vector $u$ with $Au=u$.

\emph{Look across the axis.}
If $v$ is perpendicular to $u$, orthogonality of $A$ gives
\[
(Av)\cdot u=(Av)\cdot(Au)=v\cdot u=0.
\]
Therefore $A$ maps the two-dimensional plane $u^\perp$ to itself.
Choose an orthonormal basis of this plane and append $u$.
In that basis, the matrix of $A$ has the form
\[
\begin{pmatrix}B&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad B^{\mathsf T}B=I_2,\quad \det B=\det A=1.
\]
Every real $2\times2$ orthogonal matrix of determinant $1$ has
the form
\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
\]
for some angle $\theta$. Hence $A$ fixes the axis $\mathbb Ru$
and rotates its perpendicular plane by $\theta$.

When $A=I$, any unit vector may serve as the axis and
$\theta=0$. For a non-identity rotation the axis consists of the
fixed vectors, except that reversing $u$ reverses the sign convention
for the angle. This is the geometric description used below.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:su2so3}
For a unit quaternion $q$ and a pure quaternion $v$, $R_q(v)=qvq^{-1}=qv\bar q$ is pure, and $R_q\in SO(3)$. The map $q\mapsto R_q$ is a surjective homomorphism $S^3\to SO(3)$ with kernel $\{\pm1\}$, so that
\[
SO(3)\cong S^3/\{\pm1\}=SU(2)/\{\pm I\}.
\]
Explicitly, if $u$ is a unit pure quaternion and $q=\cos\frac\theta2+\sin\frac\theta2\,u$, then $R_q$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about $u$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First, conjugation preserves space and length.} A quaternion $v$ is pure exactly when $\bar v=-v$. If $v$ is pure and $q$ is a unit quaternion, then
\[
\overline{qv\bar q}=q\bar v\bar q=-qv\bar q.
\]
Thus $qv\bar q$ is pure as well. The map $R_q:v\mapsto qv\bar q$ is real-linear on the pure quaternions, which we identify with $\R^3$. The multiplicativity of the quaternion norm gives
\[
N(R_q(v))=N(q)N(v)N(\bar q)=N(v).
\]
Therefore $R_q$ is orthogonal. Moreover
\[
R_p(R_q(v))=p(qv\bar q)\bar p=(pq)v\overline{pq}=R_{pq}(v),
\]
so $q\mapsto R_q$ is a group homomorphism.

\emph{Find the rotation explicitly.} Write
\[
q=c+su,\qquad c=\cos(\theta/2),\quad s=\sin(\theta/2),
\]
where $u$ is a pure unit quaternion. Since $u$ commutes with $q$, we have $R_q(u)=u$. Now take a pure quaternion $w$ perpendicular to $u$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:quat}(d), $uw=u\times w=-wu$, and
\[
(u\times w)u=(u\times w)\times u=w
\]
because $u$ has unit length and is perpendicular to $w$. Expand:
\[
\begin{aligned}
qw\bar q
&=(cw+s\,u\times w)(c-su)\\
&=c^2w-cs\,wu+cs\,u\times w-s^2(u\times w)u\\
&=(c^2-s^2)w+2cs\,u\times w\\
&=\cos\theta\,w+\sin\theta\,u\times w.
\end{aligned}
\]
On the plane perpendicular to $u$, this is rotation through $\theta$, with its orientation fixed by $u$. The vector $u$ stays fixed. Hence $R_q\in SO(3)$.

\emph{Every rotation occurs.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:euler}, every element of $SO(3)$ is a rotation about some unit axis $u$ through some angle $\theta$. The formula above shows that it is $R_q$ for $q=\cos(\theta/2)+u\sin(\theta/2)$. So the map is onto.

\emph{Exactly two quaternions give each rotation.} If $R_q$ is the identity, then $q$ commutes with every pure quaternion. Write $q=a+x$, with $x$ pure. For every pure $w$,
\[
0=qw-wq=xw-wx=2x\times w.
\]
Only $x=0$ has zero cross product with \emph{every} $w$. Thus $q=a$ is real, and $N(q)=a^2=1$ gives $q=\pm1$. Conversely both $1$ and $-1$ act trivially. The kernel is therefore $\{1,-1\}$, and every rotation has exactly two preimages.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
A unit quaternion $q$ preserves the quaternion norm under conjugation, because $N(qvq^{-1})=N(v)$. It also preserves the imaginary quaternions and therefore gives an orthogonal map of $\R^3$. The map depends continuously on $q$ and sends $1$ to the identity, so its determinant is $+1$, not $-1$: it is a rotation. A quaternion of the form $\cos(\theta/2)+u\sin(\theta/2)$ produces rotation through angle $\theta$ around the unit vector $u$. Both $q$ and $-q$ produce the same map; there are no other duplicates. The \emph{half-angle} is what makes the double cover work.

Composing rotations is multiplying quaternions (Exercise~\ref{ex:compose}): four numbers instead of nine, no drift away from orthogonality that cannot be cured by dividing by the norm, and smooth interpolation between orientations. This is why flight software, robots and video games store orientations as unit quaternions.

\begin{remark}[the plate trick]
Turning the plate by an angle $2\pi t$, $0\le t\le1$, about the vertical axis $k$ is the path $q(t)=\cos\pi t+\sin\pi t\,k$ in $S^3$, which goes from $1$ to $-1$: it does not close up, although the rotations $R_{q(t)}$ do. Two turns give $\cos2\pi t+\sin2\pi t\,k$, a closed loop on the $3$-sphere, which can be shrunk to a point on the sphere; its shrinking, projected to $SO(3)$, is the motion that untwists your arm. In topological language $S^3$ is simply connected and $SO(3)$ is not: its fundamental group is $\Z/2\Z$. In physics, electrons are described by vectors on which $S^3=SU(2)$, not $SO(3)$, acts, and a rotation by $2\pi$ multiplies them by $-1$: they have spin $\frac12$.
\end{remark}

Unit quaternions describe every rotation continuously. We now restrict attention to finite sets of rotations that are closed under composition. Their axes and angles can be counted, and the count leads back to the cyclic, dihedral and alternating groups.

\section{Finite groups of rotations}

For each regular polyhedron (Figure~\ref{fig:solids}), the rotations preserving it form a finite subgroup of $SO(3)$. A rotation is determined by the image of a vertex and of an adjacent vertex (a rotation fixing a vertex $x$ and an adjacent vertex $y$ fixes the two independent vectors $x,y$, hence their cross product, hence everything). For the icosahedron, a vertex can go to any of the $12$ vertices, and then its $5$ neighbours can be rotated in $5$ ways: $60$ rotations. In the same way the tetrahedron has $4\cdot3=12$ rotations, the cube and the octahedron $24$, the dodecahedron and the icosahedron $60$. The orbit--stabiliser theorem is behind each count.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finiteso3}
A finite subgroup $G$ of $SO(3)$ is cyclic, or dihedral of order $2n\ge4$, or isomorphic to $A_4$, $\mathfrak S_4$ or $A_5$ (of orders $12$, $24$, $60$).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $N=|G|>1$. Each $g\ne1$ in $G$ is a rotation about an axis, which meets the unit sphere in two \emph{poles} $\pm u$, the only unit vectors fixed by $g$. Let $P$ be the set of poles of all non-identity elements. If $p$ is a pole of $g$ and $h\in G$, then $hp$ is a pole of $hgh^{-1}$: so $G$ acts on $P$. The stabiliser $G_p$ consists of the rotations of $G$ about the axis $\R p$; it is a finite group of rotations of the plane $p^\perp$, isomorphic to a finite subgroup of $\{z\in\C:|z|=1\}$, hence cyclic (Theorem~\ref{thm:cyclicmult}), of order $n_p\ge2$.

\emph{Counting.} Count the pairs $(g,p)$ with $g\ne1$ and $p$ a pole of $g$. Each $g\ne1$ has $2$ poles, and each pole $p$ belongs to $n_p-1$ elements $g\ne1$. Group the poles into orbits $O_1,\dots,O_r$; on $O_i$, $n_p=n_i$ is constant (conjugate stabilisers) and $|O_i|=N/n_i$ (orbit--stabiliser). So
\[
\begin{gathered}2(N-1)=\sum_{i=1}^r\frac N{n_i}(n_i-1),\qq\text{i.e.}\quad2-\frac2N=\sum_{i=1}^r\Bigl(1-\frac1{n_i}\Bigr).\end{gathered}
\]
The left side is in $[1,2)$, each term on the right in $[\frac12,1)$, so $r=2$ or $r=3$.

\emph{$r=2$.} Then $\frac2N=\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}$ with $n_i\le N$, so $n_1=n_2=N$: every pole is fixed by all of $G$. There are two of them, $\pm p$, and $G=G_p$ is cyclic.

\emph{$r=3$.} Order $n_1\le n_2\le n_3$. Then $1+\frac2N=\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}+\frac1{n_3}$, so $n_1=2$ (otherwise the right side is $\le1$), and $\frac12+\frac2N=\frac1{n_2}+\frac1{n_3}$. If $n_2\ge4$ the right side is $\le\frac12$; so $n_2\in\{2,3\}$. If $n_2=2$, then $n_3=N/2$. If $n_2=3$, then $\frac1{n_3}=\frac16+\frac2N>\frac16$ gives $n_3\in\{3,4,5\}$ and $N=12,24,60$. Four cases remain: $(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(2,2,n)$ with $N=2n$, and $(2,3,3)$, $(2,3,4)$, $(2,3,5)$.

\emph{$(2,2,n)$, $N=2n$.} If $n=2$, $N=4$ and every element has order $1$ or $2$, so $G\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2=D_2$. Let $n\ge3$. The orbit $O_3$ has $N/n=2$ poles; if $p\in O_3$, then $-p$ (with the same stabiliser) has $n_{-p}=n\ge3$, so $-p\in O_3$, and $O_3=\{\pm p\}$. Let $C=G_p$, cyclic of order $n$ and index $2$. An element $s\notin C$ maps $p$ to $-p$, so it is not a rotation about $\R p$. Its poles have stabiliser of order $2$ (otherwise they would be in $O_3$), so $s$ has order $2$: it is a half-turn about an axis perpendicular to $p$ (it reverses $p$). Conjugating by $s$ maps the rotation by $\alpha$ about $p$ to the rotation by $\alpha$ about $-p$, i.e.\ by $-\alpha$ about $p$: $scs^{-1}=c^{-1}$ for $c\in C$. With $C=\langle c\rangle$ of order $n$ and $G=C\cup sC$, this is the dihedral group $D_n$ (Chapter~1: the same multiplication rules).

\emph{$(2,3,3)$, $N=12$.} The orbit $O_2$ has $4$ poles, and $G$ permutes them. A non-identity rotation fixes only $2$ unit vectors, so only the identity fixes all four: $G$ embeds in $\mathfrak S_4$, as a subgroup of order $12$. A subgroup $H$ of index $2$ in $\mathfrak S_4$ is normal and contains the square of every element ($g^2H=(gH)^2=H$ in $\mathfrak S_4/H\cong\Z/2\Z$); every $3$-cycle is a square (of its inverse), so $H$ contains the $3$-cycles, which generate $A_4$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:gens}); so $H=A_4$ and $G\cong A_4$.

\emph{$(2,3,4)$, $N=24$.} $O_2$ has $8$ poles, with stabilisers of order $3$; it is the only orbit with $n=3$, so it contains $-p$ with $p$: it consists of $4$ lines $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_4$ through $0$, which $G$ permutes. Let $g\ne1$ fix every $\ell_m$. Let $r$ be the rotation of order $3$ about $\ell_1$ in $G$. A rotation by $\pm120^\circ$ maps no line to itself except its axis, so $r$ moves $\ell_2,\ell_3,\ell_4$. If $g$ had one of the $\ell_m$ as its axis, $g$ would be a rotation of order $3$ about it (it is in the stabiliser of a pole of $O_2$), and would move the other three lines: impossible. So no $\ell_m$ is the axis of $g$, and $g$ maps each $\ell_m$ to itself only by reversing it: $g$ is a half-turn and all $\ell_m$ are perpendicular to its axis, hence lie in one plane $\Pi$. But $r$ permutes the $\ell_m$, so it maps $\Pi$, the plane they span, to itself, and therefore also the line $\Pi^\perp$; as $r$ preserves only its axis $\ell_1$, $\Pi^\perp=\ell_1\subseteq\Pi$, a contradiction. So $G$ acts faithfully on $4$ lines and embeds in $\mathfrak S_4$; with $|G|=24$, $G\cong\mathfrak S_4$.

\emph{$(2,3,5)$, $N=60$.} The orbits have $30$, $20$ and $12$ poles, with stabilisers of orders $2$, $3$, $5$. Since an axis carries $2$ poles, with the same stabiliser, $G$ consists of the identity, $15$ half-turns, $10\cdot2=20$ elements of order $3$ and $6\cdot4=24$ of order $5$ (total $60$). First, $G$ is simple. Let $H\trianglelefteq G$, $H\ne\{1\}$, contain $g\ne1$, with pole $p$. As $G_p$ has prime order, $g$ generates it, so $G_p\subseteq H$, and then $G_{hp}=hG_ph^{-1}\subseteq H$ for all $h$. So $H$ contains every element whose poles lie in the orbit of $p$: $15$, $20$ or $24$ elements. Hence $|H|=1+$ a sum of distinct terms among $15,20,24$, and $|H|$ divides $60$; the only possibility is $1+15+20+24=60$ (the others are $16,21,25,36,40,45$). So $H=G$.

Next, the half-turns are all conjugate (their poles form one orbit), so each one, $a$, has a centraliser of order $60/15=4$. By Sylow's theorems (Theorem~\ref{thm:sylow23}), $a$ lies in a subgroup $P$ of order $4$, which is abelian (Corollary~\ref{cor:pgroup}), so $P\subseteq C_G(a)$ and $P=C_G(a)$. Elements of $P\setminus\{1\}$ have order $2$ ($G$ has no element of order $4$). So the Sylow $2$-subgroups each contain $3$ half-turns, each half-turn lies in exactly one of them, and there are $15/3=5$ of them. $G$ acts on these $5$ subgroups by conjugation, transitively (Sylow), so the homomorphism $G\to\mathfrak S_5$ is not trivial; its kernel is a normal subgroup other than $G$, hence trivial. So $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of index $2$ of $\mathfrak S_5$, which is $A_5$ by the argument used for $\mathfrak S_4$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why this works.}
Every non-identity rotation fixes exactly two points of the unit sphere, the poles on its axis. Counting pairs (rotation, fixed pole) first by rotations gives $2(|G|-1)$. Counting first by pole orbits gives
\[
2-\frac2{|G|}=\sum_i\left(1-\frac1{n_i}\right),
\]
where $n_i$ is the order of a pole stabiliser. That short equation permits only two or three pole orbits. With three, the stabiliser orders must be $(2,2,n)$, $(2,3,3)$, $(2,3,4)$, or $(2,3,5)$. The rest of the proof recognises the corresponding group by how it permutes its axes. The numerical restriction comes from counting; identifying the abstract group needs the extra action arguments.

All these cases occur: rotations about one axis by multiples of $2\pi/n$, the symmetries of a regular $n$-gon in a plane (turning it over is a half-turn in space), and the rotation groups of the tetrahedron, the cube and the icosahedron, of orders $12$, $24$, $60$, which by the theorem are $A_4$, $\mathfrak S_4$ and $A_5$. One can show that two finite subgroups of $SO(3)$ that are isomorphic are conjugate; so up to a change of axes these are \emph{all} the finite rotation groups, and every finite symmetry of a molecule or a crystal, up to reflections, is in this list. In particular the rotation group of the icosahedron is $A_5$, and its natural $3$-dimensional representation is the character $\psi_3$ of Chapter~11.

The preimages of these groups in $S^3$ are the \emph{binary} polyhedral groups, of orders $24$, $48$ and $120$ (Exercises~\ref{ex:2T} and~\ref{ex:2I}). The binary icosahedral group $2I$, of order $120$, is especially rich: the quotient $S^3/2I$ is the Poincaré homology sphere, a $3$-manifold with the same homology as $S^3$ but a different fundamental group, and the $120$ elements of $2I$ are the vertices of the regular $4$-dimensional polytope called the $600$-cell. These connections, found one by one over a century, are still being explored.

The norm of a quaternion is a sum of four squares. Because norms multiply, multiplying two quaternions turns the product of two such sums into another sum of four squares. The resulting identity is a piece of number theory hidden in the algebra of rotations.

\section{Quaternions and four squares}

Volume~2 proved that every positive integer is a sum of four squares, by Lagrange's descent. Quaternions explain the theorem as unique factorisation explains the two-square theorem in $\Z[i]$. The \emph{Hurwitz quaternions} $\mathcal H$ are the quaternions $a+bi+cj+dk$ whose four coordinates $a,b,c,d$ are either all in $\Z$ or all in $\Z+\frac12$.
They form a ring in which division with remainder works on one side, and the proof that a prime is a norm follows the proof for $\Z[i]$; Exercise~\ref{ex:hurwitz} carries it out in full.

\begin{remark}[a first look at categories]
Many constructions of this volume share a pattern: they take objects (groups, rings, fields, representations) to other objects, and maps between objects to maps between the new objects, respecting composition. $G\mapsto G/G'$, $R\mapsto R[X]$, a finite abelian group $A\mapsto\widehat A$, $q\mapsto R_q$: the language of \emph{categories} (objects and morphisms) and \emph{functors} (maps between categories) was invented in 1945 by Eilenberg and Mac Lane to make such patterns precise. The duality $A\mapsto\widehat A$ reverses arrows (a homomorphism $A\to B$ gives $\widehat B\to\widehat A$), and the Galois correspondence of Chapter~10 is an order-reversing dictionary of the same kind. Volume~10 uses this language systematically.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
Three facts from different chapters meet in the icosahedron: its rotation group is $A_5$, the smallest non-abelian simple group (Chapter~4), which is why the quintic has no formula (Chapter~10); its character table contains the golden ratio (Chapter~11); and its double cover in the quaternions is the binary icosahedral group, whose $120$ elements are the vertices of a regular polytope in four dimensions, the $600$-cell. Klein's \emph{Lectures on the Icosahedron} (1884) used these symmetries to solve the quintic, not by radicals, but with one extra function, exactly as the cubic is solved with trigonometry. Symmetry, algebra and arithmetic are one subject.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:compose}
Use quaternions to find the axis and angle of the rotation obtained by turning by $90^\circ$ about the $z$-axis, then by $90^\circ$ about the $x$-axis.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:euler4}
Write Euler's identity expressing $(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)(a'^2+b'^2+c'^2+d'^2)$ as a sum of four squares of bilinear expressions, using $N(pq)=N(p)N(q)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rotmatrix}
Let $q=a+bi+cj+dk$ be a unit quaternion. Write the matrix of $R_q$ in the basis $i,j,k$, and check that its trace is $4a^2-1=1+2\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle of the rotation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:conjS3}
Show that two unit quaternions are conjugate in $S^3$ iff they have the same real part. Deduce that two rotations are conjugate in $SO(3)$ iff they have the same angle (in $[0,\pi]$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:2T}
Show that the $24$ quaternions $\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k,\frac12(\pm1\pm i\pm j\pm k)$ form a subgroup $2T$ of $S^3$, that its image in $SO(3)$ has order $12$, and that this image is isomorphic to $A_4$ (count its elements of order $2$).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:2I}
Let $2I\subset S^3$ be the preimage of a rotation group $G\cong A_5$. Show that $|2I|=120$, that $-1$ is the only element of order $2$ in $S^3$, and deduce that $2I$ is isomorphic neither to $\mathfrak S_5$ nor to $A_5\times\Z/2\Z$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:hurwitz}
(Four squares with quaternions.)
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\mathcal H$ is a subring of $\mathbb H$, stable under conjugation, and that $N(x)\in\Z$ for $x\in\mathcal H$. Show that the units of $\mathcal H$ (elements of norm $1$) are the $24$ elements of $2T$.
\item Show that for every real quaternion $x$ there is $h\in\mathcal H$ with $N(x-h)<1$. Deduce: for $a,b\in\mathcal H$, $b\ne0$, there are $q,r\in\mathcal H$ with $a=qb+r$ and $N(r)<N(b)$; and every left ideal of $\mathcal H$ is of the form $\mathcal Hb$.
\item Let $p$ be an odd prime. Using integers $x,y$ with $1+x^2+y^2\equiv0\pmod p$ (Volume~2, Chapter~7), show that $p=N(\delta)$ for some $\delta\in\mathcal H$, and then that $p$ is a sum of four integer squares.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:sylvester}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Fibonacci, 1202.) Let $0<\frac ab<1$ be rational. Subtract the largest unit fraction $\frac1n\le\frac ab$, and repeat
with the remainder. Show that the process stops, and writes $\frac ab$ as a sum of distinct unit fractions. Apply it to
$\frac4{13}$.
\item (Sylvester, 1880.) Let $s_0=2$ and $s_{k+1}=s_k^2-s_k+1$, so $2$, $3$, $7$, $43$, $1807$, \dots Show that
$\sum_{k=0}^N\frac1{s_k}=1-\frac1{s_{N+1}-1}$, and that the $s_k$ are pairwise coprime.
\item Show that there is a constant $E>1$ such that $s_k=\bigl\lfloor E^{2^{k+1}}+\frac12\bigr\rfloor$ for every $k\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:jensen}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that for real $x$ and $\theta$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\Bigl(x^2-2x\cos\Bigl(\theta+\frac{2\pi k}n\Bigr)+1\Bigr)=x^{2n}-2x^n\cos n\theta+1.
\]
\item Deduce Poisson's integral: for $|x|\ne1$, $\int_0^\pi\log(1-2x\cos\theta+x^2)\dd\theta$ is $0$ if $|x|<1$ and
$2\pi\log|x|$ if $|x|>1$.
\item Let $P=a\prod_{k=1}^d(X-z_k)\in\C[X]$, $a\ne0$, with no root of modulus $1$. Show that
\[
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log\bigl|P(e^{i\theta})\bigr|\dd\theta=\log|a|+\sum_{k=1}^d\log\max(1,|z_k|).
\]
What does Problem~\ref{pb:kronecker} say about the polynomials of $\Z[X]$ for which this is $0$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:mersennediv}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $M_p=2^p-1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that every prime factor $q$ of $M_p$ satisfies $q\equiv1\pmod{2p}$ and $q\equiv\pm1\pmod8$.
\item Show that if $p\equiv3\pmod4$ and $2p+1$ is prime, then $2p+1$ divides $M_p$. Deduce that $M_{11}$, $M_{23}$ and
$M_{83}$ are composite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:dice}
A die is a cube whose faces carry the numbers $1$ to $6$, each once. Two dice are the same if a rotation carries one onto the
other.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item How many different dice are there?
\item On a standard die, opposite faces add up to $7$. How many different standard dice are there?
\item Show that no die is the same as its mirror image, so that the dice of (a) come in mirror pairs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:ksubsets}
Let $\pi_k(\sigma)$ be the number of $k$-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ fixed by $\sigma\in\mathfrak S_n$, and let
$0\le k,l\le\frac n2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\langle\pi_k,\pi_l\rangle=\min(k,l)+1$.
\item Deduce that $\chi_0=1$ and $\chi_k=\pi_k-\pi_{k-1}$ ($1\le k\le\frac n2$) are distinct irreducible characters of
$\mathfrak S_n$, of degrees $\binom nk-\binom n{k-1}$.
\item (Livingstone and Wagner.) Let $G\le\mathfrak S_n$. Show that the number of orbits of $G$ on $k$-subsets is a
non-decreasing function of $k$ for $0\le k\le\frac n2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:egz}
\emph{Erdős--Ginzburg--Ziv (1961).} We show that among any $2n-1$ integers there are $n$ whose sum is divisible by $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove Chevalley--Warning for systems: if $f_1,\dots,f_r\in\mathbb F_p[x_1,\dots,x_m]$ and $\sum_j\deg f_j<m$, then the number
of common zeros of the $f_j$ in $\mathbb F_p^m$ is divisible by $p$.
\item Prove the theorem for $n=p$ prime, with $f_1=\sum_ia_ix_i^{p-1}$ and $f_2=\sum_ix_i^{p-1}$.
\item Show that if the theorem holds for $m$ and for $n$, it holds for $mn$. Conclude.
\item Show that $2n-1$ cannot be replaced by $2n-2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:abelian}
\emph{How many abelian groups? (Erdős and Szekeres, 1934).} Let $A(n)$ be the number of abelian groups of order $n$, up to
isomorphism.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $A(n)=\prod_ip(e_i)$ if $n=\prod_ip_i^{e_i}$, and that $\sum_nA(n)n^{-s}=\prod_{k\ge1}\zeta(ks)$ for $s>1$.
\item Show that $\sum_{n\le x}A(n)=cx+O(\sqrt x)$ with $c=\prod_{k\ge2}\zeta(k)=2.2948\ldots$: on average, there are about $2.29$
abelian groups of each order.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:dodecaturns}
\emph{Random turns of an icosahedron.} The rotation group of the regular icosahedron is isomorphic to $A_5$ (Theorem~12.5), and the
$20$ rotations by a third of a turn about the axes through the centres of opposite faces form the conjugacy class of the
$3$-cycles. An icosahedron is turned, again and again, by a uniformly chosen one of these $20$ rotations.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Let $\mu$ be a probability on a finite group $G$ that is constant on conjugacy classes, and $\mu^{*t}$ the law of the walk at time
$t$, started at $e$. Show that, for every $g\in G$,
\[
\mu^{*t}(g)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_\chi\chi(1)\overline{\chi(g)}\,\Bigl(\frac{\hat\mu(\chi)}{\chi(1)}\Bigr)^t,\qquad\hat\mu(\chi)=\sum_{h\in G}\mu(h)\chi(h),
\]
the sum being over the irreducible characters of $G$. (Show first that, for a class function $f$ and an irreducible representation $\rho$ of
dimension $d$, the matrix $\sum_hf(h)\rho(h)$ is the scalar $\frac1d\sum_hf(h)\chi_\rho(h)$.)
\item Using the character table of $A_5$ (Exercise~11.7), show that the icosahedron is back in its initial position after $t$
turns with probability
\[
\frac1{60}\Bigl(1+16\cdot4^{-t}+25\cdot\Bigl(-\frac15\Bigr)^t\Bigr).
\]
\item Find the probability that after $t$ turns the icosahedron has turned by a fifth of a turn (in either direction) about some
axis through opposite vertices, and show that the law of the position converges to the uniform law at the rate $4^{-t}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:compose}
\idea A spatial rotation through $\theta$ about a unit axis $u$ is represented by the unit quaternion $\cos(\theta/2)+u\sin(\theta/2)$. Compose rotations by multiplying the representing quaternions in the same order as the corresponding transformations: second times first.

\writeup The first rotation is by $90^\circ$ about the $z$-axis, represented by
\[
q_z=\cos45^\circ+k\sin45^\circ=\frac{1+k}{\sqrt2}.
\]
The second is by $90^\circ$ about the $x$-axis:
\[
q_x=\cos45^\circ+i\sin45^\circ=\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}.
\]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:su2so3}, $R_{pq}=R_p\circ R_q$. The composition that first applies $R_{q_z}$ and then $R_{q_x}$ is therefore represented by $q=q_xq_z$, not by $q_zq_x$. Multiply, remembering that $ik=-j$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
q&=\frac12(1+i)(1+k)\\
&=\frac12(1+i+k+ik)
=\frac12(1+i-j+k).
\end{aligned}
\]

The scalar part is $1/2$. In the axis--angle formula it equals $\cos(\theta/2)$, so choosing $0\le\theta\le180^\circ$ gives
\[
\cos\frac\theta2=\frac12
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\theta=120^\circ}.
\]
The pure part of $q$ is $\frac12(i-j+k)$. Its length is $\sqrt3/2=\sin60^\circ$, so after normalisation the rotation axis is
\[
\boxed{u=\frac1{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)}.
\]

We can check the result without quaternion multiplication. Under the first quarter-turn about $z$, the basis vectors change as $e_x\mapsto e_y$, $e_y\mapsto-e_x$, $e_z\mapsto e_z$. Under the next quarter-turn about $x$, $e_y\mapsto e_z$ and $e_z\mapsto-e_y$, while $e_x$ remains fixed. Thus the composition sends
\[
e_x\mapsto e_z,\quad e_z\mapsto-e_y,\quad -e_y\mapsto e_x.
\]
It cycles these three vectors. Their sum $e_x-e_y+e_z$ is fixed, so it gives the axis. A three-cycle of those perpendicular directions is a rotation by $120^\circ$, agreeing with the quaternion calculation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:euler4}
\idea Quaternion multiplication takes two sums of four squares into another sum of four squares. Write two general quaternions, multiply with $i^2=j^2=k^2=-1$ and $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$, then apply multiplicativity of the norm.

\writeup Let
\[
p=a+bi+cj+dk,\qquad
q=a'+b'i+c'j+d'k,
\]
where all eight coefficients are real, or integers if we want an arithmetic identity. Quaternion multiplication is not commutative, so the order of the factors matters. Collecting the real, $i$-, $j$-, and $k$-components gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
pq={}&(aa'-bb'-cc'-dd')\\
&+(ab'+ba'+cd'-dc')\,i\\
&+(ac'-bd'+ca'+db')\,j\\
&+(ad'+bc'-cb'+da')\,k.
\end{aligned}
\]
We can check one less familiar sign directly: the $j$-component contains $bd'(ik)=-bd'j$ because $ik=-j$, and $db'(ki)=db'j$ because $ki=j$.

The conjugate of $p$ is $\bar p=a-bi-cj-dk$, so
\[
N(p)=p\bar p=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2,
\qquad
N(q)=a'^2+b'^2+c'^2+d'^2.
\]
Conjugation reverses products: $\overline{pq}=\bar q\,\bar p$. By associativity,
\[
N(pq)=pq\,\overline{pq}
=pq\bar q\,\bar p=p\,N(q)\,\bar p
=N(q)N(p).
\]
Since the norm of a quaternion is the sum of the squares of its four coordinates, this proves Euler's four-square identity:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)(a'^2+b'^2+c'^2+d'^2)\\
={}&(aa'-bb'-cc'-dd')^2
+(ab'+ba'+cd'-dc')^2\\
&+(ac'-bd'+ca'+db')^2
+(ad'+bc'-cb'+da')^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
For integer inputs, every expression on the right is an integer. Thus a product of two integers each representable as a sum of four squares is again representable as a sum of four squares. This multiplicativity is a key ingredient in Volume~2's proof of Lagrange's four-square theorem; by itself, it reduces the question to constructing representations for primes rather than proving that step automatically.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rotmatrix}
\idea Multiplication of quaternions gives a vector formula for conjugation. It is quicker and clearer than expanding $qv\bar q$ independently for each basis vector. Use the formula once for $v=i,j,k$, then compute the trace from the diagonal.

\writeup Write $q=a+x$ with $x=bi+cj+dk$ pure, and identify a pure quaternion $w$ with its vector in $\R^3$. Since $q$ has norm $1$,
\[
a^2+|x|^2=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=1.
\]
The multiplication rule for pure quaternions is $xy=-x\cdot y+x\times y$. Expanding $(a+x)w(a-x)$ with this rule gives
\[
R_q(w)=(a^2-|x|^2)w+2(x\cdot w)x+2a(x\times w).
\]
For example, take $w=i$, the unit vector $(1,0,0)$. Then $x\cdot i=b$ and $x\times i=(0,d,-c)$, so
\[
R_q(i)=\begin{pmatrix}
a^2+b^2-c^2-d^2\\2(bc+ad)\\2(bd-ac)
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Applying the same formula to $j$ and $k$ gives the columns of the rotation matrix:
\[
R_q=
\begin{pmatrix}
a^2+b^2-c^2-d^2 & 2(bc-ad) & 2(bd+ac)\\
2(bc+ad) & a^2-b^2+c^2-d^2 & 2(cd-ab)\\
2(bd-ac) & 2(cd+ab) & a^2-b^2-c^2+d^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries:
\[
\operatorname{tr}R_q
=3a^2-(b^2+c^2+d^2)=4a^2-1.
\]
For a rotation through angle $\theta$, the quaternion formula of Theorem~\ref{thm:su2so3} gives $a=\cos(\theta/2)$ for one of its two representing unit quaternions. The sign of $a$ is irrelevant to $a^2$. Using the double-angle identity,
\[
4a^2-1=4\cos^2\frac\theta2-1=1+2\cos\theta.
\]
This also matches the geometric trace: in a basis consisting of the axis vector and two perpendicular vectors, the rotation matrix has diagonal entries $1,\cos\theta,\cos\theta$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:conjS3}
\idea Quaternion conjugation fixes the real part and rotates the three-dimensional pure part. Conversely, any two pure vectors of the same length are related by a spatial rotation. The double cover $S^3\to SO(3)$ then turns the quaternion statement into an angle statement.

\writeup Let $q=a+v$ be a unit quaternion with real part $a\in\R$ and pure part $v\in\R^3$. If $p\in S^3$, then
\[
pqp^{-1}=a+pvp^{-1}.
\]
The scalar $a$ is unchanged because it commutes with every quaternion, while $pvp^{-1}$ is again pure and has the same Euclidean length as $v$. Hence conjugate quaternions have the same real part.

Conversely, suppose unit quaternions $q=a+v$ and $q'=a+v'$ have the same real part. Their unit norms imply
\[
|v|^2=1-a^2=|v'|^2.
\]
If $v=0$, then $v'=0$ and $q=q'$, so they are conjugate. Otherwise $v/|v|$ and $v'/|v'|$ are unit vectors. There is a rotation $T\in SO(3)$ sending one to the other. By Theorem~\ref{thm:su2so3}, $T=R_p$ for some $p\in S^3$, so
\[
pvp^{-1}=v',
\qquad
pqp^{-1}=a+v'=q'.
\]
We have proved
\[
\boxed{q,q'\text{ conjugate in }S^3
\iff\operatorname{Re}q=\operatorname{Re}q'}.
\]

For rotations, the homomorphism $R:S^3\to SO(3)$ is onto and has kernel $\{\pm1\}$. Thus $R_q$ and $R_{q'}$ are conjugate in $SO(3)$ precisely when $q'$ is conjugate in $S^3$ to $q$ or to $-q$. By the first part, this is equivalent to
\[
\operatorname{Re}q'=\pm\operatorname{Re}q.
\]
Choose for each rotation a representing quaternion whose real part is non-negative. If $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is its unsigned rotation angle, that real part is $\cos(\theta/2)$. Cosine is strictly decreasing on $[0,\pi/2]$, so two non-negative real parts agree exactly when their angles agree. Hence
\[
\boxed{R_q,R_{q'}\text{ conjugate in }SO(3)
\iff\text{they have the same angle in }[0,\pi].}
\]
This is the conjugacy classification of spatial rotations.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:2T}
\idea Instead of checking all $24^2$ products, find two small kinds of quaternions that generate the displayed set. The eight integer-coordinate units form $Q_8$, and one half-integer quaternion $h=(1+i+j+k)/2$ cyclically conjugates $i,j,k$. This gives a group of exactly $24$ elements. Then examine the orders of its rotations.

\writeup Let
\[
Q_8=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}\subset S^3,
\qquad h=\frac{1+i+j+k}{2}.
\]
Both are already unit quaternions: $N(h)=4(1/2)^2=1$. The multiplication rules $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$ and their reversed versions give
\[
h^2=\frac{-1+i+j+k}{2},\qquad h^3=-1.
\]
The same multiplication table verifies
\[
hih^{-1}=j,\qquad hjh^{-1}=k,\qquad hkh^{-1}=i.
\]
In particular $h$ normalises $Q_8$, and the subgroup
\[
T=\langle Q_8,h\rangle
\]
is a union of right cosets of $Q_8$. Because $h^3=-1\in Q_8$, every word can be reduced to one of the forms $q$, $hq$, or $h^2q$, with $q\in Q_8$.

These three cosets are distinct. The elements of $Q_8$ have integer coordinates, while $h$ and $h^2$ have half-integer coordinates, so neither belongs to $Q_8$. Moreover $hQ_8=h^2Q_8$ would imply $h\in Q_8$, by multiplying by $h^{-1}$. Thus
\[
|T|=3|Q_8|=24.
\]

Right-multiplying $h$ or $h^2$ by $\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$ produces precisely the $16$ quaternions
\[
\frac12(\pm1\pm i\pm j\pm k)
\]
with all choices of signs. Indeed, each multiplication permutes the four coordinates and changes some signs, and the two distinct cosets contain $16$ different half-sign quaternions in total. Consequently
\[
T=Q_8\ \cup\
\left\{\frac12(\pm1\pm i\pm j\pm k)\right\},
\]
which is the claimed finite subgroup of $S^3$.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:su2so3}, the map $S^3\to SO(3)$ has kernel $\{\pm1\}$. Both kernel elements lie in $T$, so the image $\bar T$ has
\[
|\bar T|=|T|/2=12.
\]

We can identify $\bar T$ using its element orders. The six pure units $\pm i,\pm j,\pm k$ give three distinct half-turns in $SO(3)$, hence three elements of order $2$. The sixteen half-sign quaternions all have real part $\pm1/2$. Their rotations have angle $120^\circ$ or $240^\circ$, and give eight distinct elements of order $3$. Together with the identity, this accounts for
\[
1+3+8=12
\]
rotations.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:finiteso3}, a finite rotation group of order $12$ is cyclic of order $12$, dihedral $D_6$, or tetrahedral $A_4$. A cyclic group of order $12$ has exactly one involution; $D_6$ has seven (the six reflections in the planar dihedral model plus the central half-turn). Our group has exactly three. This rules out the first two possibilities and leaves
\[
\boxed{\bar T\cong A_4}.
\]
The group $T$ is called the \emph{binary tetrahedral group}, written $2T$; the double cover retains the sign of the representing quaternion, which the spatial rotation forgets.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:2I}
\idea The map $S^3\to SO(3)$ sends $q$ and $-q$ to the same rotation and has kernel $\{\pm1\}$. The preimage of a finite rotation group therefore has twice as many elements. To distinguish it from other groups of order $120$, count involutions.

\writeup Let $G\subset SO(3)$ be a rotation group isomorphic to $A_5$, so $|G|=60$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:su2so3}, the map
\[
R:S^3\to SO(3),\qquad q\mapsto R_q
\]
is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $\{1,-1\}$. Every fibre has two elements. Therefore its preimage
\[
2I=R^{-1}(G)
\]
has exactly
\[
\boxed{|2I|=2|G|=120}
\]
elements.

Let us now determine all unit quaternions of order $2$. Write a unit quaternion as $q=a+x$, where $a\in\R$ and $x$ is pure (a vector in $\R^3$). Since $x^2=-|x|^2$, we have
\[
q^2=(a+x)^2=a^2-|x|^2+2ax.
\]
If $q^2=1$, its pure part must vanish: $2ax=0$. If $x\ne0$, this forces $a=0$, but then $q^2=-|x|^2$ is a non-positive real number, contradicting $q^2=1$. Thus $x=0$, so $q=a$ is real. The unit-norm condition gives $a=\pm1$. The element $1$ has order $1$, and $-1$ has order exactly $2$. Therefore
\[
\boxed{-1\text{ is the unique element of order }2\text{ in }S^3}.
\]
In particular, $2I$ has only one involution.

The group $\mathfrak S_5$ has many involutions, including its ten transpositions. The group $A_5\times\Z/2\Z$ also has more than one: for example, $(e,1)$ and $((12)(34),0)$ are distinct elements of order $2$. An isomorphism preserves the number of elements of each order, so
\[
2I\not\cong\mathfrak S_5,
\qquad
2I\not\cong A_5\times\Z/2\Z.
\]

This is an instructive warning: two finite groups can have the same order and even map onto the same quotient, but differ in their internal multiplication. In fact the binary icosahedral group $2I$ is isomorphic to $SL_2(\mathbb F_5)$, not to either of these other groups.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:hurwitz}
\idea Hurwitz quaternions are the quaternion analogue of Gaussian integers, except that multiplication is not commutative. We first check the arithmetic of the lattice. A nearest-point argument gives Euclidean division \emph{on the left}. That lets us factor a rational prime whose reduction is a sum of three squares, producing the four-square theorem.

\writeup (a) Put
\[
L=\Z+\Z i+\Z j+\Z k,\qquad
\omega=\frac{1+i+j+k}{2},\qquad
\mathcal H=L+\Z\omega.
\]
The set $\mathcal H$ consists exactly of quaternions whose four coordinates are either all integers or all half-integers with odd numerators. The Lipschitz quaternions $L$ form a ring because the table $i^2=j^2=k^2=-1$ and $ij=k$, $jk=i$, $ki=j$ has integer structure constants.

It remains to check products involving $\omega$. Direct multiplication gives
\[
\omega^2=\omega-1,\qquad
i\omega=\frac{-1+i-j+k}{2},\qquad
\omega i=\frac{-1+i+j-k}{2},
\]
and the analogous identities with $i,j,k$ cyclically permuted. Every displayed quaternion lies in $\mathcal H$. By bilinearity over $\Z$, these identities suffice to check that products of arbitrary elements of $L+\Z\omega$ remain in $\mathcal H$: expand
\[
(a+b\omega)(c+d\omega)
=ac+a(d\omega)+(b\omega)c+bd\,\omega^2
\]
with $a,b,c,d\in\Z$-linear combinations as appropriate. Thus $\mathcal H$ is closed under addition, additive inverses, and multiplication, and contains $1$.

Quaternion conjugation preserves $L$ and sends $\omega$ to
\[
\bar\omega=\frac{1-i-j-k}{2}=1-\omega,
\]
so it preserves $\mathcal H$ too. If $x=a+bi+cj+dk$ has integer coordinates, its norm $N(x)=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2$ is an integer. If its coordinates are all odd halves, write them as $m_j/2$ with each $m_j$ odd. Since every odd square is $1$ modulo $8$,
\[
N(x)=\frac{m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2+m_4^2}{4}\in\Z.
\]
The norm is non-negative, multiplicative, and vanishes only at zero.

The units in $\mathcal H$ are exactly the elements of norm $1$. Indeed $x^{-1}=\bar x/N(x)$ lies in $\mathcal H$ when $N(x)=1$, whereas if $xy=1$ in $\mathcal H$ then $N(x)N(y)=1$ with both positive integers, forcing $N(x)=1$.

For integer coordinates, norm $1$ means exactly one coordinate equals $\pm1$, so we obtain the eight units
\[
\pm1,\quad\pm i,\quad\pm j,\quad\pm k.
\]
For odd half-integer coordinates, norm $1$ means $\sum_{j=1}^4m_j^2=4$, so every $m_j$ is $\pm1$. This gives sixteen more units
\[
\frac{\pm1\pm i\pm j\pm k}{2}.
\]
Thus the unit group has exactly $24$ elements and is the binary tetrahedral group $2T$ of Exercise~\ref{ex:2T}.

(b) Identify a quaternion with its four real coordinates. Round each coordinate of $x\in\mathbb H$ to a nearest integer, obtaining $m\in L$. Every coordinate error is at most $1/2$ in absolute value, so
\[
N(x-m)\le4\left(\frac12\right)^2=1.
\]
If equality fails, take $h=m$ and the desired strict bound follows. If equality holds, then \emph{all four} coordinate errors have absolute value $1/2$. Thus $x$ itself has four odd half-integer coordinates and belongs to $\mathcal H$. Choosing $h=x$ gives $N(x-h)=0<1$. We have proved that for every quaternion $x$ there is $h\in\mathcal H$ with
\[
\boxed{N(x-h)<1}.
\]

For $a,b\in\mathcal H$ with $b\ne0$, choose $q\in\mathcal H$ close to $ab^{-1}$. Define
\[
r=a-qb=(ab^{-1}-q)b.
\]
Because $\mathcal H$ is a ring, $r\in\mathcal H$, and multiplicativity of the norm gives
\[
N(r)=N(ab^{-1}-q)N(b)<N(b).
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{a=qb+r,\qquad N(r)<N(b)}.
\]
The order $qb$ matters because quaternion multiplication is not commutative.

Let $I\ne\{0\}$ be a left ideal of $\mathcal H$. Since norms of non-zero elements are positive integers, choose $b\in I$ of smallest positive norm. For any $a\in I$, divide as above: $a=qb+r$ with $N(r)<N(b)$. Left ideal closure gives $r=a-qb\in I$, so minimality forces $r=0$. Thus $a\in\mathcal Hb$ for every $a\in I$, and the reverse inclusion follows because $b\in I$. Therefore every left ideal is principal:
\[
\boxed{I=\mathcal Hb}.
\]

(c) Let $p$ be an odd prime. By the finite-field sum-of-two-squares result (Volume~2, Chapter~7), choose integers $x,y$ with
\[
1+x^2+y^2\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
Put $\alpha=1+xi+yj\in\mathcal H$. Its norm is a positive multiple of $p$:
\[
N(\alpha)=1+x^2+y^2=pm
\]
for some positive integer $m$.

Consider the \emph{left} ideal
\[
I=\mathcal Hp+\mathcal H\alpha.
\]
Part (b) makes it principal, say $I=\mathcal H\delta$. Because $p\in I$, there exists $\gamma\in\mathcal H$ such that
\[
p=\gamma\delta.
\]
Taking norms,
\[
p^2=N(p)=N(\gamma)N(\delta).
\]
The two factors on the right are positive integers, so the prime factorisation of $p^2$ leaves
\[
N(\delta)\in\{1,p,p^2\}.
\]

We rule out the two extremes. If $N(\delta)=1$, then $\delta$ is a unit and $I=\mathcal H$. In particular there are $u,v\in\mathcal H$ with
\[
1=up+v\alpha.
\]
Multiply on the \emph{right} by $\bar\alpha$:
\[
\bar\alpha=up\bar\alpha+v\alpha\bar\alpha
=p(u\bar\alpha+vm),
\]
using $N(\alpha)=pm$ and centrality of the rational integer $p$. This would put $\bar\alpha/p$ in $\mathcal H$. But its real coordinate is $1/p$, which is neither an integer nor an odd half-integer for odd $p\ge3$. Contradiction.

If $N(\delta)=p^2$, then the norm identity forces $N(\gamma)=1$, so $\gamma$ is a unit. The equation $p=\gamma\delta$ gives $\delta=\gamma^{-1}p$, hence
\[
I=\mathcal H\delta=\mathcal Hp.
\]
But $\alpha\in I$ would imply $\alpha=up$ for some $u\in\mathcal H$, so $\alpha/p=u\in\mathcal H$, again impossible because its real coordinate is $1/p$.

The only remaining possibility is
\[
\boxed{N(\delta)=p}.
\]
If $\delta$ has integer coordinates, we already have $p$ as a sum of four integer squares.

If instead $\delta$ has odd half-integer coordinates, write each coordinate as $m_j/2$ with $m_j$ odd. Choose signs $\varepsilon_j\in\{\pm1\}$ such that $m_j\equiv\varepsilon_j\pmod4$. The quaternion
\[
\varepsilon=\frac{\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2 i+\varepsilon_3j+\varepsilon_4k}{2}
\]
is one of the sixteen norm-one Hurwitz units. Our sign choice ensures $\delta-\varepsilon$ has \emph{even integer} coordinates, so $\delta=\varepsilon+2\mu$ with $\mu\in L$. Multiply on the right by $\bar\varepsilon$:
\[
\delta\bar\varepsilon=1+2\mu\bar\varepsilon.
\]
Since $\mu$ has integer coordinates and $\bar\varepsilon$ has half-integer coordinates, $2\mu\bar\varepsilon$ has integer coordinates. Therefore $\delta\bar\varepsilon\in L$, and
\[
N(\delta\bar\varepsilon)=N(\delta)N(\varepsilon)=p.
\]
This converts the half-integral representation into an integer four-square representation.

Finally $2=1^2+1^2+0^2+0^2$, while the argument above handles every odd prime. Euler's four-square identity (Exercise~\ref{ex:euler4}) shows that a product of two integers represented by four squares is represented by four squares. Every positive integer is a product of primes, so every positive integer is a sum of four integer squares.

\tools Volume~2 (finite-field sums of two squares and unique prime factorisation); quaternion norm and Euler's identity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:sylvester}
\idea The three parts share a theme: turn a sequence into something simpler. In the Egyptian-fraction algorithm an integer numerator strictly decreases. In Sylvester's sequence a reciprocal becomes a telescoping difference. In the final part, taking logarithms turns repeated squaring into an almost constant quantity.

\writeup (a) Let $0<a/b<1$ with $a,b$ positive integers. Choose the least integer $n$ such that $1/n\le a/b$, namely
\[
n=\left\lceil\frac ba\right\rceil\ge2.
\]
By the definition of the ceiling,
\[
\frac1n\le\frac ab<\frac1{n-1}.
\]
Subtract the unit fraction:
\[
\frac ab-\frac1n=\frac{an-b}{bn}.
\]
The new numerator satisfies $0\le an-b<a$; the strict upper bound follows from $a(n-1)<b$. If the numerator is zero, the process ends. Otherwise it is a positive integer strictly smaller than the old numerator, and the same procedure can be repeated. A strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers cannot continue forever, so the algorithm terminates.

The unit fractions are distinct. Indeed, the current remainder is less than
\[
\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n=\frac1{n(n-1)}\le\frac1n.
\]
Thus the next unit fraction chosen is strictly smaller than $1/n$, and the denominators strictly increase.

For $4/13$, the first denominator is $n=\lceil13/4\rceil=4$, leaving
\[
\frac4{13}-\frac14=\frac3{52}.
\]
Now $\lceil52/3\rceil=18$, and
\[
\frac3{52}-\frac1{18}=\frac1{468}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{\frac4{13}=\frac14+\frac1{18}+\frac1{468}}.
\]

(b) Let $s_0=2$ and $s_{k+1}=s_k^2-s_k+1$. Rearranging gives
\[
s_{k+1}-1=s_k(s_k-1).
\]
Consequently
\[
\frac1{s_k-1}-\frac1{s_{k+1}-1}
=\frac1{s_k-1}-\frac1{s_k(s_k-1)}
=\frac1{s_k}.
\]
Sum from $k=0$ to $N$. The intermediate terms cancel:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^N\frac1{s_k}
=\frac1{s_0-1}-\frac1{s_{N+1}-1}
=\boxed{1-\frac1{s_{N+1}-1}}.
\]
Iterating the recurrence gives
\[
s_{k+1}-1=s_k(s_k-1)
=s_ks_{k-1}\cdots s_0(s_0-1)
=\prod_{j=0}^ks_j,
\]
because $s_0-1=1$. Hence $s_{k+1}\equiv1\pmod{s_j}$ for every $j\le k$. If a positive integer divided both $s_j$ and $s_{k+1}$, it would divide their difference $1$, so the gcd is $1$. The terms are pairwise coprime.

(c) Write
\[
u_k=s_k-\frac12.
\]
Then the recurrence becomes
\[
u_{k+1}=u_k^2+\frac14,\qquad u_0=\frac32.
\]
In particular $u_k$ is positive and grows strictly to infinity. Put
\[
c_k=2^{-k}\log u_k.
\]
Taking logarithms of the recurrence yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_{k+1}
&=2^{-k-1}\log\left(u_k^2\left(1+\frac1{4u_k^2}\right)\right)\\
&=c_k+2^{-k-1}\log\left(1+\frac1{4u_k^2}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $c_{k+1}>c_k$. Using $\log(1+x)\le x$ for $x\ge0$ and $u_k\ge3/2$ gives
\[
0<c_{k+1}-c_k\le\frac{2^{-k-1}}{4u_k^2}
\le\frac{2^{-k-1}}9.
\]
The right-hand side is summable, so the increasing sequence $c_k$ has a finite limit $c>0$. Define
\[
E=e^{c/2}>1.
\]
Then $E^{2^{k+1}}=e^{2^kc}$.

We need to show that this number is within one half of $s_k$. The telescoping tail identity is
\[
2^kc-\log u_k
=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}2^{k-j-1}
\log\left(1+\frac1{4u_j^2}\right).
\]
The tail is strictly positive, so $u_k<e^{2^kc}$. Since $u_j\ge u_k$, its upper bound is
\[
2^kc-\log u_k
\le\frac1{4u_k^2}\sum_{j=k}^\infty2^{k-j-1}
=\frac1{4u_k^2}.
\]
Exponentiating,
\[
u_k<E^{2^{k+1}}\le u_ke^{1/(4u_k^2)}.
\]
For $u_k\ge3/2$, use $e^t-1\le te^t$ and $t=1/(4u_k^2)\le1/9$:
\[
u_k(e^t-1)\le\frac{e^{1/9}}{4u_k}
\le\frac{e^{1/9}}6<\frac12.
\]
Therefore
\[
s_k-\frac12=u_k<E^{2^{k+1}}<u_k+\frac12=s_k.
\]
Adding $1/2$ places the power strictly between $s_k$ and $s_k+1/2$, so
\[
\boxed{s_k=\left\lfloor E^{2^{k+1}}+\frac12\right\rfloor}
\]
for every $k\ge0$. The constant is approximately $E=1.2640847353\ldots$; the argument proves existence without relying on the numerical approximation.

\tools Volume~1 (monotone sequences and series); Volume~2 (Egyptian fractions and coprimality).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:jensen}
\idea Put the quadratic factors into the form $|x-e^{i\varphi}|^2$. Their arguments run through the $n$-th roots of one complex number, so their product collapses to a single quadratic expression in $x^n$. Taking logarithms gives Riemann sums, and summing over polynomial roots produces Jensen's formula.

\writeup (a) For real $x$ and $\varphi$,
\[
x^2-2x\cos\varphi+1
=(x-e^{i\varphi})(x-e^{-i\varphi})
=|x-e^{i\varphi}|^2.
\]
The $n$ complex numbers
\[
\omega_k=e^{i(\theta+2\pi k/n)},\qquad 0\le k<n,
\]
are exactly the roots of the equation $Z^n=e^{in\theta}$. Therefore
\[
\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(x-\omega_k)=x^n-e^{in\theta}.
\]
Conjugating gives $\prod_k(x-\bar\omega_k)=x^n-e^{-in\theta}$. Multiplying these two identities yields
\[
\boxed{\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}
\left(x^2-2x\cos\left(\theta+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)+1\right)
=x^{2n}-2x^n\cos(n\theta)+1}.
\]
The identity is purely algebraic and remains true when one of its factors is zero.

(b) Suppose $|x|\ne1$. Then
\[
f(\theta)=\log(1-2x\cos\theta+x^2)
\]
is a continuous $2\pi$-periodic real function: its logarithm's argument is $|x-e^{i\theta}|^2>0$. The equally spaced Riemann sums converge to its integral. Taking the logarithm in part (a) with $\theta=0$ gives
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(2\pi k/n)
=\log\bigl((x^n-1)^2\bigr)
=2\log|x^n-1|.
\]
Hence
\[
\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)\,\dd\theta
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{4\pi}{n}\log|x^n-1|.
\]

If $|x|<1$, then $x^n\to0$, so $\log|x^n-1|\to0$ and this limit equals $0$. If $|x|>1$, factor
\[
|x^n-1|=|x|^n|1-x^{-n}|,
\]
and the limit becomes
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(4\pi\log|x|+\frac{4\pi}{n}\log|1-x^{-n}|\right)
=4\pi\log|x|.
\]
Because $f(2\pi-\theta)=f(\theta)$, the integral from $0$ to $\pi$ is half the integral over a full period. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\int_0^\pi\log(1-2x\cos\theta+x^2)\,\dd\theta=
\begin{cases}
0,&|x|<1,\\
2\pi\log|x|,&|x|>1.
\end{cases}}
\]

(c) Write
\[
P(X)=a\prod_{k=1}^d(X-z_k),
\quad a\ne0,\quad |z_k|\ne1.
\]
For $z_k=r_ke^{i\alpha_k}$ with $r_k\ge0$, we have
\[
\log|e^{i\theta}-z_k|
=\tfrac12\log(1-2r_k\cos(\theta-\alpha_k)+r_k^2).
\]
A shift of $\theta$ does not change the integral over a full period. Applying part (b) and doubling its half-period result gives
\[
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\log|e^{i\theta}-z_k|\,\dd\theta
=\log\max(1,r_k).
\]
(The case $z_k=0$ contributes $0$, directly or by the same formula.)

Now $\log|P(e^{i\theta})|
=\log|a|+\sum_k\log|e^{i\theta}-z_k|$. Integrating this finite sum proves
\[
\boxed{\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|P(e^{i\theta})|\,\dd\theta
=\log|a|+\sum_{k=1}^d\log\max(1,|z_k|)}.
\]
The exponential of this quantity,
\[
M(P)=|a|\prod_k\max(1,|z_k|),
\]
is the \emph{Mahler measure} of $P$.

What if $P\in\Z[X]\setminus\{0\}$ and the integral is $0$? Then $M(P)=1$. Because $|a|$ is a positive integer and each factor $\max(1,|z_k|)$ is at least $1$, we must have $|a|=1$ and $|z_k|\le1$ for every root. Kronecker's theorem (Problem~\ref{pb:kronecker}) says that a monic integer polynomial with all roots in the closed unit disc has only roots of unity and zero as roots; equivalently, up to sign, it is a product of a power of $X$ and cyclotomic polynomials.

**There is a useful hypothesis check here.** Part (c) excluded roots on the unit circle. Under \emph{that} restriction, cyclotomic factors cannot occur. Thus the only integer polynomials in part (c) for which the integral vanishes are
\[
\boxed{P(X)=\pm X^m\quad(m\ge0)}.
\]
If roots on the unit circle are allowed, the logarithmic singularities are integrable, Jensen's formula still holds, and products of cyclotomic polynomials with monomials give all the additional zero-measure examples.

\tools Volume~1 (roots of unity and Riemann sums); Problem~\ref{pb:kronecker}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:mersennediv}
\idea A prime factor $q$ of $2^p-1$ makes $2$ an element of order $p$ modulo $q$. Lagrange controls $q$ modulo $p$, and quadratic reciprocity controls $q$ modulo $8$. For the converse, Euler's criterion produces a factor when $2p+1$ is prime.

\writeup (a) Let $p$ be an odd prime and let $q$ be a prime factor of $M_p=2^p-1$. The number $M_p$ is odd, so $q$ is odd. In $\mathbb F_q^*$,
\[
2^p\equiv1\pmod q.
\]
The order of $2$ divides the prime $p$, hence is $1$ or $p$. It cannot be $1$, since $2\equiv1\pmod q$ would imply $q\mid1$. Therefore $\operatorname{ord}_q(2)=p$. By Lagrange,
\[
p\mid(q-1).
\]
Since $q-1$ is even and $p$ is odd, $2p\mid(q-1)$, giving
\[
\boxed{q\equiv1\pmod{2p}}.
\]

Also
\[
2^{p+1}\equiv2\pmod q,
\]
and $p+1$ is even. Thus
\[
2\equiv\bigl(2^{(p+1)/2}\bigr)^2\pmod q,
\]
so $2$ is a quadratic residue modulo $q$. The supplementary law $(2/q)=(-1)^{(q^2-1)/8}$ gives
\[
\boxed{q\equiv\pm1\pmod8}.
\]

(b) Now assume $p\equiv3\pmod4$ and $q=2p+1$ is prime. Since $2p+1\equiv7\pmod8$, the same supplementary law gives $(2/q)=1$. Euler's criterion then yields
\[
2^{(q-1)/2}=2^p\equiv1\pmod q.
\]
Therefore $q\mid(2^p-1)=M_p$.

For $p=11,23,83$, the corresponding numbers $q=23,47,167$ are prime. Each is a proper divisor of $2^p-1$ (as $p>3$), so
\[
\boxed{23\mid M_{11},\quad47\mid M_{23},\quad167\mid M_{83}}
\]
and all three Mersenne numbers are composite. The case $p=3$ is different: $2p+1=M_3=7$, which is prime, so divisibility alone would not prove compositeness.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:dice}
\idea All six face labels are distinct, so no non-identity rotation can preserve a numbering. The rotation group acts freely on the numberings. Orbit--stabiliser then divides the raw counts by $24$. Reflection reverses handedness and never belongs to the rotation group.

\writeup (a) The rotation group $C$ of a cube has $24$ elements, as shown in Chapter~1: choose the image of one face in six ways and then choose one of four quarter-turn orientations. There are $6!=720$ ways to place the six distinct labels on the faces. No non-trivial rotation fixes a numbered die, because fixing the numbering forces it to fix every face individually, hence to be the identity. So every orbit has $24$ numberings. The number of different dice up to rotation is
\[
\boxed{\frac{6!}{24}=30}.
\]

(b) On a standard die, the opposite-face pairs are
\[
\{1,6\},\quad\{2,5\},\quad\{3,4\}.
\]
There are three axes through pairs of opposite faces. Assign the three numerical pairs to these axes in $3!$ ways. For each axis choose which end carries the smaller label in $2$ ways. This produces
\[
3!\cdot2^3=48
\]
labelled standard dice, all distinct. Again the rotational stabiliser is trivial, so there are
\[
\boxed{48/24=2}
\]
standard dice up to rotation.

(c) Fix a numbering $f$ of the faces and let $s$ be a reflection of the cube, which reverses orientation and has determinant $-1$. Its mirror image has numbering $f\circ s$ with the appropriate face-action convention. Suppose the mirror image were rotationally equivalent to the original. Then for some rotation $r$,
\[
f\circ s=f\circ r.
\]
Because the six labels are distinct, $f$ is injective on the faces, so cancellation gives $s=r$ as face permutations. But a genuine cube reflection is orientation-reversing, whereas every cube rotation has determinant $+1$. This is impossible.

Thus no die is equivalent to its mirror image. Reflection pairs the $30$ rotational classes with no fixed class, yielding
\[
\boxed{15\text{ mirror pairs}}.
\]
The two standard dice in part (b) are one such mirror pair.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ksubsets}
\idea A permutation character counts fixed subsets. Its inner product with another permutation character counts orbits on \emph{pairs} of subsets. For $\mathfrak S_n$, two pairs lie in the same orbit exactly when their intersection sizes agree. Subtract neighbouring permutation characters to obtain orthonormal irreducibles.

\writeup (a) Let $\Omega_k$ be the set of $k$-element subsets of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, and let $\pi_k$ be its permutation character. Burnside's lemma applied to the product action on $\Omega_k\times\Omega_l$ says
\[
\langle\pi_k,\pi_l\rangle_{\mathfrak S_n}
=\#\bigl(\mathfrak S_n\backslash(\Omega_k\times\Omega_l)\bigr).
\]
An ordered pair of subsets $(A,B)$ is classified, up to a permutation of the ground set, by the four sizes
\[
|A\cap B|,\quad |A\setminus B|,\quad|B\setminus A|,\quad|A^c\cap B^c|.
\]
Since $|A|=k$ and $|B|=l$ are fixed, these four sizes are determined by $i=|A\cap B|$. Conversely, a bijection of each of the four regions of the ground set gives a permutation carrying any pair with intersection size $i$ to any other such pair.

The possible values of $i$ satisfy
\[
\max(0,k+l-n)\le i\le\min(k,l).
\]
Because $k,l\le n/2$, their sum is at most $n$, so the lower bound is $0$. The number of possibilities is therefore
\[
\boxed{\langle\pi_k,\pi_l\rangle=\min(k,l)+1}.
\]

(b) Set $\pi_{-1}=0$ and define $\chi_0=\pi_0=1$ and $\chi_k=\pi_k-\pi_{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$. Using part (a) four times,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle\chi_k,\chi_l\rangle
={}&\langle\pi_k,\pi_l\rangle
-\langle\pi_{k-1},\pi_l\rangle\\
&-\langle\pi_k,\pi_{l-1}\rangle
+\langle\pi_{k-1},\pi_{l-1}\rangle
=\begin{cases}1,&k=l,\\0,&k\ne l,\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]
with the boundary cases involving $k=0$ or $l=0$ checked directly by the same formula. Thus these virtual characters form an orthonormal set.

Expand one $\chi_k$ in the irreducible-character basis: $\chi_k=\sum_\psi m_\psi\psi$ with \emph{integer} coefficients $m_\psi$, because it is a difference of genuine characters. Its norm is
\[
1=\langle\chi_k,\chi_k\rangle=\sum_\psi m_\psi^2.
\]
Hence exactly one coefficient is $\pm1$ and the others are zero. Its value at the identity is
\[
\chi_k(e)=\binom nk-\binom n{k-1}>0
\]
for $k\le n/2$, so the sign is positive. Therefore each $\chi_k$ is a genuine irreducible character, and the orthogonality above makes them distinct. Their degrees are the displayed binomial differences.

(c) Let $G\le\mathfrak S_n$ be any subgroup. Burnside's lemma says the number of orbits on $k$-subsets is
\[
o_k=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\pi_k(g)
=\langle\pi_k|_G,1_G\rangle_G.
\]
The identity $\pi_k=\chi_0+\cdots+\chi_k$ gives
\[
o_k=\sum_{j=0}^k\langle\chi_j|_G,1_G\rangle_G.
\]
Each $\chi_j|_G$ is the character of a representation of $G$. The inner product with the trivial character is the dimension of the $G$-invariant subspace, a non-negative integer. Therefore
\[
o_k-o_{k-1}=\langle\chi_k|_G,1_G\rangle_G\ge0
\]
for $1\le k\le n/2$. The number of orbits is non-decreasing, proving Livingstone--Wagner's theorem in this range.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:egz}
\idea Over $\mathbb F_p$, the expression $x^{p-1}$ is $0$ at $x=0$ and $1$ everywhere else. Two low-degree equations can therefore select a non-empty set of indices whose size and element sum are both divisible by $p$. Chevalley--Warning produces that selection. A grouping argument then passes from primes to all moduli.

\writeup (a) Let $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ be polynomials in $m$ variables over $\mathbb F_p$, with $\sum_j\deg f_j<m$. Fermat's theorem gives
\[
1-y^{p-1}=\begin{cases}1,&y=0,\\0,&y\ne0,\end{cases}
\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_p.
\]
Thus the number $N$ of common zeros, reduced modulo $p$, equals
\[
N\equiv\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p^m}
\prod_{j=1}^r\bigl(1-f_j(x)^{p-1}\bigr)\pmod p.
\]
The polynomial inside the sum has total degree at most
\[
(p-1)\sum_j\deg f_j<(p-1)m.
\]
Every monomial in its expansion therefore has at least one variable exponent less than $p-1$. When summed over all $m$ variables, its contribution factors into a product of one-variable power sums, and the sum for that exponent is $0$ in $\mathbb F_p$, including exponent $0$ (where it is $p=0$). Every monomial sum vanishes, so $N\equiv0\pmod p$. This proves the systems version of Chevalley--Warning.

(b) Now take $2p-1$ integers $a_1,\ldots,a_{2p-1}$ and reduce them modulo $p$. In $m=2p-1$ variables consider
\[
f_1=\sum_{i=1}^{2p-1}a_ix_i^{p-1},
\qquad
f_2=\sum_{i=1}^{2p-1}x_i^{p-1}.
\]
Their total degree is $2(p-1)=2p-2<m$. The zero vector is one common solution. By part (a), the number of common solutions is divisible by $p$, so it cannot equal $1$; at least one common solution $x$ is non-zero.

Let $I=\{i:x_i\ne0\}$. Then
\[
f_2(x)=|I|\equiv0\pmod p,\qquad
f_1(x)=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
Because $x\ne0$, the set $I$ is non-empty, and because there are only $2p-1$ variables, $1\le|I|\le2p-1$. The only positive multiple of $p$ in this interval is $p$. Thus exactly $p$ of the integers have sum divisible by $p$.

(c) Suppose the theorem holds for positive integers $m$ and $n$. Begin with $2mn-1$ integers. As long as at least $2n-1$ remain, select a disjoint block of $n$ integers with sum divisible by $n$. We can do this $2m-1$ times: before the last selection, the number remaining is
\[
2mn-1-(2m-2)n=2n-1.
\]
Let the selected block sums be $nb_1,\ldots,nb_{2m-1}$. Apply the theorem for $m$ to the $2m-1$ integers $b_i$; choose $m$ of them with sum divisible by $m$. The union of the corresponding $m$ blocks consists of $mn$ integers and has sum
\[
n\sum_{\text{chosen blocks}}b_i
\]
divisible by $mn$. Therefore the theorem holds for $mn$.

It holds for $n=1$ trivially and for all primes by part (b). Factor any positive integer into primes and apply this multiplication step inductively. Thus the theorem holds for every $n\ge1$.

(d) Sharpness is witnessed by $n-1$ zeros and $n-1$ ones. Among any $n$ selected entries there must be at least one of each, so the sum is an integer between $1$ and $n-1$, not divisible by $n$. Therefore the threshold $2n-1$ cannot be lowered.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:abelian}
\idea Classifying abelian $p$-groups amounts to partitioning an exponent. That turns the counting function $A(n)$ into an Euler product. For its average, separate the first factor $\zeta(s)$; its coefficients count divisors of the remaining factor, and the latter has a square-root summatory bound.

\writeup (a) Let
\[
n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}
\]
be its prime factorisation. Every finite abelian group decomposes uniquely as the direct product of its Sylow subgroups. An abelian group of order $p^e$ is a direct product
\[
\Z/p^{\lambda_1}\Z\times\cdots\times\Z/p^{\lambda_t}\Z,
\]
where $\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_t=e$ is a partition of $e$. The classification theorem says that distinct partitions give non-isomorphic groups and every such group occurs. If $p(e)$ denotes the number of partitions of $e$, then
\[
\boxed{A(n)=\prod_{i=1}^rp(e_i)}.
\]

The familiar partition generating function is
\[
\sum_{e\ge0}p(e)x^e
=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac1{1-x^k}.
\]
For real $s>1$ we may put $x=p^{-s}$ and form the absolutely convergent Euler product:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{A(n)}{n^s}
&=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}
\left(\sum_{e\ge0}p(e)p^{-es}\right)\\
&=\prod_p\prod_{k\ge1}(1-p^{-ks})^{-1}\\
&=\boxed{\prod_{k\ge1}\zeta(ks)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Absolute convergence allows us to rearrange these non-negative factors.

(b) Separate the $k=1$ term:
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}A(n)n^{-s}
=\zeta(s)G(s),\qquad
G(s)=\prod_{k\ge2}\zeta(ks)
=\sum_{m\ge1}g(m)m^{-s}.
\]
All coefficients $g(m)$ are non-negative, and multiplication by $\zeta(s)$ means Dirichlet convolution with the constant-one function. Comparing coefficients gives
\[
A(n)=\sum_{m\mid n}g(m).
\]
Therefore for $x\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}A(n)
=\sum_{m\le x}g(m)\left\lfloor\frac xm\right\rfloor.
\]
Write $\lfloor x/m\rfloor=x/m+O(1)$ to obtain
\[
\sum_{n\le x}A(n)
=x\sum_{m\le x}\frac{g(m)}m
+O\!\left(\sum_{m\le x}g(m)\right).
\]

We now bound the second term and the missing tail of the first. Factor
\[
G(s)=\zeta(2s)K(s),\qquad
K(s)=\prod_{k\ge3}\zeta(ks)=\sum_{j\ge1}h(j)j^{-s}.
\]
The coefficients $h(j)$ are non-negative, and $K(1/2)<\infty$ because $ks>1$ for every $k\ge3$ at $s=1/2$, and the product converges.

Since the coefficients of $\zeta(2s)$ are the indicator of perfect squares, Dirichlet convolution gives
\[
g(m)=\sum_{r^2j=m}h(j).
\]
Consequently
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{m\le x}g(m)
&=\sum_{j\le x}h(j)\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac xj}\right\rfloor\\
&\le\sqrt x\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{h(j)}{\sqrt j}
=K(1/2)\sqrt x.
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves that the rounding error is $O(\sqrt x)$.

Let $G_0(y)=\sum_{m\le y}g(m)$. By partial summation and $G_0(y)\le K(1/2)\sqrt y$,
\[
\sum_{m>x}\frac{g(m)}m
\le \int_x^\infty\frac{G_0(y)}{y^2}\,\dd y
\le K(1/2)\int_x^\infty y^{-3/2}\,\dd y
=O(x^{-1/2}).
\]
The first inequality can be seen by writing each $g(m)/m$ as the integral of $g(m)y^{-2}$ over $y\ge m$, and accounting for the endpoint; it is also an immediate consequence of the partial-summation formula.

Thus
\[
\sum_{m\le x}\frac{g(m)}m
=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{g(m)}m+O(x^{-1/2})
=G(1)+O(x^{-1/2}).
\]
Substitution yields
\[
\boxed{\sum_{n\le x}A(n)=cx+O(\sqrt x)},
\qquad
c=G(1)=\boxed{\prod_{k\ge2}\zeta(k)}
=2.2948\ldots.
\]

Dividing by $x$ gives an average of approximately $2.29$ isomorphism classes of abelian groups per positive integer order. The error term controls the average; it does not imply that each individual $A(n)$ is close to $c$.

\tools The classification of finite abelian groups; Euler products and partial summation from Volumes~2 and~6.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:dodecaturns}
\idea A probability law that is constant on conjugacy classes acts as a scalar in each irreducible representation. This is Schur's lemma. Convolution then raises those scalars to powers, and character orthogonality recovers the probability of any final rotation.

\writeup (a) Let $G$ be a finite group and let $f:G\to\C$ be a class function. For an irreducible complex representation $\rho:G\to GL_d(\C)$, form
\[
A_f=\sum_{h\in G}f(h)\rho(h).
\]
For any $g\in G$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho(g)A_f\rho(g)^{-1}
&=\sum_h f(h)\rho(ghg^{-1})\\
&=\sum_k f(g^{-1}kg)\rho(k)=A_f,
\end{aligned}
\]
where the last equality uses that $f$ is constant on conjugacy classes. By Schur's lemma, $A_f$ is scalar: $A_f=c_\rho I$. Taking traces yields
\[
dc_\rho=\sum_h f(h)\chi_\rho(h)=:\widehat f(\chi_\rho),
\]
so
\[
\boxed{A_f=\frac{\widehat f(\chi_\rho)}d I}.
\]

Now let $\mu$ be a probability class function and let $X_t$ be the product of $t$ independent increments with law $\mu$, starting at $e$. Its law is $\mu^{*t}$, with convolution
\[
(f*g)(x)=\sum_{h\in G}f(h)g(h^{-1}x).
\]
Directly changing variables shows $A_{f*g}=A_fA_g$. Hence
\[
A_{\mu^{*t}}=(A_\mu)^t
=\left(\frac{\widehat\mu(\chi_\rho)}d\right)^tI.
\]
Taking traces again gives
\[
\widehat{\mu^{*t}}(\chi_\rho)
=d\left(\frac{\widehat\mu(\chi_\rho)}d\right)^t.
\]

Irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis of the class functions. Fourier inversion for a class function $F$ is
\[
F(g)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{\chi\text{ irreducible}}
\widehat F(\chi)\overline{\chi(g)}.
\]
Insert $F=\mu^{*t}$ to obtain
\[
\boxed{\mu^{*t}(g)=\frac1{|G|}
\sum_\chi\chi(1)\overline{\chi(g)}
\left(\frac{\widehat\mu(\chi)}{\chi(1)}\right)^t}.
\]
At $t=0$, each scalar power is interpreted as $1$, including when the scalar is zero.

(b) The rotation group of the icosahedron is $A_5$, of order $60$. Its irreducible degrees are $1,3,3,4,5$. On the conjugacy class of $20$ three-cycles, the corresponding character values are
\[
1,\quad0,\quad0,\quad1,\quad-1.
\]
Since $\mu$ is uniform on that class, its Fourier scalar in the representation of dimension $d$ is simply the class value divided by $d$. Thus the five scalars are
\[
1,\quad0,\quad0,\quad\frac14,\quad-\frac15.
\]
At $g=e$, each character takes its degree as value. For $t\ge1$, the two zero-scalar terms disappear, and the inversion formula becomes
\[
\boxed{\Pr(X_t=e)
=\frac1{60}\left(1+16\cdot4^{-t}
+25\left(-\frac15\right)^t\right)}.
\]
For $t=1$ this is $0$, as expected because one turn cannot be the identity. For $t=2$ it gives
\[
\frac1{60}(1+1+1)=\frac3{60}=\frac1{20}.
\]
Indeed the second turn must be the inverse of the first; among the $20$ equally likely turns there is exactly one such inverse.

(c) Fix a rotation $g$ through $72^\circ$ or $-72^\circ$ about a vertex axis. These rotations form a conjugacy class of $12$ elements. The degree-$4$ character has value $-1$ on this class, and the degree-$5$ character has value $0$; the two degree-$3$ characters again contribute nothing for $t\ge1$. Hence
\[
\Pr(X_t=g)=\frac1{60}\left(1-4\cdot4^{-t}\right).
\]
There are $12$ such rotations: six axes and two fifth-turn directions along each axis. Summing their equal probabilities gives
\[
\boxed{\Pr(\text{a fifth-turn rotation at time }t)
=\frac15\left(1-4^{1-t}\right)}
\qquad(t\ge1).
\]
At $t=1$ the value is $0$; at $t=2$ it is $3/20$.

For convergence, the Fourier formula gives for any $g\in A_5$ and $t\ge1$
\[
\left|\Pr(X_t=g)-\frac1{60}\right|
\le\frac1{60}\left(16\cdot4^{-t}+25\cdot5^{-t}\right)
\le\frac{41}{60}\,4^{-t},
\]
because every character value has absolute value at most its degree. Thus the distribution converges uniformly to the uniform law, with total-variation distance at most
\[
\frac12\sum_{g\in G}\left|\Pr(X_t=g)-\frac1{60}\right|
\le\frac{41}{2}\,4^{-t}.
\]
This exponential base is sharp: at the identity, the difference from $1/60$ is
\[
\frac{16}{60}\,4^{-t}+\frac{25}{60}\left(-\frac15\right)^t
\sim\frac{16}{60}\,4^{-t}.
\]
So the uniform law is approached at the genuine rate $4^{-t}$, not merely at some unspecified geometric rate.

\tools Chapter~11 (Schur's lemma, character orthogonality and $A_5$ table).
\end{solution}
