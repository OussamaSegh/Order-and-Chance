# Description

Linear algebra is the part of mathematics that works: whenever a problem can be made linear, it can be solved. This book shows how far that idea reaches. It takes the matrices and vector spaces of a first course and follows them to the depth that modern mathematics, physics and computer science actually use.

The journey has twelve stages. Quotients and duality, and the determinant as a volume. Eigenvalues and invariant subspaces, and the Jordan form read off from Young diagrams. Inner products, projections and least squares. The spectral theorem and its relatives, then the singular value decomposition, the best low-rank approximation and why it compresses images. The Perron-Frobenius theorem for positive matrices, which ranks the pages of the web and drives Markov chains. Graphs seen through their eigenvalues: expanders, the friendship theorem, the Hoffman bound and Hao Huang's short proof of the sensitivity conjecture. Symmetry that diagonalises, with the codes of Delsarte. Tensors and the cost of multiplying matrices, from Strassen's surprise to the exponent omega. Inequalities between matrices, from Hadamard and Minkowski to Weyl. Linear dynamics, from Fibonacci to the heavy-ball method that makes learning algorithms converge in a hundred steps instead of ten thousand.

Along the way you will count lozenge tilings and meet the arctic circle, see why random walks forget where they started, and learn why some graphs cannot exist. Every theorem is proved in full. Every chapter opens with a question, asks you to pause and guess before the key results, and ends with graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that starts by explaining how to find the idea. Each chapter closes with sources and further reading.

The book is written for pleasure, in a storytelling style, for readers who know a first course in linear algebra and want to see what it can really do. It comes with a free companion Python notebook for every chapter, with exercises first and solutions after, which tests the theorems, draws the pictures and runs the algorithms.
