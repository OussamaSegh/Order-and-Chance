\chapter{Generating Functions and Random Families}

\begin{center}\itshape
A family name dies out, or it does not. The probability is the smallest root of $s=f(s)$,\\
where $f$ is the generating function of the number of children.
\end{center}

A random variable has a list of probabilities. In Chapter~2, we used that list to calculate an average. Suppose instead that we want the entire law of the sum of twenty independent variables. Writing out every possible outcome is possible, but it becomes a long multiplication problem. A clever way to organise that multiplication is to attach the probability of $X=k$ to the symbol $s^k$.

This produces a function whose coefficients are probabilities. For independent sums, the functions multiply: the ordinary rule $s^i s^j=s^{i+j}$ keeps track of adding the two values. The function also helps when the number of summands is random. Then multiplication becomes composition.

Francis Galton asked a related question in 1873: why can a family name disappear even when each generation may have many children? Henry William Watson studied the question using the same kind of function. We will build the tool with a tiny example first, then follow it all the way to extinction and to a familiar counting problem involving balanced paths.

\section{The probability generating function}

Choose uniformly from four tickets marked $0$, $1$, $1$, and $2$. Let $X$ be the number written on the ticket. Its law is
\[
 P(X=0)=\tfrac14,\qquad P(X=1)=\tfrac12,\qquad P(X=2)=\tfrac14.
\]
Attach a term $p_k s^k$ to each possible value $k$, then add. We obtain
\[
 f(s)=\tfrac14+\tfrac12s+\tfrac14s^2.
\]
This polynomial records all three probabilities. The constant term is the chance of $0$; the coefficient of $s^2$ is the chance of $2$. At $s=1$ the terms add to $1$, the total probability. Figure~\ref{fig:lawpoly} shows the correspondence.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-lawpoly}
\caption{The three values of $X$ become three powers of $s$, with coefficients equal to their probabilities. This is an exact calculation for four tickets labelled $0,1,1,2$, not a fitted curve.}
\label{fig:lawpoly}
\end{figure}

In general, let $X$ take values in $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, and write $p_k=P(X=k)$. Its \emph{probability generating function}, abbreviated \emph{PGF}, is
\[
 f(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k\ge0}p_k s^k,\qquad 0\le s\le1.
\]
At $s=0$, interpret $s^0=1$, including the term $0^0$ in this formal expression: thus $f(0)=p_0$. At $s=1$, $f(1)=\sum_kp_k=1$. For $0\le s<1$, every term satisfies $0\le p_ks^k\le p_k$, so the series converges, even when $X$ has infinitely many possible values. A PGF is an ordinary numerical function on $[0,1]$; we will justify every differentiation of an infinite series before using it.

To use the endpoint $s=1$, we should first know that $f(s)$ approaches $1$ as $s$ approaches $1$. Choose $K$ so that the leftover probability $\sum_{k>K}p_k$ is small. Then, for $0\le s\le1$,
\[
 0\le f(1)-f(s)\le\sum_{k=0}^Kp_k(1-s^k)+\sum_{k>K}p_k.
\]
There are only finitely many terms in the first sum; each tends to $0$ as $s\uparrow1$. The second sum stays small because the total mass equals $1$. Thus $f(s)\to1=f(1)$. The same comparison with powers proves continuity at every point of $[0,1)$, or one may use the uniformly convergent geometric tail on each $[0,r]$ with $r<1$. This is not an informal exchange of an infinite sum and a limit.

Our next goal is to recover the probabilities from the function. Ordinary polynomials reveal their coefficients through derivatives at $0$. The same is true here, but we must justify differentiating the series. At $0$, derivatives mean right-hand derivatives; on $(0,1)$, they are ordinary two-sided derivatives.

\begin{proposition}[the masses are the derivatives at $0$]\label{prop:pgfcoeff}
For every integer $k\ge0$ and every $s\in[0,1)$,
\[
 f^{(k)}(s)=\sum_{n\ge k}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\,p_n s^{n-k},
\]
with the $k=0$ case equal to $f(s)$. In particular $p_k=f^{(k)}(0)/k!$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The case $k=0$ is the series for $f$. Suppose the formula holds for some $k\ge0$, and write $h$ for its right-hand side. The summand of index $n=k$ is the constant $k!\,p_k$. Every later summand has degree $m=n-k\ge1$.

The comparison series does not involve the unknown masses. For $0\le r<1$ and $n\ge k+1$ set
\[
 d_n(r)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k)\,r^{n-k-1}.
\]
When $k=0$ the product is the single factor $n$. For $k\ge1$ it runs from $n$ down to $n-k$, which is $k+1$ positive factors once $n\ge k+1$. The ratio $d_{n+1}(r)/d_n(r)=(n+1)/(n-k)\cdot r$ tends to $r<1$, so $\sum_{n\ge k+1}d_n(r)$ converges by the ratio test in Volume~1, Chapter~3. Since $0\le p_n\le1$, the differentiated series at any point of $[0,r]$ is dominated by the same numerical series.

Fix $0\le s<1$ and $t_0$ with $s<t_0<1$. For $s<t\le t_0$ and $m\ge1$,
\[
 \frac{t^m-s^m}{t-s}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t^{m-1-j}s^j\le m t_0^{m-1},
\]
because each of the $m$ summands is at most $t_0^{m-1}$. For $m=1$ both sides equal $1$. The difference quotient of $h$ is a sum, over $n\ge k+1$ only, of nonnegative terms. For $k\ge1$ the term of index $n$ equals $p_n$ times the falling product of $k$ factors, $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$, times the difference quotient of $t^{n-k}$. That quotient has $m=n-k$ and is at most $m t_0^{m-1}$, so the term is at most $p_n\, n(n-1)\cdots(n-k)\,t_0^{n-k-1}=p_n d_n(t_0)$. For $k=0$ the falling product is empty and equals $1$, and the term is $p_n(t^n-s^n)/(t-s)\le p_n n t_0^{n-1}=p_n d_n(t_0)$. As $t\downarrow s$ that term decreases to
\[
 \beta_n=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k)\,p_n s^{n-k-1},
\]
and $\beta_n\le d_n(t_0)$. Let $Q(t)$ be the quotient and let $L=\sum_{n\ge k+1}\beta_n$, which converges by the comparison just made. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $K\ge k$ so that $\sum_{n>K}d_n(t_0)<\varepsilon$. Then, for every $t\in(s,t_0]$,
\begin{align*}
|Q(t)-L|
&\le\sum_{n>K}d_n(t_0)
 +\Bigl|\sum_{n=k+1}^{K}\bigl(\text{term}_n(t)-\beta_n\bigr)\Bigr|
 +\sum_{n>K}d_n(t_0).
\end{align*}
The middle sum is finite, so it tends to $0$ as $t\downarrow s$. Hence $\limsup_{t\downarrow s}|Q(t)-L|\le 2\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, the right-hand derivative of $h$ at $s$ equals $L$.

Now let $s\in(0,1)$ and $0\le u<s$. The same algebra with the increment running up to $s$ gives
\[
 \frac{s^m-u^m}{s-u}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}s^{m-1-j}u^j\le m s^{m-1}.
\]
The resulting quotient $Q_-(u)$ has terms at most $d_n(s)$, and $\sum d_n(s)$ converges because $s<1$. The identical three-term split, with $u\uparrow s$, shows that the left-hand derivative equals the same series $L$. Thus on $(0,1)$ the derivative is two-sided, and at $0$ only the right-hand derivative is asserted. In both cases it equals the series in the statement at order $k+1$.

At $s=0$ the order-$k$ series reduces to the constant term. The falling product $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$ at $n=k$ equals $k!$, so $f^{(k)}(0)=k!\,p_k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[mean from the derivative]\label{prop:pgfmean}
The limit $\lim_{s\uparrow1}f'(s)$ equals $\mathbb E[X]$, in $[0,\infty]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfcoeff} gives $f'(s)=\sum_{k\ge1}kp_k s^{k-1}$ for $s\in[0,1)$. For $s\in(0,1)$ the terms $k p_k s^{k-1}$ increase to $k p_k$ as $s\uparrow1$. Since every term is nonnegative, $f'(s)\le\sum_{k\ge1}kp_k=\mathbb E[X]$. For every fixed $K$, $f'(s)\ge\sum_{k=1}^K kp_k s^{k-1}$, whose limit is $\sum_{k=1}^K kp_k$. Taking the supremum over $K$ gives the matching lower bound, even when the mean is infinite, by the nonnegative-series convention of Volume~1.
\end{proof}

In particular, if $\mathbb E[X]<\infty$, the difference quotient $(f(1)-f(s))/(1-s)$ equals $f'(\xi_s)$ for some $\xi_s\in(s,1)$, by the mean value theorem in Volume~1, Chapter~3. That quotient tends to $\mathbb E[X]$, so the left-hand derivative $f'(1)$ exists and equals the mean.

Thus two functions on $[0,1]$ that agree as probability generating functions come from the same law: the derivatives at $0$ recover every $p_k$. For the Poisson function $e^{\lambda(s-1)}=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k\ge0}(\lambda s)^k/k!$, the Taylor coefficient of $s^k$ is $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$, which is the Poisson mass. The function was built from the masses, and the derivatives give them back.

\begin{proposition}[variance from the second derivative]\label{prop:pgfvar}
The limit $\lim_{s\uparrow1}f''(s)$ equals $\mathbb E[X(X-1)]$, in $[0,\infty]$. If that limit and $\mathbb E[X]$ are both finite, then $f''(1)$ exists as a left derivative and
\[
 \operatorname{Var}X=f''(1)+f'(1)-\bigl(f'(1)\bigr)^2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfcoeff} gives $f''(s)=\sum_{n\ge2}n(n-1)p_n s^{n-2}$ on $[0,1)$. The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfmean} applies to this series: the terms increase to $n(n-1)p_n$, so $\lim_{s\uparrow1}f''(s)=\mathbb E[X(X-1)]$. Assume that limit and $\mathbb E[X]$ are finite. The paragraph after Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfmean} then says that the left-hand derivative $f'(1)$ exists and equals the mean, and that $f'(s)\to f'(1)$ as $s\uparrow1$. For $s\in(0,1)$ the mean value theorem in Volume~1, Chapter~3, supplies $\xi_s\in(s,1)$ with
\[
 \frac{f'(1)-f'(s)}{1-s}=f''(\xi_s).
\]
As $s\uparrow1$ one has $\xi_s\uparrow1$, so the right-hand side tends to $\mathbb E[X(X-1)]$. Thus the left-hand derivative $f''(1)$ exists and equals that limit. Then $\mathbb E[X^2]=\mathbb E[X(X-1)]+\mathbb E[X]$, and the variance identity is the definition in Chapter~2.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[three laws]\label{ex:pgfexamples}
A Bernoulli variable of parameter $p$ has $f(s)=1-p+ps$. A binomial count of $n$ trials has $f(s)=(1-p+ps)^n$, by the binomial theorem. A Poisson variable of parameter $\lambda$ has
\[
 f(s)=\sum_{k\ge0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}s^k=e^{\lambda(s-1)}.
\]
Differentiating the Poisson function gives $f'(s)=\lambda e^{\lambda(s-1)}$ and $f''(s)=\lambda^2 e^{\lambda(s-1)}$, so $f'(1)=\lambda$ and $f''(1)=\lambda^2$. Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfvar} returns $\operatorname{Var}X=\lambda^2+\lambda-\lambda^2=\lambda$, which matches Volume~1. For the binomial function, $f'(s)=np(1-p+ps)^{n-1}$ and $f''(s)=n(n-1)p^2(1-p+ps)^{n-2}$. At $s=1$ the variance formula becomes
\[
 n(n-1)p^2+np-(np)^2=np(1-p).
\]
\end{example}

\section{Sums}

Two fair coins, scored as $0$ or $1$, have four outcomes of mass $1/4$. The sum is $0$, $1$, or $2$ with masses $1/4$, $1/2$, $1/4$. Each coin has generating function $\tfrac12+\tfrac12 s$, and the product is
\[
 \Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac12 s\Bigr)^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac12 s+\tfrac14 s^2,
\]
the generating function of the sum. The next proposition is this multiplication with the masses left general.

\begin{proposition}[independent sums]\label{prop:pgfsum}
If $X$ and $Y$ are independent and nonnegative integer valued, the generating function of $X+Y$ is the product of the generating functions. The same holds for any finite independent sum.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Independence gives $P(X=i,Y=j)=P(X=i)P(Y=j)$. Therefore
\[
 \mathbb E[s^{X+Y}]=\sum_{i,j}P(X=i)P(Y=j)s^{i+j}=\mathbb E[s^X]\,\mathbb E[s^Y].
\]
The double sum is absolutely convergent for $s\in[0,1]$, so the product of the sums is the sum of the products.
\end{proof}

A binomial count is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli variables, and $(1-p+ps)^n$ is the product of the factors $1-p+ps$. A sum of independent Poisson variables of parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$ has generating function $e^{\lambda(s-1)}e^{\mu(s-1)}=e^{(\lambda+\mu)(s-1)}$, which is the Poisson$(\lambda+\mu)$ function. That is the generating-function proof of Exercise~\ref{ex:twopoisson}.

The product becomes a composition when the number of summands is itself random. Take $N$ with masses $P(N=0)=\tfrac12$, $P(N=1)=P(N=2)=\tfrac14$, and let each summand be a fair Bernoulli, independent of $N$ and of the other summand. The generating function of $N$ is $g(u)=\tfrac12+\tfrac14 u+\tfrac14 u^2$, and $f(s)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12 s$, so
\begin{align*}
 g(f(s))
 &=\tfrac12+\tfrac14\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac12 s\Bigr)+\tfrac14\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac12 s\Bigr)^2\\
 &=\tfrac12+\tfrac18+\tfrac18 s+\tfrac1{16}\bigl(1+2s+s^2\bigr)
 =\tfrac{11}{16}+\tfrac14 s+\tfrac1{16}s^2.
\end{align*}
The same masses come from listing the outcomes. The case $N=0$ contributes $\tfrac12$ to $\{S=0\}$. The case $N=1$ contributes $\tfrac18$ to $\{S=0\}$ and $\tfrac18$ to $\{S=1\}$. The case $N=2$ contributes $\tfrac1{16}$, $\tfrac18$, and $\tfrac1{16}$ to $\{S=0,1,2\}$. Adding gives $11/16$, $1/4$, and $1/16$.

\begin{proposition}[random sums]\label{prop:randomsum}
Let $N$ be nonnegative and integer valued. Conditional on $N=n$, draw $n$ independent, identically distributed nonnegative integer-valued variables with a fixed common law that does not depend on $n$. Let $S$ be their sum, with $S=0$ when $n=0$. If $g$ is the generating function of $N$ and $f$ is the generating function of one summand, then $S$ has generating function $g(f(s))$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Condition on $N=n$. For $n=0$, $\mathbb E[s^S\mid N=0]=1$. For $n\ge1$, independence and Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfsum} give $\mathbb E[s^S\mid N=n]=f(s)^n$. Therefore
\[
 \mathbb E[s^S]=\sum_{n\ge0}P(N=n)f(s)^n=g\bigl(f(s)\bigr).
\]
Sample $N$ first, then draw its finite list of summands. The outcomes are a countable union of finite lists, so the calculation is a nonnegative sum on a countable space. No infinite product is required. The masses are $P(N=n)$ times the conditional list masses, and they add to $\sum_n P(N=n)=1$.
\end{proof}

If $N$ is Poisson of parameter $\lambda$, then $g(u)=e^{\lambda(u-1)}$ and the sum has generating function $e^{\lambda(f(s)-1)}$. Exercise~\ref{ex:thinning} is the case in which each summand is a Bernoulli trial that decides whether a point is kept.

\section{Galton--Watson extinction}

Start with one individual. Each individual has a number of children distributed as $X$, independently of everyone else, with $P(X=k)=p_k$ and generating function $f$. Let $Z_n$ be the number of individuals in generation $n$, so $Z_0=1$ and, given $Z_n=m$, the next generation is a sum of $m$ independent copies of $X$. Let $\eta=P(Z_n=0\text{ for some }n)$ be the probability that the line dies out.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-familytree}
\caption{A possible family tree in the $0$-or-$2$-children model. One first-generation branch ends, while the other divides into two independent subfamilies. This is one possible outcome, not an observed frequency.}
\label{fig:familytree}
\end{figure}

The picture is one outcome. The size of a generation has a mean whether or not that outcome dies.

\begin{proposition}[generation means]\label{prop:genmean}
Let $m=\mathbb E[X]$ be finite. Then $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$ for every $n\ge0$. If the mean of $X$ is infinite, then $\mathbb E[Z_1]=\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The case $n=0$ is $\mathbb E[Z_0]=1$. Given $Z_n=k$, generation $n+1$ is a sum of $k$ independent copies of $X$, so its conditional mean is $km$. Therefore
\[
 \mathbb E[Z_{n+1}]=\sum_{k\ge0}P(Z_n=k)\,km=m\,\mathbb E[Z_n],
\]
and $\mathbb E[Z_n]=m^n$ follows by induction. If $\sum_k kp_k=\infty$, the same split at $n=0$ gives $\mathbb E[Z_1]=\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
Each person has two children with probability $2/3$ and none with probability $1/3$. The mean number of children is $4/3$, so $\mathbb E[Z_n]=(4/3)^n$ grows. Must the surname survive?
\end{think}

\begin{theorem}[extinction]\label{thm:extinction}
The extinction probability $\eta$ is the smallest root of $s=f(s)$ in $[0,1]$. If $\mathbb E[X]\le1$ and $P(X=1)<1$, then $\eta=1$. If $\mathbb E[X]>1$, then $\eta<1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\eta_n=P(Z_n=0)$. Then $\eta_0=0$ and $\eta_1=p_0=f(0)$. Conditioning on the first generation, $Z_n$ is the sum of $Z_1$ independent copies of a process with the same law, so $\eta_{n}=f(\eta_{n-1})$ for $n\ge1$. Since $f$ is increasing on $[0,1]$ and $\eta_1=f(0)\ge0=\eta_0$, induction gives $\eta_n\le\eta_{n+1}\le1$. The limit $L=\lim\eta_n$ satisfies $L=f(L)$ by continuity of $f$.

Extinction by time $n$ depends only on the first $n$ finite generations, whose joint laws are determined by the offspring masses. Let $D_j$ be the event of \emph{first} extinction in generation $j$. These events are disjoint and $\eta_n=\sum_{j=1}^nP(D_j)$. The event of eventual extinction is their countable union, with probability defined by the nonnegative sum $\eta=\sum_{j\ge1}P(D_j)$. Hence $\eta=\lim_n\eta_n=L$. No conclusion about an increasing union is drawn from finite additivity alone.

Now let $r\in[0,1]$ be any root of $s=f(s)$. Then $\eta_0=0\le r$, and if $\eta_{n-1}\le r$ then $\eta_n=f(\eta_{n-1})\le f(r)=r$. So $L\le r$. The limit is the smallest root.

It remains to locate that root. The function $f(s)-s$ has $f(1)-1=0$. Suppose $\mathbb E[X]=m$ exists in $[0,\infty]$. If $m>1$, then the nonnegative-series identity $(1-f(s))/(1-s)=\sum_{k\ge1}p_k(1+s+\cdots+s^{k-1})$ tends to $m$ as $s\uparrow1$ (by the same finite-partial-sum argument as Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfmean}), so $1-f(s)>1-s$ for $s$ close to $1$, that is $f(s)<s$ on a left neighbourhood of $1$. Combined with $f(0)\ge0$, the intermediate-value theorem supplies a root in $[0,1)$. The smallest root is then strictly less than $1$, so $\eta<1$.

If $m<1$, the same expansion gives
\[
 \frac{1-f(s)}{1-s}=\sum_{k\ge1}p_k(1+s+\cdots+s^{k-1})\le\sum_{k\ge1}kp_k=m<1
\]
for $s\in[0,1)$, so $f(s)>s$. There is no root in $[0,1)$, and $\eta=1$.

If $m=1$ and $P(X=1)<1$, then for $s\in[0,1)$,
\[
 \frac{1-f(s)}{1-s}=\sum_{k\ge1}p_k(1+s+\cdots+s^{k-1}).
\]
Each inner sum is at most $k$, and it is strictly smaller than $k$ whenever $k\ge2$. The hypothesis $P(X=1)<1$ together with mean $1$ forces some $p_k>0$ for a $k\ne1$. The case of mass only at $0$ and $1$ with mean $1$ is $p_1=1$, which is excluded. So the displayed average is strictly less than $\sum k p_k=1$, hence $f(s)>s$ on $[0,1)$. There is no root in $[0,1)$, and $\eta=1$.

If $P(X=1)=1$, then $f(s)=s$, every point is a root, the smallest root is $0$, and the population stays at $1$, so $\eta=0$. The hypothesis $P(X=1)<1$ excludes this case from the sentence $\eta=1$.
\end{proof}

When $m<1$ the generation means give a second proof that does not locate the root. Write $S_N=Z_0+\cdots+Z_N$, so $\mathbb E[S_N]=\sum_{n=0}^N m^n$ increases to $1/(1-m)$. The partial sums increase to the total progeny $T$, which may be infinite. Volume~1's tail sum gives $\mathbb E[S_N]=\sum_{k\ge0}P(S_N>k)$. For each fixed $k$, the events $\{S_N>k\}$ increase to $\{T>k\}$, so
\[
 \sum_{k=0}^{M-1}P(T>k)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}P(S_N>k)\le\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb E[S_N]=\frac1{1-m}.
\]
The left side is at least $M\,P(T=\infty)$, because $T>k$ whenever $T=\infty$. Therefore $P(T=\infty)\le 1/((1-m)M)$ for every integer $M\ge1$. Let $M\to\infty$. Thus $T$ is finite almost surely and $\eta=1$. The same sum of means diverges when $m\ge1$, and that divergence does not decide extinction. One child for certain has $m=1$ and $\eta=0$. The next two examples are a supercritical law, where the name survives with positive probability, and a critical law, where it dies almost surely.

Chapter~14 uses the same branching count on a sparse random graph. There a first-moment count of spanning trees bounds the expected number of components of one fixed size only while that size is at most $n^{1/3}$. The conclusion that every component has order $\log n$ is the later exploration estimate. The survival probability $1-\eta$ is the natural guess for the fraction of vertices in a giant component. What that chapter proves in that direction is a positive lower bound on the chance that one fixed vertex lies in a linear-sized component.

\begin{example}[two children or none]\label{ex:binary}
Suppose $P(X=0)=1-p$ and $P(X=2)=p$, with $0<p\le1$. Then $f(s)=1-p+ps^2$ and $\mathbb E[X]=2p$. The fixed-point equation is
\[
 f(s)-s=1-p+ps^2-s=(1-s)\bigl(1-p(1+s)\bigr).
\]
The roots are $s=1$ and, when $p>0$, $s=(1-p)/p$. If $p\le1/2$, then $(1-p)/p\ge1$, so the smallest root in $[0,1]$ is $1$ and the line dies almost surely. If $p>1/2$, the smallest root is $(1-p)/p<1$. For $p=2/3$ that root is $1/2$.

That answers the question above. The mean generation size grows like $(4/3)^n$, and still $P(Z_n=0)$ increases to $1/2$. The lines that are still alive carry the mean: since $P(Z_n>0)\to1/2$,
\[
 \mathbb E[Z_n\mid Z_n>0]=\frac{(4/3)^n}{P(Z_n>0)}
\]
is asymptotic to $2\,(4/3)^n$. Growth of the expectation is not the same event as survival.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[three equally likely family sizes]\label{ex:thirds}
Let $p_0=p_1=p_2=\tfrac13$. Then $f(s)=(1+s+s^2)/3$ and
\[
 f'(s)=\frac{1+2s}3,\qquad f'(1)=1.
\]
Also $p_1=\tfrac13<1$. Theorem~\ref{thm:extinction} gives $\eta=1$. The same conclusion is the factorisation
\[
 f(s)-s=\frac{1-2s+s^2}3=\frac{(1-s)^2}3,
\]
so $f(s)>s$ on $[0,1)$ and the only root in $[0,1]$ is $1$. This is not the law of Exercise~\ref{ex:lotka}. There the masses are $\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac14$, and the fixed-point equation has a double root at $1$ for a different reason.
\end{example}

The proof of the theorem builds $\eta$ as the limit of $\eta_{n+1}=f(\eta_n)$ starting at $\eta_0=0$. Figure~\ref{fig:cobweb} draws that staircase for two laws with offspring in $\{0,1,2\}$.

\section{Catalan numbers}

Let $C_0=1$ and, for $n\ge1$, let $C_n$ be the number of Dyck paths of semilength $n$, from Exercise~\ref{ex:catalan}. For semilength $3$ the five paths are
\[
 XXXYYY,\quad XXYXYY,\quad XXYYXY,\quad XYXXYY,\quad XYXYXY.
\]
Each stays nonnegative and ends at height $0$. There is no sixth.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-contour}
\caption{A plane tree on $9$ vertices and the contour walk around it, a Dyck path of $16$ steps, so of semilength $8$. The path first returns to the axis at step $6$, and that step is a $Y$. Between the opening $X$ and this $Y$ is a Dyck path of semilength $2$. After the $Y$ is a Dyck path of semilength $5$. Those two paths are the factors $C_2$ and $C_5$ in the recurrence for $C_8$.}
\label{fig:contour}
\end{figure}

A nonempty Dyck path starts with $X$. After that opening step, the first time the tallies of $X$ and $Y$ match again is a later step, and that step is a $Y$. Between the opening $X$ and that $Y$ sits a Dyck path, possibly empty. After that $Y$ sits another. Figure~\ref{fig:contour} is one plane tree. Its contour first returns to the axis at step $6$, and that step is a $Y$. The four steps between the opening $X$ and this $Y$ are a Dyck path of semilength $2$. The ten steps after the $Y$ are a Dyck path of semilength $5$. The walk up to the return, opening $X$ and closing $Y$ included, is a Dyck path of semilength $3$, which is not one of the two factors. Therefore
\[
 C_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}C_i C_{n-1-i},\qquad n\ge1.
\]
Since $0\le C_n\le\binom{2n}{n}\le4^n$, the series $c(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_nx^n$ converges absolutely for $|x|<1/4$. We may multiply its series and collect terms there. Multiply the recurrence by $x^n$ and sum:
\[
 c(x)-1=x\,c(x)^2.
\]
The quadratic equation $x c^2-c+1=0$ has solutions $(1\pm\sqrt{1-4x})/(2x)$ for $x\ne0$ and $|x|<1/4$, where the square root denotes the positive root. Rationalise the minus sign:
\[
 \frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}=\frac{1-(1-4x)}{2x\bigl(1+\sqrt{1-4x}\bigr)}=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1-4x}}.
\]
The right-hand side tends to $1$ as $x\to0$ from either side. The plus sign is $(1+\sqrt{1-4x})/(2x)$, which is unbounded as $x\to0$. Thus the generating function, which stays bounded near $0$, is
\[
 c(x)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x},\qquad 0<|x|<\frac14,
\]
and $c(0)=1$. Exercise~\ref{ex:binseries} identifies the series of $(1-4x)^{-1/2}$ and recovers $C_n=\binom{2n}{n}/(n+1)$ from this formula, which matches the count in Chapter~1.

The first-step split is the same idea as a random walk's first return. Chapter~8 counts those returns. The generating function here is only the enumeration.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:thinning}
Each point of a Poisson$(\lambda)$ count is kept with probability $p$, independently. Show that the number kept is Poisson$(\lambda p)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:golden}
In Example~\ref{ex:binary} with $p=2/3$, the extinction probability is $1/2$. Check the fixed-point equation numerically at $s=1/2$, and compute the mean number of children.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:lotka}
Take $p_0=1/4$, $p_1=1/2$, $p_2=1/4$. Find the extinction probability and the mean.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:randomsumvar}
Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:randomsum}, assume $\mathbb E[X_1^2]$ and $\mathbb E[N^2]$ are finite. Show that $\mathbb E[S]=\mathbb E[N]\,\mathbb E[X_1]$ and
\[
 \operatorname{Var}S=\mathbb E[N]\,\operatorname{Var}X_1+\operatorname{Var}N\,(\mathbb E[X_1])^2.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:progeny}
Let $T=Z_0+Z_1+\cdots$ be the total progeny in a Galton--Watson process, including the founder, and let $h(s)=\mathbb E[s^T\mathbf 1_{T<\infty}]$. Show that $h(s)=s f(h(s))$ for $s\in[0,1]$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:firstpassage}
A walk on the integers starts at $0$ and steps $+1$ with probability $p\in(0,1)$ and $-1$ with probability $q=1-p$, independently. Let $\tau$ be the first time the walk reaches $+1$, or $\infty$ if it never does. Let $F(s)=\mathbb E[s^\tau\mathbf 1_{\tau<\infty}]$. Show that $F(s)=ps+qs F(s)^2$ for $s\in[0,1)$, and that $P(\tau<\infty)=\min(1,p/q)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:binseries}
Let $a_n=\binom{2n}{n}$ and $g(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n x^n$ for $|x|<1/4$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $a_n=2(2n-1)a_{n-1}/n$ for $n\ge1$, and deduce $(1-4x)g'(x)=2g(x)$.
\item Conclude that $g(x)=(1-4x)^{-1/2}$.
\item Show that $\binom{-1/2}{j}(-4)^j=\binom{2j}{j}$ for $j\ge0$.
\item From $c(x)=(1-\sqrt{1-4x})/(2x)$ and part~(b), recover $C_n=\binom{2n}{n}/(n+1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:poissongw}
Let the offspring law be Poisson$(c)$ with $c>1$, and let $\rho=1-\eta$ be the survival probability. Show that $\rho$ is the unique root in $(0,1]$ of $1-\rho=e^{-c\rho}$, and that $\rho\sim 2(c-1)$ as $c\downarrow1$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{hint}{ex:thinning}
Condition on the Poisson count. Each point of a Poisson count is kept with probability $p$, so the exponent $\lambda(s-1)$ becomes $\lambda p(s-1)$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:golden}
Substitute $s=1/2$ into $1-p+ps^2$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:lotka}
The equation $s=(1+s)^2/4$ has a double root at $1$. The mean is $1$, and the law is not concentrated at $1$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:randomsumvar}
Differentiate $g(f(s))$, or condition on $N$ and use $\mathbb E[S\mid N]=N\mathbb E[X_1]$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:progeny}
The founder contributes the factor $s$, and each of their children starts an independent copy of the total progeny.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:firstpassage}
The first step is $+1$, and then $\tau=1$, or the first step is $-1$ and the walk must reach $0$ and then $+1$, two independent copies of $\tau$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:binseries}
The ratio $a_n/a_{n-1}$ is the coefficient relation that $(1-4x)g'=2g$ asks for. Then differentiate $(1-4x)^{1/2}g(x)$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:poissongw}
Set $\rho=1-s$ in $s=e^{c(s-1)}$. For the near-critical slope, first show $\rho(c)\to0$ as $c\downarrow1$, then use the second-order Taylor expansion of $e^{-c\rho}$. Direct implicit differentiation at the critical point is singular and is not a proof of the asymptotic.
\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:thinning}
\idea A binomial thinning of a Poisson generating function is a shift of the exponent.
\writeup Given $N=n$, the number kept is binomial$(n,p)$, with generating function $(1-p+ps)^n$. Remove the conditioning:
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}(1-p+ps)^n=\exp\bigl(\lambda(1-p+ps)-\lambda\bigr)=\exp(\lambda p(s-1)).
\]
That is the Poisson$(\lambda p)$ function.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:golden}
\idea One substitution checks a root. The mean is the derivative, or a direct sum.
\writeup With $p=2/3$, $f(1/2)=1-2/3+(2/3)(1/4)=1/3+1/6=1/2$. The mean is $2p=4/3>1$, in agreement with survival probability $1-1/2=1/2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:lotka}
\idea The mean equals $1$ and the distribution is not a single child for certain, so Theorem~\ref{thm:extinction} gives extinction probability $1$.
\writeup $f(s)=\tfrac14+\tfrac12 s+\tfrac14 s^2=\bigl((1+s)/2\bigr)^2$. Then $f'(s)=(1+s)/2$ and $f'(1)=1$. Since $p_1=1/2<1$, the theorem yields $\eta=1$. The equation $s=f(s)$ is $(1-s)^2=0$, a double root at $1$, so there is no smaller root in $[0,1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:randomsumvar}
\idea Condition on $N$. The conditional mean is linear in $N$, and the conditional variance is $N$ times one variance.
\writeup Given $N=n$, $S$ is a sum of $n$ independent copies, so $\mathbb E[S\mid N=n]=n\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N=n)=n\sigma^2$, where $\mu=\mathbb E[X_1]$ and $\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}X_1$. Therefore $\mathbb E[S]=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[S\mid N]]=\mu\mathbb E[N]$. The law of total variance, which is the computation
\[
 \mathbb E[S^2]=\mathbb E\bigl[\mathbb E[S^2\mid N]\bigr]=\mathbb E\bigl[\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N)+(\mathbb E[S\mid N])^2\bigr]
 =\sigma^2\mathbb E[N]+\mu^2\mathbb E[N^2],
\]
gives $\operatorname{Var}S=\sigma^2\mathbb E[N]+\mu^2\mathbb E[N^2]-\mu^2(\mathbb E[N])^2=\mathbb E[N]\sigma^2+(\mathbb E[X_1])^2\operatorname{Var}N$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:progeny}
\idea One individual, then a random number of independent copies of the same total.
\writeup If the founder has $k$ children, the total progeny is $1$ plus $k$ independent copies of $T$. The whole line is finite exactly when every one of those copies is finite. Independence multiplies the factors, so each finite copy contributes a factor $h(s)$, and the founder contributes the factor $s$:
\[
 \mathbb E[s^T\mathbf 1_{T<\infty}]=\sum_{k\ge0}p_k s\,\bigl(h(s)\bigr)^k=s f\bigl(h(s)\bigr).
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:firstpassage}
\idea One step up finishes the wait. One step down leaves two successive climbs, each distributed as $\tau$.
\writeup With probability $p$ the first step is $+1$ and $\tau=1$. With probability $q$ the first step is $-1$, the walk is at $-1$, and the time to reach $+1$ is a copy of $\tau$ to get back to $0$, plus an independent copy to reach $+1$. Therefore
\[
 F(s)=ps\cdot1+qs\,F(s)\,F(s).
\]
The quadratic $qs F^2-F+ps=0$ gives $F(s)=\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-4pqs^2}\bigr)/(2qs)$ for $q>0$, taking the root that vanishes at $s=0$. Let $s\uparrow1$. Then $P(\tau<\infty)=F(1-)$ equals the smaller of $1$ and the value $(1-|p-q|)/(2q)$. If $p\ge q$, then $|p-q|=p-q$ and $(1-(p-q))/(2q)=(1-p+q)/(2q)=(2q)/(2q)=1$. If $p<q$, then $|p-q|=q-p$ and $(1-(q-p))/(2q)=(1-q+p)/(2q)=(2p)/(2q)=p/q$. So $P(\tau<\infty)=\min(1,p/q)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:binseries}
\idea The central binomial coefficients satisfy a first-order ratio. That ratio is a differential equation, and the equation has one bounded solution.
\writeup (a) For $n\ge1$,
\[
 \frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}}=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2}\cdot\frac{((n-1)!)^2}{(2n-2)!}=\frac{(2n)(2n-1)}{n^2}=\frac{2(2n-1)}{n}.
\]
Hence $n a_n=2(2n-1)a_{n-1}=(4n-2)a_{n-1}$. On $|x|<1/4$ the ratio test gives a positive radius of convergence $1/4$, because $a_n/a_{n-1}\to4$. The differentiated series needs its own check. Volume~1 does not prove termwise differentiation of a general power series, and the bound $p_n\le1$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfcoeff} does not cover $a_n=\binom{2n}{n}$. For $|x|\le r<1/4$ and for an increment small enough that $|x|$ and $|x+h|$ are at most some $r'$ with $r<r'<1/4$, the difference quotient of $x^n$ is at most $n(r')^{n-1}$. The series $\sum n a_n (r')^{n-1}$ converges: the ratio of successive terms tends to $4r'<1$. The tail argument in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pgfcoeff} therefore differentiates $g$ term by term on $|x|\le r$. The coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ in $(1-4x)g'(x)$ is $n a_n-4(n-1)a_{n-1}=(4n-2)a_{n-1}-4(n-1)a_{n-1}=2a_{n-1}$, which is the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ in $2g(x)$.

(b) Let $u(x)=(1-4x)^{1/2}g(x)$ for $|x|<1/4$. Differentiating the product and using $g'=2g/(1-4x)$ gives
\[
 u'=(-2)(1-4x)^{-1/2}g+(1-4x)^{1/2}\cdot\frac{2g}{1-4x}
 =(-2)(1-4x)^{-1/2}g+2(1-4x)^{-1/2}g=0.
\]
So $u$ is constant. At $x=0$ it equals $1$. Therefore $g(x)=(1-4x)^{-1/2}$.

(c) 
\[
 \binom{-1/2}{j}=\frac{\bigl(-\tfrac12\bigr)\bigl(-\tfrac32\bigr)\cdots\bigl(\tfrac12-j\bigr)}{j!}
 =\frac{(-1)^j\,1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2j-1)}{2^j\,j!}.
\]
Multiply by $(-4)^j=(-1)^j 2^{2j}$:
\[
 \binom{-1/2}{j}(-4)^j=\frac{2^j\,1\cdot3\cdots(2j-1)}{j!}.
\]
And $\binom{2j}{j}=(2j)!/(j!)^2$ equals the same quantity, because $(2j)!=2^j j!\,1\cdot3\cdots(2j-1)$.

(d) From the chapter, $xc(x)=(1-\sqrt{1-4x})/2$. Differentiate:
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\bigl(xc(x)\bigr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4x}}=g(x).
\]
Since $0\le C_n\le4^n$, the same ratio test shows that $\sum (n+1)C_n r^n$ converges for every $r<1/4$. The argument of part~(a) therefore differentiates the series $xc(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_n x^{n+1}$ term by term, and the derivative is $\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)C_n x^n$. Comparing coefficients with $g$ gives $(n+1)C_n=\binom{2n}{n}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:poissongw}
\idea The Poisson generating function is exponential, so the fixed-point equation is transcendental and still elementary.
\writeup Here $f(s)=\exp(c(s-1))$. The equation $s=f(s)$ is $s=e^{c(s-1)}$. Set $\rho=1-s$. Then $1-\rho=\exp(-c\rho)$. At $\rho=0$ both sides equal $1$. The derivative of the right side at $0$ is $-c e^{0}=-c$, and the derivative of the left side is $-1$. For $c>1$ the graphs meet again in $(0,1)$: consider $\phi(\rho)=e^{-c\rho}-(1-\rho)$. Then $\phi(0)=0$, $\phi'(0)=1-c<0$, so $\phi$ is negative just to the right of $0$, and $\phi(1)=e^{-c}>0$, so a root exists in $(0,1)$. Uniqueness: $\phi''(\rho)=c^2 e^{-c\rho}>0$, so $\phi'$ has at most one zero and $\phi$ has at most two zeros. One is at $0$, so there is exactly one in $(0,1)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:extinction} says $\eta$ is the smallest root of $s=f(s)$ in $[0,1]$, hence $\eta=1-\rho$ for this $\rho$, and the survival probability is $\rho$.

For the near-critical slope, avoid differentiating the root branch at the singular point $c=1$. Instead expand $e^{-c\rho}=1-c\rho+c^2\rho^2/2+O(\rho^3)$ for $c\in[1,2]$ and $\rho\in[0,1]$, with a uniform remainder. Then $1-\rho=1-c\rho+c^2\rho^2/2+O(\rho^3)$, so $\rho(c-1)=c^2\rho^2/2+O(\rho^3)$. For $\rho\ne0$, $c-1=c^2\rho/2+O(\rho^2)$. As $c\downarrow1$, $\rho\to0$: any subsequential limit $r\in[0,1]$ would solve $1-r=e^{-r}$, whose only solution is $r=0$ (since $e^{-r}>1-r$ for $r>0$). The Taylor remainder is uniform for $c\in[1,2]$ and $0\le\rho\le1$, so the preceding identity then gives $\rho\sim2(c-1)$.
\end{solution}
