\chapter*{Preface}

Imagine tossing a fair coin again and again. You are waiting for the first head. It might come on the first toss. It might take ten. What is the chance that you will still be waiting after three tosses?

You must have seen three tails: TTT. Because we assume the tosses are independent, the first three tosses have eight equally likely strings, and TTT is one of them. Its chance is therefore $1/8$. To be waiting after $n$ tosses, all $n$ must have been tails. Independence lets us multiply their probabilities, so
\[
  P(\text{the first $n$ tosses are tails})=\underbrace{\frac12\cdot\frac12\cdots\frac12}_{n\text{ factors}}=2^{-n}.
\]
Each fresh toss halves the chance of still waiting.

Now ask a stranger question. What if the head never comes? We can write the endless string TTT$\cdots$, so the outcome is not ruled out by the words of the experiment. Yet the chance of matching its first $n$ tosses is $2^{-n}$, which becomes as small as we please. Should the endless string have probability zero? What would \emph{probability zero} mean for an outcome that is still possible?

The calculation for three tosses is easy. The question about infinitely many tosses is different. To answer it, we need a model that gives meaning to the whole sequence, not just its first few steps. That is where Chapter~1 begins.

\section*{What we already know}

Volume~1, \emph{First Principles}, taught us to count carefully, distinguish evidence from proof, work with limits and integrals, and assign probabilities to finite experiments and some countable ones. Volume~2, \emph{The Integers}, showed how simple arithmetic questions can hide patterns. Volume~3, \emph{Symmetry}, gave us ways to study permutations and the structures that survive a change of order. Those ideas are useful when we shuffle a deck or count its cycles.

\textbf{Your only prerequisites for this book are high-school mathematics and Volumes~1--3.} You do not need a separate university course in probability, statistics, measure theory, or stochastic processes. Nor do you need a result from a later volume. When we need a new tool, such as a probability law for infinitely many tosses, an integral over a region, or a rule for conditioning on past information, we build the version we use and state its limits. A useful picture or an approximation never replaces a proof.

\section*{Where the questions lead}

The first part, \emph{The Language of Chance}, puts the outcomes and their probabilities on firm ground. A random variable then turns an outcome into a number: how many tosses we waited, how many cycles a permutation has, or how many boxes are still empty. Generating functions let us calculate with whole families of such numbers.

In \emph{The Laws of Uncertainty}, we ask why the totals of many random choices can become predictable. We prove inequalities that control large deviations, and we find out exactly when a bell-shaped curve is a good approximation. Entropy asks a different question: how many bits does a message really need? The answer begins with the probabilities of its symbols.

The third part, \emph{Chance in Motion}, lets the experiment change with time. A random walk takes steps left and right. A Markov chain remembers its present state, but not the whole route by which it arrived there. Events can arrive at random times; a fair game may be stopped; a continuous random path can be built from ordinary normal variables. At each turn we ask what has been assumed, and which conclusion the assumptions earn.

The final part, \emph{Chance Reveals Structure}, turns the question around. Instead of predicting what randomness will do, we sometimes use a random choice to prove that a particular, perfectly definite object must exist. Random graphs give us a place to watch order emerge from independent choices of edges.

\section*{Reading with a pencil}

Try the small case before the general proof. Draw a path, list the outcomes, or check the first few terms of a sum. A \emph{Pause and think} question is there so that you can make a guess before you see the argument. The figures are there to show a mathematical step, not to stand in for it. When a drawing is schematic rather than observed data, its caption says so.

The exercises use one star for a short check, two for a problem that needs several ideas, and three for a longer investigation. Hints and worked solutions are there when you need them, but try the question first. The most useful habit from the earlier volumes remains the same: ask why each step follows, and go back to the definition when it does not.

Volume~5, \emph{Calculation: From Formula to Insight}, takes up the work of computing and approximating the quantities mathematics defines. Before that, we need to know which quantities our random experiment has actually defined.

Start with two independent tosses of a fair coin. Someone says that the possible numbers of heads are $0$, $1$, and $2$, so each should have probability $1/3$. The three labels are real, but the conclusion is wrong. Chapter~1 begins by counting the outcomes behind those three labels.
