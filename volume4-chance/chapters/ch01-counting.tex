\chapter{From Games to Probability Spaces}

\begin{center}\itshape
Before a fraction is a probability, the outcomes have to be named.\\
One uniform number in $[0,1)$ already contains every toss of an infinite sequence.
\end{center}

Volume~1, Chapter~10, built probability for a finite set, and for one waiting time on a countable set. It left a single space that holds every toss. That space is built below from binary digits. The ballot theorem then shows what a fully named finite model can decide. Bertrand's three chords and Buffon's needle use a continuous sample, so they wait until Chapter~5, next to densities.

\section{Finite experiments, recalled}

A finite experiment is a nonempty finite set $\Omega$ and masses $p(\omega)\ge0$ with $\sum_\omega p(\omega)=1$. An event is a subset. Its probability is the sum of the masses it contains. Volume~1, Chapter~10, records the rules we use without re-proving them: the complement, finite additivity for disjoint events, conditional probability
\[
 P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\qquad\text{when }P(B)>0,
\]
Bayes' formula, and the distinction between pairwise and mutual independence. A uniform model is the special case $p(\omega)=1/|\Omega|$. Uniformity is an assumption about the experiment. A list of names for the outcomes does not create it.

The same chapter proves linearity of expectation, including the case of dependent indicators, and Chebyshev's inequality. Those tools come back in Chapter~2 and Chapter~4. This chapter needs only the finite rules. Buffon's needle, which needs an integral, is proved in Chapter~5.

\begin{think}
Two fair coins are tossed. Someone offers the sample space ``no heads, one head, two heads'' and calls it uniform. Which event has this model assigned the wrong probability?
\end{think}

The four outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT are the right uniform space, each of mass $1/4$. ``One head'' contains two of them. The three-word list is a legal set of events. It is not a uniform set of outcomes. Volume~1 drew this picture; the habit is the one this book keeps. Name $\Omega$ before you name a fraction.

Linearity turns the same count into an inclusion--exclusion for probabilities. For three events the indicator of the union is the sum of the three indicators, minus the three pairwise products, plus the triple product. Taking expectations,
\[
 P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)+P(A\cap B\cap C).
\]
On three fair coins, let $A$, $B$, $C$ be the events that the first, second, and third coins are heads. Each has probability $1/2$, each pair has probability $1/4$, and the triple has probability $1/8$. The union has probability $3/2-3/4+1/8=7/8$. The eight outcomes list the same fact: only TTT is missing.

\section{One uniform number, and every toss}

A single finite set can carry the first $n$ tosses, for one fixed $n$. It cannot carry every prefix at once: the consistent weights on all lengths would require infinitely many coordinates. A uniform point of the unit interval does, because its binary digits are the tosses.

\begin{definition}[uniform on the unit interval]\label{def:uniform}
The sample space is $\Omega=[0,1)$. An interval event $[c,d)\subseteq[0,1)$ has probability $d-c$. A finite union of disjoint interval events has probability equal to the sum of their lengths.
\end{definition}

This assigns a probability to every set that can be written as a finite union of half-open intervals. It does not yet assign a probability to an arbitrary subset of $[0,1)$. Chapter~5 says what we do instead of pretending that every subset has a length.

Every $u\in[0,1)$ has a binary expansion $u=\sum_{k\ge1}b_k(u)2^{-k}$ with digits $b_k(u)\in\{0,1\}$. Dyadic rationals have two expansions, one ending in infinite $0$s and one ending in infinite $1$s. Choose the expansion that ends in infinite $0$s. The set of such rationals is countable. Each one will sit in the boundary of an interval, and boundaries have length $0$, so the choice does not move the probability of any interval event.

\begin{proposition}[digits are fair bits]\label{prop:bits}
For every $n\ge1$ and every string $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n\in\{0,1\}$,
\[
 P\bigl(b_1=\varepsilon_1,\ldots,b_n=\varepsilon_n\bigr)=2^{-n}.
\]
In particular the digits are mutually independent and each is fair: $P(b_k=1)=1/2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The set of $u$ whose first $n$ chosen digits are $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$ is the half-open interval $[m/2^n,(m+1)/2^n)$, where $m=\varepsilon_1 2^{n-1}+\cdots+\varepsilon_n$. The left endpoint has the chosen expansion, the one that ends in zeros, and the right endpoint belongs to the next interval. That interval has length $2^{-n}$.

To read one digit, take any $n\ge k$. The event $\{b_k=1\}$ is the union of the $2^{n-1}$ cylinders of length $2^{-n}$ in which the $k$th digit is $1$. The total length is $1/2$. For distinct positions $k_1,\ldots,k_r$, choose $n$ at least as large as every one of them. The intersection fixes $r$ digits and leaves the other $n-r$ free, so it is a union of $2^{n-r}$ cylinders and has probability $2^{-r}$. That is the product of the one-digit probabilities. Finite-bit events are finite unions of such cylinders.
\end{proof}

An event that depends on finitely many digits is a finite union of these cylinders. Call it a \emph{finite-bit event}. Its probability is a sum of lengths, hence a multiple of a negative power of $2$.

Some questions ask about the whole sequence: whether infinitely many digits are $1$, or whether the frequency of $1$s settles. The set of such sequences need not be a finite union of intervals. We measure it from the outside.

\begin{definition}[outer content]\label{def:outer}
For $A\subseteq[0,1)$, the \emph{outer content} $P^*(A)$ is the infimum of all sums $\sum_i(d_i-c_i)$ in which the intervals $[c_i,d_i)$ cover $A$. A property holds \emph{almost surely} when the set of outcomes where it fails has outer content $0$.
\end{definition}

If $A$ is a finite union of disjoint intervals, one cover is $A$ itself, so $P^*(A)$ is at most the probability already assigned to $A$. Every argument in this chapter uses that upper bound. A matching lower bound, that no cover is shorter, is not required here.

\begin{proposition}[countable subadditivity]\label{prop:subadd}
If $A\subseteq\bigcup_{n\ge1}A_n$, then $P^*(A)\le\sum_{n\ge1}P^*(A_n)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fix $\eta>0$. Cover $A_n$ by intervals whose lengths sum to less than $P^*(A_n)+\eta 2^{-n}$. The combined cover of $A$ has total length less than $\sum_n P^*(A_n)+\eta$. The infimum $P^*(A)$ is at most that sum. Let $\eta\downarrow0$.
\end{proof}

This is the only infinite additivity the early chapters use. If $A_n$ are finite-bit events and $\sum_n P(A_n)<\infty$, then the set of outcomes that lie in infinitely many $A_n$ has outer content $0$. Indeed, that set is contained in $\bigcup_{n\ge N}A_n$ for every $N$, so its outer content is at most $\sum_{n\ge N}P(A_n)$, which can be made small. Chapter~4 turns this remark into the first Borel--Cantelli lemma and pairs it with a converse for independent events.

\begin{example}[a run of heads, eventually]\label{ex:run}
Let $A_n$ be the event that tosses $n,n+1,n+2$ are all heads, that is $b_n=b_{n+1}=b_{n+2}=1$. Then $P(A_n)=1/8$, and $\sum_n P(A_n)=\infty$. The convergent-sum remark needs a finite sum, so it does not apply to the sequence $(A_n)$. Its proof is a union bound, and a union bound allows the events to overlap. The blocks $A_{3},A_{6},A_{9},\ldots$ use disjoint digits, so they are independent by Proposition~\ref{prop:bits}, and $\sum_m P(A_{3m})=\infty$ as well. For each $M$,
\[
 P\bigl(A_3^c\cap A_6^c\cap\cdots\cap A_{3M}^c\bigr)=\Bigl(\frac78\Bigr)^M,
\]
because the blocks use distinct digits. Volume~1, Chapter~9, gives $1-x\le e^{-x}$, so $(7/8)^M\le e^{-M/8}$. For any fixed starting index $J\ge1$, the event that none of the blocks $A_{3J},\ldots,A_{3(J+M-1)}$ occurs has probability $(7/8)^M$. Thus the set $Z_J$ of sequences having no such run at any block from $J$ onward has outer content at most $(7/8)^M$ for every $M$, so $P^*(Z_J)=0$. If a sequence has only finitely many runs among the disjoint blocks, it belongs to $Z_J$ for some $J$. By countable subadditivity, $P^*(\bigcup_{J\ge1}Z_J)=0$. Almost every sequence therefore has infinitely many disjoint runs of three heads.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[infinitely many of each digit]\label{prop:infdigits}
Almost every $u\in[0,1)$ has infinitely many digits equal to $1$ and infinitely many equal to $0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A chosen expansion with only finitely many $1$s ends in a tail of $0$s. For each $N\ge0$ and each string of length $N$, there is exactly one such expansion: those $N$ digits, then all zeros. It is one point of $[0,1)$, and it sits in an interval of length $2^{-M}$ for every $M$, so its outer content is $0$. The pairs $(N,\text{string})$ are countable. Countable subadditivity gives outer content $0$ for their union. A number whose chosen digits contain only finitely many $0$s would be eventually all $1$s. Our rule never chooses that expansion: a dyadic rational is written with a tail of $0$s. The numbers that admit an expansion with a tail of $1$s are exactly the dyadic rationals, a countable set of points, and that set has outer content $0$. Every other $u$ has infinitely many $0$s in its only expansion.
\end{proof}

The first head is a finite-bit question with an infinite answer. Let $T$ be the smallest $k\ge1$ with $b_k=1$, or $T=\infty$ if there is no such $k$. Then $\{T=k\}$ is the cylinder of $k-1$ zeros followed by a one, so $P(T=k)=2^{-k}$. The event $\{T=\infty\}$ is the single point $0$, whose outer content is $0$, so $T$ is finite almost surely. Its mean is $\sum_{k\ge1}k\,P(T=k)=\sum_{k\ge1}k\,2^{-k}=2$. In the run example, the set $Z_J$ sits inside a finite-bit event of probability $(7/8)^M$ for every $M$, so the definition of outer content gives $P^*(Z_J)=0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:subadd} is used afterwards, on the union of the sets $Z_J$.

\section{The ballot theorem}

Candidate $A$ receives $a$ votes and candidate $B$ receives $b$ votes, with $a>b\ge0$. The ballots are ordered uniformly at random. Bertrand asked for the probability that $A$ is strictly ahead after every ballot, and stated the answer $(a-b)/(a+b)$ in the \emph{Comptes Rendus} of 1887, volume~105, page~369. D\'esir\'e Andr\'e, in the same volume, pages~436--437, counted the favourable orders by a direct bijection. The proof below is the comparison of paths obtained by reflecting an initial segment. It is the form we reuse for random walks in Chapter~8.

A sequence of $a$ letters $A$ and $b$ letters $B$ is a path of $n=a+b$ steps of size $+1$ for $A$ and $-1$ for $B$. Write $S_j$ for the height after $j$ steps, so $S_0=0$ and $S_n=a-b$. Strict leadership throughout the count means $S_j\ge1$ for every $j=1,\ldots,n$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch01-ballot}
\caption{Left: a path that stays at height at least $1$. Right: the dashed path steps first to $+1$ and then meets $0$. The solid path is the reflection of that opening, so its first step is to $-1$. After the meeting the two paths share the route. Reflecting up to the first hit of $0$ trades a start at height $+1$ for a start at height $-1$.}
\label{fig:ballot}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[ballot theorem]\label{thm:ballot}
Let $a>b\ge0$. In a uniform random order of $a$ ballots for $A$ and $b$ ballots for $B$, the probability that $A$ is strictly ahead after every ballot is
\[
 \frac{a-b}{a+b}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $n=a+b$ and $k=a-b>0$. The number of paths from height $0$ to height $k$ in $n$ steps is $\binom{n}{a}$, since exactly $a$ steps are $+1$.

If $b=0$, then $n=a$ and the only path is a string of $A$s. It stays positive, and the probability is $1=a/a$. Assume $b\ge1$ for the rest of the count, so that every factorial below has a nonnegative argument.

A favourable path has first step $+1$. From time $1$ onward it is a path of length $n-1$ from height $1$ to height $k$ that stays at height at least $1$. Among all paths of length $n-1$ from $1$ to $k$, subtract those that touch $0$.

The total number of paths of length $n-1$ from $1$ to $k$ equals the number of paths of length $n-1$ with displacement $k-1$, which is $\binom{n-1}{(n+k)/2-1}$. Here $(n+k)/2=a$ is an integer, because $n$ and $k$ are both even or both odd.

Take a path of length $n-1$ from height $1$ to height $k$ that does touch $0$, and let $\tau$ be the first time it is at $0$. Reflect the graph over the horizontal axis up to time $\tau$, and leave the rest unchanged. The reflected path runs from height $-1$ to height $k$. Every path from $-1$ to $k$ must hit $0$, because it starts negative and ends positive and the steps have size $1$; reflecting its own initial segment up to the first hit of $0$ returns a path from $+1$ to $k$ that touches $0$. The two maps are inverses. The number of paths of length $n-1$ from $-1$ to $k$ is $\binom{n-1}{(n+k)/2}$.

The number of favourable paths is therefore
\[
 \binom{n-1}{a-1}-\binom{n-1}{a}.
\]
The binomial recurrence $\binom{n}{a}=\binom{n-1}{a}+\binom{n-1}{a-1}$ is not yet the form we want. Compute the difference directly:
\begin{align*}
\binom{n-1}{a-1}-\binom{n-1}{a}
&=\frac{(n-1)!}{(a-1)!(n-a)!}-\frac{(n-1)!}{a!(n-a-1)!}\\
&=\frac{(n-1)!}{a!(n-a)!}\bigl(a-(n-a)\bigr)
=\frac{(n-1)!}{a!(n-a)!}\,k
=\frac{k}{n}\binom{n}{a}.
\end{align*}
Dividing by the total number of paths, $\binom{n}{a}$, leaves $k/n=(a-b)/(a+b)$.
\end{proof}

Check the smallest unequal election, $a=2$ and $b=1$. The three orders are $AAB$, $ABA$, $BAA$. Only $AAB$ stays strictly ahead: the heights are $1,2,1$. The formula gives $(2-1)/(2+1)=1/3$.

If $a=b$, the final height is $0$, so strict leadership through the last ballot is impossible. The probability is $0$, and the hypothesis $a>b$ is not decorative.

The digits constructed above are functions on $[0,1)$. A function on a sample space is a random variable, and an average of one is an expectation. Chapter~2 takes up those words for countable values, including the Poisson limit that Volume~1 stated as a family of masses and did not yet obtain from the binomial law.

\section{The condition changes the space}

The formula $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$ is a new experiment: the outcomes are the ones inside $B$, and the masses are the old masses divided by $P(B)$. The set $B$ has to be named before the division. An informer who chooses which fact to mention is a different experiment, and it can give a different number.

Two children are ordered, older then younger. Each is a boy or a girl with probability $1/2$, independently. The information in the first question is exactly the event ``not GG'', and no other selection is made. The four outcomes BB, BG, GB, GG each have mass $1/4$. Removing GG leaves three outcomes, still equally likely. Exactly one of them is BB, so the conditional probability of two boys is $1/3$. The same fraction appears if a parent who has at least one boy points to a boy chosen uniformly among the boys and says ``this one is a boy''. The procedure occurs exactly on the event ``not GG''. A mixed family has one boy, so the point is forced and the family keeps its mass $1/4$. The two-boy family splits its mass in half, so each of its two pointings has mass $1/8$. The total mass is still $3/4$, and BB still carries mass $1/4$, hence $(1/4)/(3/4)=1/3$. Conditioning on the older child being the one indicated gives the same value: that indication has mass $1/8$ from BB and mass $1/4$ from GB, and $(1/8)/(3/8)=1/3$. Treating the four pointings as equally likely is a different experiment. That count gives $1/2$.

Each child is also born on a day of the week, chosen independently and uniformly. The sample space is the $14^2=196$ ordered pairs of types, each of mass $1/196$. The information is the event that at least one child is a boy born on Tuesday. Call that type BT. It is not the announcement of a parent who first picks a child and then reports that child's day. Families with no BT child number $13^2=169$, so $196-169=27$ families lie in the event. Among those $27$, the two-boy families are: first child BT and second any of the $7$ boy-days, or second child BT and first a boy who is not BT, which is $6$ further families. That is $13$ families. The conditional probability of two boys is $13/27$. A pointing rule would weight a two-Tuesday-boy family twice and a one-Tuesday-boy family once, and $13/27$ would no longer be the answer.

The host is a function of the car and of a fresh uniform choice, not a person who sometimes opens the car. The rules are these.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The car is equally likely behind any of $n\ge3$ doors. You always begin at door $1$.
\item The host never opens door $1$ and never opens the car. He opens exactly $n-2$ other doors, all goats, and leaves one other door closed. Call that door $H$.
\item If several legal sets of doors can be opened, he chooses the set uniformly. When the car is behind door $1$, every other door is a goat, and each choice of the leftover door $H=j$ corresponds to exactly one set, namely the other $n-2$ doors. There are $n-1$ possible leftovers, so $P(H=j\mid C_1)=1/(n-1)$.
\item If the car is behind door $j\neq1$, the only legal leftover is $j$ itself, so $P(H=j\mid C_j)=1$. If the car is behind a third door, he cannot leave $j$ closed.
\end{enumerate}
Therefore, for each fixed $j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}$,
\[
 P(H=j)=\frac1n\cdot\frac1{n-1}+\frac1n\cdot1=\frac1{n-1}.
\]
Bayes' formula returns your door to probability $1/n$ and gives the remaining closed door probability $(n-1)/n$. For $n=3$ the single legal leftover, when the car is behind door $1$, is equally likely to be door $2$ or door $3$. The host's reveal does not move the $1/3$ on your door. It piles the other $2/3$ onto the door he left shut.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:mybirthday}
You and $22$ other people have birthdays independent and uniform among $365$ days. Show that the probability that at least one of the others shares your birthday is at most $22/365<0.0603$. Volume~1, Chapter~10, shows that the probability of some shared birthday among $23$ people already exceeds $1/2$. Why do the two numbers differ?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:twochildren}
Each child is a boy or a girl with equal probability $1/2$, independently, and, in part~(b), is born on a day of the week chosen independently and uniformly.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item You are told that a family of two children is not two girls, and that this is all the information. What is the conditional probability of two boys?
\item You are told that the family has at least one boy born on Tuesday. What is the conditional probability of two boys?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:penney}
A fair coin is tossed until the pattern HHH or the pattern THH appears. Show that HHH appears first with probability $1/8$, and explain the fraction by a property of any sequence that contains a tail before HHH has already occurred.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:montyn}
There are $n\ge3$ doors, one car, and $n-1$ goats. You pick door~$1$. The host, who knows where the car is, opens $n-2$ other doors to reveal goats, choosing uniformly at random if he has a choice, and offers you the remaining closed door. Show that the probability the car is behind your door is still $1/n$, and that the probability it is behind the other closed door is $(n-1)/n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:pairwise}
Two fair dice are rolled, independently. Let $A$ be ``the first die is odd'', $B$ ``the second die is odd'', and $C$ ``the sum is odd''. Show that the three events are pairwise independent and not mutually independent. Give the eight probabilities that make the distinction.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:aces}
A deck has $52$ cards, of which $4$ are aces. Two cards are drawn.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If the draw is without replacement, ordered, what is the probability that both are aces? What is the conditional probability that the second is an ace, given that the first is?
\item Answer the same two questions if each card is drawn independently from a full deck, so replacement is allowed.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:catalan}
A \emph{Dyck path} of semilength $n\ge1$ is a sequence of $n$ letters $X$ and $n$ letters $Y$ in which every prefix has at least as many $X$s as $Y$s. Using Theorem~\ref{thm:ballot}, show that the number of Dyck paths is the Catalan number
\[
 C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:smallballot}
Candidate $A$ has $3$ votes and candidate $B$ has $1$. List the orders and mark those in which $A$ is strictly ahead throughout. Check the count against Theorem~\ref{thm:ballot}.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:mybirthday}
\idea The event depends on $22$ independent comparisons with one fixed day, not on the $\binom{23}2$ pairs among the whole group.
\writeup Let $q=(364/365)^{22}$ be the probability that all $22$ other birthdays miss yours. The inequality $(1-x)^n\ge1-nx$ for $x=1/365$ and $n=22$ follows by induction, or from the tangent line to $(1-x)^n$ at $0$ and the fact that $t\mapsto(1-t)^n$ is convex on $[0,1]$ for $n\ge1$: its second derivative $n(n-1)(1-t)^{n-2}$ is nonnegative. Thus $q\ge1-22/365$, and
\[
 1-q\le\frac{22}{365}.
\]
Since $22/365=0.06027\ldots<0.0603$, the probability is less than $0.0603$. The shared-birthday event of Volume~1 is different. There $P(\text{all }23\text{ birthdays distinct})=\prod_{k=1}^{22}(1-k/365)\le\exp\bigl(-\sum_{k=1}^{22}k/365\bigr)=\exp(-253/365)$. The sum $253/365$ is larger than $\ln2$, because $365\ln2=252.9987\ldots$, so that product is strictly less than $1/2$. The event in this exercise uses only the $22$ comparisons with your own day.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twochildren}
\idea Conditioning changes the sample space. A rarer description of ``at least one boy'' removes fewer of the two-boy outcomes, in proportion, than it removes of the mixed outcomes.
\writeup (a) The four outcomes BB, BG, GB, GG are equally likely. Excluding GG leaves three outcomes, one of which is BB. The conditional probability is $1/3$.

(b) Each child has $7$ boy-day types and $7$ girl-day types, so $14$ types, and the two children give $14^2=196$ equally likely outcomes. A boy born on Tuesday is one type; call it BT. The outcomes with no BT child number $13^2=169$, so $196-169=27$ outcomes have at least one BT child. Among them, the two-boy outcomes are: the first child is BT and the second is any boy ($7$ outcomes), or the second is BT and the first is a boy who is not BT ($6$ outcomes). That is $13$ outcomes. The conditional probability is $13/27$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:penney}
\idea Once a tail has appeared, the pattern THH completes at the first later occurrence of HH, and HHH cannot occur without a prior HH.
\writeup Let a state be the longest current suffix that is a prefix of HHH or of THH. The transient states are $\varnothing$, H, HH, T, and TH. From HH, heads ends the game in favour of HHH, and tails moves to T. From TH, heads ends the game in favour of THH, and tails moves to T. From T, heads moves to TH and tails stays at T. Thus every state that has seen a tail is absorbed in THH, never in HHH.

Let $p_s$ be the probability that HHH wins from state $s$. Then $p_{\mathrm{THH}}=0$, $p_{\mathrm{HHH}}=1$, and the first-step relations are
\begin{align*}
p_\varnothing&=\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm H}+\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm T},&
p_{\mathrm H}&=\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm{HH}}+\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm T},&
p_{\mathrm{HH}}&=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm T},\\
p_{\mathrm T}&=\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm{TH}}+\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm T},&
p_{\mathrm{TH}}&=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12 p_{\mathrm T}.
\end{align*}
The last two equations force $p_{\mathrm T}=p_{\mathrm{TH}}=0$. Then $p_{\mathrm{HH}}=1/2$, $p_{\mathrm H}=1/4$, and $p_\varnothing=1/8$.

The same value $1/8$ is the probability of the single word HHH. Any other sequence has a first tail, and until that tail the tosses are a block of heads. The pattern HHH has already occurred in that block only if the block has length at least $3$, which is the word HHH itself. After the first tail the state is T, and $p_{\mathrm T}=0$, so HHH does not win from there.

From T the game still ends. Each visit to TH is followed by heads, and a win for THH, with probability $1/2$, independently of the past. The probability of $k$ failed visits is $2^{-k}$, which tends to $0$. So THH wins with probability $7/8$. Walter Penney posed this waiting game in the \emph{Journal of Recreational Mathematics} in 1974. The odds $7$ to $1$ against HHH are this computation, not a comparison of the two words as unordered sets of letters.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:montyn}
\idea The host's forced reveals do not move the probability of your original door. They pile the remaining probability onto the one door he left shut.
\writeup Let $C_i$ be the event that the car is behind door $i$, each of probability $1/n$. Let $H$ be the door the host leaves closed, other than door~$1$. We want $P(C_1\mid H=j)$ for a fixed $j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}$, and also the probability for the offered door.

If the car is behind door~$1$, the host chooses the door to leave closed uniformly among the other $n-1$ doors, so $P(H=j\mid C_1)=1/(n-1)$. If the car is behind door~$j$, he must leave $j$ closed and open every other goat door, so $P(H=j\mid C_j)=1$. If the car is behind some other door $i\notin\{1,j\}$, he must leave $i$ closed, so $P(H=j\mid C_i)=0$. Total probability gives
\[
 P(H=j)=\frac1n\cdot\frac1{n-1}+\frac1n\cdot1=\frac1n\Bigl(\frac1{n-1}+1\Bigr)=\frac1{n-1}.
\]
Bayes' formula then gives
\[
 P(C_1\mid H=j)=\frac{(1/(n-1))\cdot(1/n)}{1/(n-1)}=\frac1n,
\]
and
\[
 P(C_j\mid H=j)=\frac{1\cdot(1/n)}{1/(n-1)}=\frac{n-1}n.
\]
Switching to the remaining closed door wins with probability $(n-1)/n$. For $n=3$ this is the $2/3$ of Volume~1, Chapter~10.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:pairwise}
\idea Opposite parity is a condition on the pair. It factors across one die at a time, and it cannot hold together with both dice odd.
\writeup There are $36$ ordered outcomes, each of mass $1/36$. Each die is odd in $18$ outcomes, so $P(A)=P(B)=1/2$. The sum is odd in the $18$ outcomes where the dice have opposite parity, so $P(C)=1/2$.

The intersection $A\cap B$ is both dice odd: $3\times3=9$ outcomes, probability $1/4=P(A)P(B)$. The intersection $A\cap C$ is first die odd and second die even: again $9$ outcomes, probability $1/4$. Likewise $P(B\cap C)=1/4$. So every pair multiplies.

The triple intersection requires both dice odd and the sum odd, which is impossible. So $P(A\cap B\cap C)=0$, while $P(A)P(B)P(C)=1/8$. The family is not mutually independent.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:aces}
\idea Without replacement the second draw is uniform on what remains. With independent draws the deck is restored, so the information about the first card does not change the second.
\writeup (a) The first card is an ace with probability $4/52$. Given that, three aces remain among $51$ cards, so the conditional probability is $3/51=1/17$. The probability both are aces is $(4/52)(3/51)=12/(52\cdot51)=1/221$.

(b) Each draw is an ace with probability $4/52=1/13$, independently, so both are aces with probability $1/169$. The conditional probability that the second is an ace, given that the first is, is still $1/13$, because the second draw does not use the first card.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:catalan}
\idea Put one extra $X$ in front of a Dyck path. The new sequence is always strictly ahead, and every strictly-ahead sequence with one extra $X$ arises this way.
\writeup Let $\mathcal D_n$ be the set of Dyck paths of semilength $n$, and let $\mathcal B_n$ be the set of sequences of $n+1$ letters $X$ and $n$ letters $Y$ in which every prefix has more $X$s than $Y$s. Theorem~\ref{thm:ballot}, with $a=n+1$ and $b=n$, says
\[
 |\mathcal B_n|=\frac{1}{2n+1}\binom{2n+1}{n+1}.
\]
The symmetry $\binom{2n+1}{n+1}=\binom{2n+1}{n}$ rewrites the count as $\frac1{2n+1}\binom{2n+1}{n}$. Then
\[
 \frac1{2n+1}\binom{2n+1}{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{n!\,(n+1)!}=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}.
\]

The map $\mathcal D_n\to\mathcal B_n$ that puts an $X$ in front is a bijection. In the image, the first letter is $X$, so the first prefix is strict, and any later prefix has one more $X$ than the corresponding prefix of the Dyck path, which already had at least as many $X$s as $Y$s. Conversely, a sequence in $\mathcal B_n$ must start with $X$, because a leading $Y$ fails at the first place. Delete that $X$. Every prefix of what remains has at least as many $X$s as $Y$s, since one extra $X$ was enough to make the longer prefix strict, and the totals are $n$ and $n$. So the remainder is a Dyck path.

Therefore $|\mathcal D_n|=C_n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:smallballot}
\idea Four sequences, and the theorem predicts half of them.
\writeup The orders are AAAB, AABA, ABAA, BAAA. Their height sequences are
\[
 (1,2,3,2),\qquad (1,2,1,2),\qquad (1,0,1,2),\qquad (-1,0,1,2).
\]
The first two stay at least $1$. The third hits $0$. The fourth starts negative. Theorem~\ref{thm:ballot} gives $(3-1)/(3+1)=1/2$, and $2/4=1/2$.
\end{solution}
