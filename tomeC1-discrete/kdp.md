# Description

A computer is a machine for discrete mathematics. Every program is a formula of logic, every data structure a graph, every search a walk through an enormous finite space. This book is a journey through the mathematics behind them, told through the problems and the people who solved them, from George Boole's algebra of thought to a satisfiability solver that settled a question about Pythagorean triples with a proof two hundred terabytes long.

Twelve chapters lead from logic to geometry. Logic and proof: truth tables, resolution, compactness, Horn clauses and 2-SAT. Orders: lattices and fixed points, termination, the theorems of Dilworth and Sperner, and why every long enough sequence has a long monotone run. Counting: double counting, the Möbius function, trees as words, and counting up to symmetry with Burnside and Pólya, which tells how many molecules of a given formula can exist. Generating functions and the symbolic method, with Lagrange inversion. Graphs: Euler tours, de Bruijn sequences, tournaments and Menger's theorem. Matchings and flows: Hall, König, max-flow min-cut, Birkhoff, Tutte, and stable marriages. Planarity and colouring: Euler's formula, Kuratowski, the five colour theorem and the chromatic polynomial. Extremal problems and Ramsey theory: why total disorder is impossible. Spectral graph theory: counting spanning trees, Cheeger's inequality and expanders. Matroids: the exact reason the greedy algorithm works. The linear algebra method: Oddtown, the combinatorial Nullstellensatz, and the cap set problem, whose answer about the card game SET was found in 2016 with a proof a few pages long. Designs and finite geometries: Kirkman's schoolgirls, projective planes, Euler's thirty-six officers, and the covering arrays used to test software.

Every theorem is proved in full. Every chapter opens with a story and a question to think about, and has exercises graded by difficulty, each with a hint and a solution that first explains how to find the idea and then writes it up. Figures show the constructions, and every chapter ends with its sources and suggestions for further reading.

Each chapter comes with a substantial Python notebook that turns the theorems into working programs: a SAT solver, counting by computer, flow and matching algorithms, planarity and colouring, spectra of graphs, the greedy algorithm on matroids, and searches for designs and caps.

The book is written for the pleasure of seeing why things are true. It asks for no more than a first course in mathematics, some familiarity with proofs, and curiosity. It is the first volume of the computing track of a series that goes from the foundations of number theory, algebra and probability to the edge of research.
