\chapter*{Preface}

Five and seven are both prime. Yet $5=1^2+2^2$, while $7$ cannot be written as a sum of two integer squares.

The impossibility is surprisingly easy to prove. Divide any integer square by $4$: the remainder is always $0$ or $1$. A sum of two squares can therefore leave remainder $0$, $1$ or $2$, but never $3$. Since $7$ leaves remainder $3$, no search for two squares is needed. The question is settled for every possible pair of integers at once.

Now turn the question around. The primes $5$, $13$ and $17$ all leave remainder $1$ when divided by $4$, and each is a sum of two squares. Is that always true? And if so, how can a condition about remainders guarantee the existence of two integers whose squares add up to a given prime?

The examples suggest a theorem. They do not prove it. The remainder argument establishes an obstruction, but removing an obstruction is not the same as constructing a solution.

This distinction lies at the heart of number theory. A small calculation may reveal a pattern; a carefully chosen argument can establish that the pattern must persist, or explain precisely where it fails.

\section*{From proof to arithmetic}

Volume~1, \emph{First Principles}, introduced the central habit of mathematics: distinguish what the evidence suggests from what the argument proves. We learned to work with definitions, follow the logic of a statement, and find the right method rather than merely the right answer.

In this volume, those habits meet one of mathematics' oldest subjects: the integers.

The objects are familiar. We can add, multiply and divide integers, identify primes and calculate remainders. But familiarity is deceptive. Simple questions about these operations lead to remarkably deep structures.

Why does Euclid's algorithm always find the greatest common divisor? Why can every positive integer be factored uniquely into primes? Why do some equations have integer solutions while others have none? And why can changing the number system make a difficult factorisation suddenly possible?

The answers require different ways of looking at the same numbers.

Sometimes the decisive tool is divisibility. Sometimes it is a remainder, a symmetry or an approximation. Sometimes we must enlarge the integers themselves and discover which of their properties survive.

The aim is not to collect tricks. It is to understand why a method works, what it proves and when a different method is needed.

\section*{The journey through the integers}

The book follows four connected parts.

\emph{The Arithmetic of Integers} begins with divisibility and Euclid's algorithm, then develops prime factorisation, congruences and arithmetic functions. These foundations let us replace large calculations with structural arguments.

\emph{Patterns in Remainders} studies the multiplicative order of numbers, primitive roots and quadratic reciprocity. The apparently simple question of whether a number is a square modulo a prime leads to one of the most remarkable laws of elementary number theory.

\emph{Equations in Integers} moves from deciding what is possible to finding and classifying solutions. We study sums of squares, Diophantine equations, infinite descent and continued fractions. Pell's equation reveals how an approximation to an irrational number can produce an exact integer solution. Gaussian and Eisenstein integers then show how a new setting for multiplication can reveal factorisations hidden from ordinary arithmetic.

\emph{The Hidden Structure} brings several threads together. Dirichlet characters turn arithmetic conditions into functions that respect multiplication. Euler products express a relationship between primes and infinite series, while sieves count integers by excluding divisibility conditions. These tools reveal new connections, but they also mark the limits of what we have proved: deeper results about the distribution of primes require further ideas.

Each part grows out of questions raised by the preceding one. The progression matters more than the number of results collected along the way.

\section*{How to read this book}

This volume builds on \emph{First Principles}. Its main reading route assumes the proof habits and elementary tools developed there, but no prior course in abstract algebra or number theory. New arithmetic concepts are defined when they become useful.

Read with a pencil. Before a theorem, examine the examples. Before a proof, ask what would count as a complete argument. When a calculation works, look for the reason it works; when it fails, ask whether the failure reveals a general obstruction.

For a first reading, follow the four parts in order, work through the examples and attempt the one-star exercises. The main route is designed to stand without the optional advanced investigations. Later, return to the more demanding problems, which invite you to combine methods developed in this volume and explore their consequences further.

All 144 graded exercises have hints and complete solutions. Use the hints when you need a direction, but give yourself time to discover one. A finished proof often looks inevitable; the work of finding it rarely does.

The \emph{Arithmetic Field Guide} at the end gathers definitions, useful tests and problem-solving strategies for situations in which no chapter heading tells you which method to choose.

\section*{Beyond the integers}

The integers offer more than a collection of theorems. They provide a setting in which larger mathematical ideas first become visible.

The invertible remainders modulo a number form a group. Gaussian integers form a ring. Arithmetic characters preserve multiplication. Each of these structures appears here because it answers a concrete question.

Volume~3, \emph{Symmetry}, will study the principles behind them systematically, developing groups, rings and fields as mathematical objects in their own right. Later, Volume~6 returns to the distribution of primes with analytical tools, and Volume~13 develops general algebraic integers, ideals and $p$-adic number systems.

For now, the integers are enough.

They remind us that a simple question need not have a simple answer, that a proof can reveal more than a calculation, and that the deepest connections often begin with the most familiar objects.

The task is to find those connections---and understand why they hold.
