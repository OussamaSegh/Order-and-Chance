\chapter*{Preface}
The integers are the oldest objects of mathematics and still among the most mysterious. A child can ask their
open questions; the answers call on the whole of mathematics. This volume of \emph{Order and Chance}
studies them with elementary tools, and discovers at every turn that elementary does not mean easy.

Two gear wheels, a repeating decimal, a triangle with integer sides: these are invitations to the same adventure.
One starts by calculating, notices a pattern, makes a conjecture, breaks it with a counterexample, and eventually
finds a proof. A proof is the moment when the pattern stops being a coincidence. The jokes in the margins of
this journey are optional; the hypotheses of a theorem are not.

The book starts with divisibility and Euclid's algorithm, the primes and their infinitude, and congruences, the
arithmetic of remainders. Orders turn non-zero remainders modulo a prime into
a cyclic multiplicative group. Their squares obey the law that Gauss called
the \emph{theorema aureum}: quadratic reciprocity, the centrepiece of the
elementary progression. Its two proofs expose different structures.
Arithmetic functions and Dirichlet convolution then offer a first glimpse
of the analytic and probabilistic number theory of Volume~5. The last chapter
makes that glimpse precise: Dirichlet characters, Euler products and Legendre's
sieve are the language Volumes~5, 12 and~13 take as known.

From there we follow questions that look innocent: which numbers are sums of two or four squares, how
continued fractions find good rational approximations and solve Pell's equation, and how to prove that an
integer equation has no solution. The questions force us to enlarge the integers, first to the Gaussian and
Eisenstein integers. In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ the equality $6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ shows that unique factorisation can fail; Volume~13 restores it with ideals, and builds the $p$-adic numbers in which divisibility by $p$ becomes nearness. The integers have acquired
geometry and algebra without ceasing to be the integers.

\section*{What to bring}
Bring the mathematical preparation of Volume~0: proof language and induction,
finite counting, polynomial algebra, complex numbers, inequalities,
sequences and limits, elementary calculus and finite-dimensional linear algebra.
Basic finite probability is used in selected bridge exercises.
No previous abstract-algebra course is assumed: groups, rings, ideals,
quotients and the fraction field are explained where needed.

The prerequisites enter at different stages. Euclidean division and basic
congruences need integer arithmetic, induction and finite sets.
Quantitative prime estimates use logarithms, integral comparison and
derivative monotonicity. Arithmetic averages use the harmonic-number estimate,
absolute convergence and the Basel sum from Volume~0.
The second reciprocity proof uses roots of unity and prime cyclotomic
irreducibility; continued fractions use real completeness.
Norm matrices use bases, trace and determinant.
These are references to mathematical tools, independent of printed numbering.

We use the standard integer, real and complex systems and the Volume~0
results just identified. New algebraic and $p$-adic constructions are
justified here. The converse three-square theorem is expressly admitted
only in its triangular-number exercise; advanced destinations remain
outlooks with sources, rather than hidden ingredients in proofs.

Every numbered theorem, proposition, lemma and corollary has a proof, with earlier dependencies identified.
Deep results mentioned as destinations, such as the prime number theorem, the three-square theorem and the
Hasse--Minkowski theorem, are explicitly distinguished from results proved here. The point is to know exactly
what has been established, what is being used, and what remains beyond the horizon.

\section*{How to travel}
The eleven chapters follow a continuous route. Chapters~\ref{ch:divisibility}
and~\ref{ch:primes} build arithmetic; Chapters~\ref{ch:congruences}--\ref{ch:reciprocity}
develop modular structure and reciprocity, including Hensel's lemma for a simple root of a congruence. Chapter~\ref{ch:functions} collects
divisor information into arithmetic functions.
Chapters~\ref{ch:squares}--\ref{ch:diophantine} investigate representations,
approximation and equations.
Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian} establishes arithmetic in the Gaussian and Eisenstein integers, and records the failure $6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$.
Chapter~\ref{ch:characters} then introduces Dirichlet characters, Euler products
and Legendre's sieve.

For a shorter elementary route, read through Chapter~\ref{ch:diophantine}.
For algorithms, start with Chapters~\ref{ch:divisibility},
\ref{ch:congruences} and~\ref{ch:orders}, then continued fractions and
Euclidean ring arithmetic.
Chapter~\ref{ch:characters} can be read as soon as Chapters~\ref{ch:orders},
\ref{ch:reciprocity} and~\ref{ch:functions} are known.

Volume~3 develops groups, rings and fields systematically. Volume~13 takes the failure of unique factorisation, the quadratic integers and the $p$-adic numbers into the arithmetic of number fields.
This book proves Pell's equation, the Gaussian and Eisenstein integers, and reciprocity, and leaves those hidden worlds to their proper destination. The final \emph{Arithmetic Field Guide} collects
notation and proof strategies.

\section*{Eight bridge problems}
These exercises deliberately use more than one mathematical language:
binomial carries (Exercise~\ref{ex:t1-binomial-carry}), Lucas' theorem
(\ref{ex:lucas}), random residue signs (\ref{ex:t1-residue-chance}),
coprime-pair density (\ref{ex:coprimeprob}), continued fractions and
B\'ezout (\ref{ex:cf415}), and representations and factorization
(\ref{ex:euler1000009}). The norm-matrix and random-valuation questions are taken up in Volume~13, with the quadratic integers and the $p$-adic valuation.
Each has both a discovery strategy and a complete write-up.
Several were already among the book's strongest problems;
a connection need not be a newly invented detour.

At each worked example, cover the next line and predict it. At a ``Pause and think'' box, make a guess before
reading on. Try a small case and an awkward case: $0$, $1$, the prime $2$, a repeated prime factor. Many false
proofs have excellent manners until they meet one of these guests. Diagrams help us conjecture, but their
captions say what they establish; a handsome picture is not an induction argument.

Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a longer investigation and
\textbf{$\star\star\star$} for a substantial challenge. Each has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its
chapter. A solution begins with \emph{How to find it}, explaining what to notice, before the \emph{Write-up}.
Try the hint before the solution, and after reading a solution close the book and reconstruct the key step.
That last small act turns recognition into understanding. Experiments test claims on finite data;
they do not replace proofs about all integers. Cryptographic examples are deliberately small demonstrations,
not systems suitable for protecting information. Historical portraits and photographs are credited where they
appear and in the illustration credits. The new mathematical diagrams are drawn for this volume and remain
readable in black and white.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random yet obey laws. This floor is the masonry: Euclid, reciprocity, and the one field where unique factorisation fails and ideals repair it. The next floor takes up what survives a transformation. The characters proved here are the detectors a later floor will aim at residue classes.
