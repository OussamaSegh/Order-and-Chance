\chapter{The Lebesgue Integral}\label{ch:integral}

\begin{center}\itshape
Coins on a table can be added in the order they happen to lie, or sorted into heaps of equal value.\\
Riemann cuts the axis of \(x\). Lebesgue cuts the axis of the values.\\
Why does the second cut let a limit pass inside the sum?
\end{center}

In 1926 Henri Lebesgue lectured in Copenhagen on the way the integral had grown. He asked the audience to picture two merchants counting the day's takings. The first merchant has no system. He adds each coin and each note as it comes to hand. A Riemann sum does the same job on a graph: cut the \(x\)-axis into short pieces and add \(f(x_i)\) times the length. The second merchant sorts the money into heaps of equal value, counts a heap, and multiplies. A Lebesgue sum does that on the graph. The set where \(y_j\le f<y_{j+1}\) may be scattered, and you add \(y_j\) times its measure (Figure~\ref{fig:sums}). Finitely many coins give both merchants the same total. For a function, the sorted count is the one that survives a limit. The wording here is a paraphrase; the lecture is listed in the sources.

Riemann's habilitation of 1854 cut the axis of the variable into small intervals and added \(f(x_i)\,\Delta x_i\). Lebesgue cut the axis of the values. A level set may be any measurable set. Two gains follow. The same sentence defines the integral for a length, an area, a probability, or counting measure. And a limit may pass under the integral sign once a mild hypothesis is checked. The indicator of the rationals in \([0,1]\) has upper Darboux sum \(1\) and lower Darboux sum \(0\) for every subdivision, so Riemann's integral refuses it. The Lebesgue integral of that indicator is \(0\).

The three theorems that make the integral usable were found soon after. Beppo Levi proved monotone convergence in 1906. For a rising sequence of nonnegative measurable functions, the integral of the limit equals the limit of the integrals, finite or not.

\portrait{levi}{Beppo Levi (1875--1961). Photograph, early twentieth century, author unknown. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

Pierre Fatou, also in 1906, proved the inequality for \(\liminf\) that now carries his name. The paper is about trigonometric series and Taylor series.

\portrait{fatou}{Pierre Fatou (1878--1929). Photograph, author unknown. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

Dominated convergence, the theorem for a signed sequence, was published by Lebesgue in 1908, in a note on Fredholm's equation. He cuts off a set on which the integral of the dominant is smaller than a chosen \(\varepsilon\), and uses bounded convergence on what remains. The proof below goes through Fatou's lemma, and the underlying space is allowed to have infinite measure. These three statements are the ones you will use.

Three debts from earlier volumes are paid here. Volume~1 used the integral of a continuous function on a segment, together with four working rules, and said that Volume~7 would construct it. Theorem~\ref{thm:riemanncont} is that construction. Lebesgue's criterion then names the bounded functions that possess a Riemann integral. Volume~4 named uniform integrability and did not call on it. Vitali's theorem is the exact condition for a limit in \(L^1\). Volume~5 differentiated under the integral sign for continuous integrands with a continuous dominant, the move sometimes called Feynman's trick. Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter} is the same conclusion for measurable functions and an arbitrary measure.

\section{Measurable functions}

The integral needs one regularity condition, and it is about level sets. Fix a measure space \((E,\mathcal A,\mu)\). A function may take the values \(\pm\infty\). The set \([0,+\infty]\) carries the order and the topology you expect: neighbourhoods of \(+\infty\) are the sets \((a,+\infty]\).

\begin{definition}
A function \(f:E\to[-\infty,+\infty]\) is \emph{measurable} when \(\{f\le t\}\in\mathcal A\) for every real \(t\). A \emph{simple} function is a finite sum \(s=\sum_ia_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\) with each \(A_i\in\mathcal A\).
\end{definition}

Chapter~\ref{ch:measures} already records the consequence for Borel sets. The family of Borel sets \(B\) such that \(\{f\in B\}\in\mathcal A\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, and it contains every half-line \((-\infty,t]\), so it contains every Borel set. Thus \(\{f\in B\}\in\mathcal A\) whenever \(B\) is Borel. Continuous functions preserve this. If \(g:\R^n\to\R\) is continuous and \(f_1,\dots,f_n\) are measurable and real-valued, then \(\{g(f_1,\dots,f_n)<t\}\) is the preimage of an open set. An open set in \(\R^n\) is a countable union of boxes, and the preimage of a box \(\prod_i\,]a_i,b_i]\) is a finite intersection of sets \(\{a_i<f_i\le b_i\}\). Each of those is a difference of sets \(\{f_i\le b_i\}\) and \(\{f_i\le a_i\}\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:measurablefns}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Sums, products, maxima and minima of finitely many real measurable functions are measurable, and so are \(f^+=\max(f,0)\), \(f^-=\max(-f,0)\) and \(|f|\).
\item If \(f_n\) are measurable, so are \(\sup_nf_n\), \(\inf_nf_n\), \(\limsup_nf_n\), \(\liminf_nf_n\), and \(\lim_nf_n\) where it exists.
\item If \(f\ge0\) is measurable, the simple functions \(s_n=\min\bigl(n,2^{-n}\lfloor2^nf\rfloor\bigr)\) are measurable, satisfy \(0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots\), and \(s_n\to f\) pointwise; uniformly if \(f\)
is bounded.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Sum, product, max and min are continuous functions of the tuple, and so are \(t\mapsto\max(t,0)\), \(t\mapsto\max(-t,0)\) and \(t\mapsto|t|\). The paragraph above applies.

(b) The inequality \(\sup_nf_n\le t\) holds exactly on \(\bigcap_n\{f_n\le t\}\). Also \(\inf_nf_n=-\sup_n(-f_n)\), and \(\limsup_nf_n=\inf_m\sup_{n\ge m}f_n\). The limit exists precisely on the measurable set \(\{\limsup_nf_n=\liminf_nf_n\}\).

(c) The value \(s_n(x)\) equals \(k2^{-n}\) for some integer \(k\) with \(0\le k\le n2^n\), on the measurable set \(\{k2^{-n}\le f<(k+1)2^{-n}\}\), and equals \(n\) on \(\{f\ge n\}\). To see the sequence rise, write \(m=\lfloor2^nf(x)\rfloor\), so \(2m\le2^{n+1}f(x)<2m+2\). Then \(\lfloor2^{n+1}f(x)\rfloor\ge2m\), and the value at stage \(n+1\) before the cap is at least the value at stage \(n\) before the cap. The cap itself rises from \(n\) to \(n+1\). If stage \(n\) is already capped, then \(f(x)\ge n\), so the stage \(n+1\) value is at least \(n\). Thus \(s_n\le s_{n+1}\). Where \(f(x)\) is finite and \(f(x)<n\), one has \(0\le f(x)-s_n(x)<2^{-n}\). Where \(f(x)=+\infty\), one has \(s_n(x)=n\). If \(0\le f\le M\), then for every \(n>M\) the error is less than \(2^{-n}\) at every \(x\), so the convergence is uniform.
\end{proof}

\section{The integral, and passing to the limit}

A simple function is a finite list of heights and measurable heaps. Add height times measure. For a general nonnegative measurable function, take every simple function underneath it and keep the least upper bound of those sums.

\begin{definition}
For a simple \(s=\sum_ia_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\ge0\) with the sets \(A_i\) disjoint, set \(\int s\,d\mu=\sum_ia_i\mu(A_i)\), using the convention \(0\cdot\infty=0\). For a measurable \(f\ge0\), set
\[\int f\,d\mu=\sup\Bigl\{\int s\,d\mu:\ s\text{ simple},\ 0\le s\le f\Bigr\}\in[0,+\infty].\]
For \(A\in\mathcal A\), write \(\int_Af\,d\mu=\int f\mathbf 1_A\,d\mu\).
\end{definition}

The product \(0\cdot\infty\) is \(0\) because a zero height contributes nothing, however large the set. The number \(\int s\,d\mu\) does not depend on the way \(s\) is written. If \(s=\sum_ia_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}=\sum_jb_j\mathbf 1_{B_j}\) with both families disjoint, the intersections \(A_i\cap B_j\) refine both partitions. On a nonempty intersection the two coefficients agree, and both sums become \(\sum_{i,j}a_i\mu(A_i\cap B_j)\). On a common refinement, the integral of a sum of two nonnegative simple functions is the sum of the integrals, and \(0\le s\le t\) implies \(\int s\le\int t\). So the supremum definition agrees with the finite sum when \(f\) itself is simple and nonnegative. The same comparison gives \(f\le g\Rightarrow\int f\le\int g\).

Figure~\ref{fig:v7c08slices} is one such pair. The coral graph is \(f\). The charcoal steps are one simple \(s\le f\). Reading the picture in horizontal bands, rather than in vertical strips, is the whole of the definition.

The first convergence theorem is the engine of the other two. When the functions only rise, mass cannot disappear. The integrals rise to the integral of the limit, and both sides may be infinite.

\begin{theorem}[monotone convergence]\label{thm:mct6}
If \(0\le f_1\le f_2\le\cdots\) are measurable and \(f_n\to f\) pointwise, then \(\int f_n\,d\mu\to\int f\,d\mu\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(b) makes \(f\) measurable. The integrals \(\int f_n\) increase, and the inequality \(f_n\le f\) forces their limit \(L\) to satisfy \(L\le\int f\).

For the reverse, take a simple \(s=\sum_ia_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\) with \(0\le s\le f\) and the sets \(A_i\) disjoint, and fix \(c\in\,]0,1[\). The sets \(E_n=\{f_n\ge cs\}\) increase. Their union is all of \(E\). Where \(s(x)>0\), one has \(f_n(x)\to f(x)\ge s(x)>cs(x)\), so \(x\) eventually lies in \(E_n\). Where \(s(x)=0\), every \(E_n\) contains \(x\). Thus
\[
\int f_n\ge\int cs\mathbf 1_{E_n}=c\sum_ia_i\mu(A_i\cap E_n).
\]
Continuity from below (Proposition~\ref{prop:measureprops}(c)) sends \(\mu(A_i\cap E_n)\) to \(\mu(A_i)\), so the right-hand side tends to \(c\int s\). Hence \(L\ge c\int s\). Let \(c\uparrow1\), then take the supremum over \(s\). Both steps are valid in \([0,+\infty]\).
\end{proof}

A one-line check: \(f_n=\mathbf 1_{[0,1-1/n]}\) increases to \(\mathbf 1_{[0,1)}\), and the integrals \(1-1/n\) tend to \(1\).

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:linearity6}
For measurable \(f,g\ge0\) and \(a,b\ge0\), \(\int(af+bg)=a\int f+b\int g\). For measurable \(f_n\ge0\), \(\int\sum_nf_n=\sum_n\int f_n\). If \(f\ge0\), then \(\nu(A)=\int_Af\,d\mu\) is a measure.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(c) supplies simple functions increasing to \(f\) and to \(g\). Their linear combination increases to \(af+bg\). The identity holds for simple functions, and monotone convergence passes it to the limit. The partial sums of \(\sum_nf_n\) increase to the sum, so the series form is another case of the theorem. For \(\nu\), apply that series form to the functions \(f\mathbf 1_{A_k}\) with the \(A_k\) disjoint: their sum is \(f\mathbf 1_{\bigcup A_k}\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:markov}
Let \(f\ge0\) be measurable. (a) (Markov) \(\mu(f\ge t)\le\frac1t\int f\,d\mu\) for \(t>0\). (b) \(\int f=0\) iff \(f=0\) a.e. (c) If \(\int f<\infty\), then \(f<\infty\) a.e. (d) If \(f=g\) a.e., then
\(\int f=\int g\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The inequality \(t\mathbf 1_{\{f\ge t\}}\le f\) integrates to the claim.

(b) If \(\int f=0\), part (a) yields \(\mu(f\ge1/k)=0\) for every \(k\ge1\). The set \(\{f>0\}\) is the union of these null sets. Conversely, if \(f=0\) almost everywhere, every simple \(s\) with \(0\le s\le f\) vanishes almost everywhere, so each term \(a_i\mu(A_i)\) in its integral is \(0\). The supremum is \(0\).

(c) For every \(t>0\), \(\mu(f=\infty)\le\mu(f\ge t)\le t^{-1}\int f\). The right-hand side tends to \(0\) as \(t\to\infty\).

(d) Let \(N\) be a null set with \(f=g\) off \(N\). Corollary~\ref{cor:linearity6} splits the integral into \(N\) and its complement. The function \(f\mathbf 1_N\) is \(0\) almost everywhere, so part (b) gives \(\int f\mathbf 1_N=0\). The same holds for \(g\), and \(f\mathbf 1_{N^c}=g\mathbf 1_{N^c}\).
\end{proof}

Markov is sharp enough to be useful and too crude to be the last word. For \(f=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}\) and \(t=\frac12\), the set \(\{f\ge t\}\) has measure \(1\), while \(t^{-1}\int f=2\). The inequality reads \(1\le2\).

\begin{theorem}[Fatou's lemma]\label{thm:fatou6}
For measurable \(f_n\ge0\), \(\int\liminf_nf_n\,d\mu\le\liminf_n\int f_n\,d\mu\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The functions \(g_m=\inf_{n\ge m}f_n\) increase to \(\liminf_nf_n\), and \(g_m\le f_n\) whenever \(n\ge m\), so \(\int g_m\le\inf_{n\ge m}\int f_n\). Let \(m\to\infty\) and apply monotone convergence on the left.
\end{proof}

Fatou gives an inequality, and the inequality can be strict. The spikes below have left side \(0\) and right side \(1\).

A complex-valued function is called measurable when its real and imaginary parts are measurable. Then \(|f|\), \(\operatorname{Re}f\) and \(\operatorname{Im}f\) are measurable.

\begin{definition}
A measurable \(f:E\to\R\) (or \(\C\)) is \emph{integrable} when \(\int|f|\,d\mu<\infty\). One then sets \(\int f=\int f^+-\int f^-\) for real \(f\), and \(\int f=\int\operatorname{Re}f+i\int\operatorname{Im}f\) for complex \(f\). The integrable functions
form a vector space \(\mathcal L^1(\mu)\), on which \(f\mapsto\int f\) is linear and \(|\int f|\le\int|f|\).
\end{definition}

The real case of linearity comes from Corollary~\ref{cor:linearity6}. If \(h=f+g\), the nonnegative functions \(h^++f^-+g^-\) and \(h^-+f^++g^+\) agree at every point, because their difference is \(h-(f+g)=0\). Integrate both sides and rearrange. Each piece has a finite integral, since it is at most \(|f|+|g|\). For a scalar \(c\ge0\), \((cf)^+=cf^+\) and \((cf)^-=cf^-\). For \(c<0\) the two parts exchange, and a minus sign comes out. For a complex integrand, split into real and imaginary parts. In particular \(\int(if)=i\int f\), because \(i(u+iv)=-v+iu\).

For the absolute-value inequality with complex \(f\), choose a complex number \(c\) with \(|c|=1\) and \(c\int f=|\int f|\). Then \(|\int f|=\operatorname{Re}(c\int f)=\int\operatorname{Re}(cf)\le\int|cf|=\int|f|\).

\begin{theorem}[dominated convergence]\label{thm:dct6}
Let \(f_n\) and \(f\) be measurable, with \(f_n\to f\) a.e., and \(|f_n|\le g\) a.e.\ for an integrable \(g\). Then \(f\) is integrable, \(\int|f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0\), and \(\int f_n\to\int f\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(N\) be a null set off which \(f_n(x)\to f(x)\) and \(|f_n(x)|\le g(x)\). Replace \(f_n\) and \(g\) by \(0\) on \(N\), and define \(\varphi\) to be \(\lim_nf_n\) off \(N\) and \(0\) on \(N\). For a complex sequence, do this separately for the real and imaginary parts. The new functions are measurable, \(f_n\to\varphi\) at every point, and \(|f_n|\le g\) at every point. Proposition~\ref{prop:markov}(d) says that the integrals are the same as before the replacement. In particular \(|\varphi|\le g\), so \(\varphi\) is integrable, and the nonnegative function \(2g-|f_n-\varphi|\) is available for Fatou's lemma:
\[
\int 2g\le\liminf_n\int\bigl(2g-|f_n-\varphi|\bigr)=\int 2g-\limsup_n\int|f_n-\varphi|.
\]
The number \(\int g\) is finite, so the \(\int 2g\) terms cancel and \(\limsup_n\int|f_n-\varphi|\le0\). Thus \(\int|f_n-\varphi|\to0\). The triangle inequality \(|\int f_n-\int\varphi|\le\int|f_n-\varphi|\) gives \(\int f_n\to\int\varphi\).

The hypothesis says that \(f\) itself is measurable. Off \(N\), \(f\) equals \(\varphi\), so \(|f|\) and \(|\varphi|\) agree almost everywhere. Proposition~\ref{prop:markov}(d) gives \(\int|f|=\int|\varphi|<\infty\), and \(f\) is integrable. The same comparison on the positive and negative parts, and on the real and imaginary parts when \(f\) is complex, gives \(\int f=\int\varphi\) and \(\int|f_n-f|=\int|f_n-\varphi|\). Therefore \(\int|f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0\) and \(\int f_n\to\int f\).
\end{proof}

On a finite measure space a constant is integrable. \emph{Bounded convergence} is the case \(g=M\) with \(\mu(E)<\infty\): a uniformly bounded sequence that converges almost everywhere to a measurable function may pass inside the integral. Counting measure on \(\N\) turns integrals into series, and the three theorems into theorems about series. The dominated case is Tannery's theorem. If \(a_{n,k}\to a_k\) and \(|a_{n,k}|\le b_k\) with \(\sum_kb_k<\infty\), then \(\sum_k\lim_na_{n,k}=\lim_n\sum_ka_{n,k}\).

Let \(f_n=n\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}\) on \([0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure. Then \(f_n(x)\to0\) at every \(x\), while \(\int f_n=1\) for every \(n\). Which hypothesis of each of the three theorems fails?
\end{think}

At a fixed \(x>0\) only the blocks with \(1/n>x\) cover \(x\), so the pointwise limit is \(0\) and the integral of the limit is \(0\). The sequence is not increasing. At \(x=\frac12\) one has \(f_1\bigl(\frac12\bigr)=1\) and \(f_n\bigl(\frac12\bigr)=0\) for every \(n\ge2\). Fatou's lemma does apply, and it is strict, since \(0<1\). The integral of \(1/(2x)\) over \(]0,\frac12]\) diverges, so no integrable \(g\) sits above every block. The mass \(1\) slips toward the point \(0\), where the limit cannot see it. \section{Riemann's integral, and the criterion}

The comparison with Riemann starts from Darboux sums. Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be bounded. For a subdivision \(P:a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=b\), the lower and upper sums are
\[L(f,P)=\sum_i(x_i-x_{i-1})\inf_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f,\qquad U(f,P)=\sum_i(x_i-x_{i-1})\sup_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f.\]
Refining \(P\) raises \(L\) and lowers \(U\). For any two subdivisions, \(L(f,P)\le U(f,P')\), because both sums sit on the correct side of the sums for the common refinement. The function \(f\) is \emph{Riemann integrable} when \(\sup_PL(f,P)=\inf_PU(f,P)\). The common value is written \(\int_a^bf\). Every Riemann sum \(\sum_i(x_i-x_{i-1})f(c_i)\), with \(c_i\) in the \(i\)-th interval, lies between \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\). So once \(f\) is Riemann integrable, the Riemann sums of Volume~1 approach the same number as the mesh of \(P\) tends to \(0\).

\begin{think}
Take the indicator of \(\Q\cap[0,1]\). For an arbitrary subdivision, what are the lower and upper Darboux sums? What is the Lebesgue integral? Which of the two integrals can tell that \(\Q\) is small?
\end{think}

On every subinterval the infimum of this indicator is \(0\) and the supremum is \(1\), because every interval contains both rationals and irrationals. The lower sum is \(0\) and the upper sum is \(1\). The Lebesgue integral is \(0\), since the function vanishes almost everywhere. Lebesgue's integral sees the smallness. Riemann's sums do not.

\begin{theorem}[the integral of a continuous function]\label{thm:riemanncont}
A continuous \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) is Riemann integrable. The integral is linear, monotone, additive over adjacent intervals, and \(\int_a^bc=c(b-a)\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Heine's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:heine}) supplies, for each \(\varepsilon>0\), a \(\delta>0\) such that \(|x-y|<\delta\) implies \(|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon\). If every interval of \(P\) is shorter than \(\delta\), then on each of them \(\sup f-\inf f\le\varepsilon\), so \(U(f,P)-L(f,P)\le\varepsilon(b-a)\). Always \(\sup_PL\le\inf_PU\). The gap between them is at most \(\varepsilon(b-a)\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\), so the two numbers agree.

The equalities \(\int_a^bc=c(b-a)\) and monotonicity are immediate from the sums: a larger function has larger infima and suprema. For additivity, a subdivision of \([a,c]\) joined to a subdivision of \([c,b]\) is a subdivision of \([a,b]\), and the sums add. For linearity, \(\inf f+\inf g\le\inf(f+g)\le\sup(f+g)\le\sup f+\sup g\), so
\[
L(f,P)+L(g,P)\le L(f+g,P)\le U(f+g,P)\le U(f,P)+U(g,P).
\]
If \(c\ge0\), then \(L(cf,P)=cL(f,P)\) and \(U(cf,P)=cU(f,P)\). If \(c<0\), infima and suprema exchange, and the common value of \(\sup L\) and \(\inf U\) is multiplied by \(c\).
\end{proof}

This is the integral Volume~1 used. The fundamental theorem, integration by parts, and Taylor's formula with remainder were derived there from the four properties in the theorem, so those arguments now rest on a proof given here. The next theorem compares that integral with \(\int_{[a,b]}f\,d\lambda\).

\begin{theorem}[Lebesgue, 1902]\label{thm:lebesguecriterion}
A bounded function \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) is Riemann integrable iff the set of its points of discontinuity is null. Then \(f\) is Lebesgue measurable and
\(\int_a^bf=\int_{[a,b]}f\,d\lambda\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(P_n\) cut \([a,b]\) into \(2^n\) equal intervals. On each open interval of \(P_n\), let \(\ell_n\) equal the infimum of \(f\) on the corresponding closed interval, and let \(u_n\) equal the supremum. At the finitely many grid points, set both functions equal to \(f\). Then \(\ell_n\le f\le u_n\). Refinement makes \(\ell_n\) increase and \(u_n\) decrease. The limits \(\ell\le f\le u\) are Borel measurable, being pointwise limits of the step functions \(\ell_n\) and \(u_n\). If \(|f|\le M\), the constant \(M\) is integrable on \([a,b]\). Dominated convergence gives \(\int\ell_n\,d\lambda=L(f,P_n)\to\int\ell\,d\lambda\) and \(U(f,P_n)\to\int u\,d\lambda\). The grid points do not affect the integrals.

\emph{Step 1: \(f\) is Riemann integrable if and only if \(U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)\to0\).} Suppose the difference tends to \(0\). For an arbitrary subdivision \(P\) and every \(n\), \(L(f,P)\le U(f,P_n)\), so \(L(f,P)\le\lim_nU(f,P_n)\). Hence \(\sup L\le\lim U(f,P_n)\). The same limit equals \(\lim L(f,P_n)\), which is at most \(\sup L\). The matching argument for upper sums gives \(\sup L=\inf U\).

Conversely, suppose some subdivision \(P\) satisfies \(U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon\), and let \(P\) have \(m\) division points strictly between \(a\) and \(b\). An interval of \(P_n\) that contains none of the \(m+2\) points of \(P\) (the endpoints included) lies inside one interval of \(P\), so its contribution to \(U-L\) is at most the contribution of that interval of \(P\). Each point of \(P\) lies in at most two intervals of \(P_n\), so at most \(2(m+2)\) intervals of \(P_n\) meet \(P\). On each of those, \(\sup f-\inf f\le2M\), and the length is \((b-a)2^{-n}\). Therefore
\[
U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)\le U(f,P)-L(f,P)+2(m+2)\cdot2M\cdot\frac{b-a}{2^n}.
\]
For large \(n\) the second term is less than \(\varepsilon\), and the difference of Darboux sums is less than \(2\varepsilon\).

\emph{Step 2: off the countable set \(D=\bigcup_nP_n\), continuity of \(f\) at \(x\) is equivalent to \(\ell(x)=u(x)\).} For \(x\notin D\), the interval of \(P_n\) that contains \(x\) is a neighbourhood of \(x\), and these neighbourhoods shrink to \(x\). If \(f\) is continuous at \(x\), the oscillation of \(f\) on that interval tends to \(0\), so \(u(x)=\ell(x)\). If \(u(x)-\ell(x)<\varepsilon\), then for large \(n\) one has \(\sup f-\inf f<\varepsilon\) on a neighbourhood of \(x\). So \(\ell(x)=u(x)\) means that the oscillation at \(x\) is \(0\).

\emph{Conclusion.} Step~1, together with the two limits at the start of the proof, says that \(f\) is Riemann integrable if and only if \(\int(u-\ell)\,d\lambda=0\). By Proposition~\ref{prop:markov}(b) this happens if and only if \(u=\ell\) almost everywhere. Step~2 and the countability of \(D\) turn that into continuity almost everywhere. Then \(\ell=f=u\) almost everywhere. The function \(f\) differs from the Borel function \(\ell\) on a subset of a null set. Lebesgue measure is complete (Theorem~\ref{thm:caratheodory}), so \(f\) is Lebesgue measurable, and
\[
\int_a^bf=\lim_nL(f,P_n)=\int\ell\,d\lambda=\int f\,d\lambda.
\]
\end{proof}

Monotone functions, continuous functions, and functions with only countably many discontinuities are therefore Riemann integrable: a monotone function is discontinuous on an at most countable set. The indicator of \(\Q\cap[0,1]\) is discontinuous at every point, so it is not Riemann integrable, even though \(\int\mathbf 1_\Q\,d\lambda=0\). Thomae's function, Exercise~\ref{ex:thomae}, is continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every rational. The discontinuities are countable, the criterion applies, and the function equals \(0\) almost everywhere, so the integral is \(0\). From here on, \(\int_a^bf\,dx\) means the Lebesgue integral. On the functions used in Volumes~1 to~5 the two integrals agree, by the theorem just proved.

\emph{Improper integrals.} Suppose \(f\ge0\) is continuous on \([a,+\infty[\). The functions \(f\mathbf 1_{[a,x]}\) increase to \(f\mathbf 1_{[a,\infty[}\) as \(x\to\infty\). Monotone convergence says that the improper Riemann integral \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_a^xf\) equals \(\int_{[a,\infty[}f\,d\lambda\), whether the common value is finite or not. For a signed continuous \(f\) with \(\int|f|<\infty\), dominated convergence gives the same agreement along any sequence of upper limits tending to infinity. The two integrals part company for \(\frac{\sin t}t\). Exercise~\ref{ex:dirichlet} shows that \(\int_0^x\frac{\sin t}t\,dt\) tends to \(\frac\pi2\). On the other hand \(\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin t|}t\,dt=\infty\): on \([k\pi,(k+1)\pi]\) one has \(1/t\ge1/((k+1)\pi)\) and \(\int|\sin t|\,dt=2\), so the piece is at least \(2/((k+1)\pi)\), and the series of those lower bounds diverges. Thus \(\frac{\sin t}t\) is not Lebesgue integrable on \([0,\infty[\). Its improper integral is a limit of proper integrals, not an integral of an integrable function.

\section{Modes of convergence}

Pointwise convergence is one way a sequence of functions can settle. Two others are used just as often.

\begin{definition}
Let \(f\) and \(f_n\) be measurable. The sequence \emph{converges in measure} to \(f\) when \(\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)\to0\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\), and \emph{in \(L^1\)} when \(\int|f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0\).
\end{definition}

On a probability space, convergence in measure is convergence in probability, and convergence in \(L^1\) is \(\mathbb E|X_n-X|\to0\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:modes}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Convergence in \(L^1\) implies convergence in measure.
\item If \(f_n\to f\) in measure, a subsequence converges to \(f\) a.e.
\item If \(\mu(E)<\infty\), convergence a.e.\ implies convergence in measure.
\item (Egorov) If \(\mu(E)<\infty\) and \(f_n\to f\) a.e.\ (all finite), then for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(A\) with \(\mu(A)<\varepsilon\) such that \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on \(E\setminus A\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Markov's inequality gives \(\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)\le\varepsilon^{-1}\int|f_n-f|\).

(b) Choose indices \(n_1<n_2<\cdots\) with \(\mu(|f_{n_k}-f|>2^{-k})<2^{-k}\). The sum of these measures converges, so Borel--Cantelli (Proposition~\ref{prop:measureprops}(e)) puts almost every \(x\) in only finitely many of the sets. At such an \(x\), \(|f_{n_k}(x)-f(x)|\le2^{-k}\) for all large \(k\).

(c) The indicators \(\mathbf 1_{\{|f_n-f|>\varepsilon\}}\) tend to \(0\) almost everywhere and are bounded by \(1\). Bounded convergence, which is dominated convergence with dominant \(1\), yields \(\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)\to0\).

(d) Fix \(k\ge1\) and set \(B_{k,m}=\bigcup_{n\ge m}\{|f_n-f|>1/k\}\). As \(m\) grows these sets decrease, and their intersection is contained in the null set where the convergence fails. Since \(\mu(E)<\infty\), one has \(\mu(B_{k,1})<\infty\), so continuity from above (Proposition~\ref{prop:measureprops}(d)) supplies an index \(m_k\) with \(\mu(B_{k,m_k})<\varepsilon2^{-k}\). The union \(A=\bigcup_kB_{k,m_k}\) satisfies \(\mu(A)<\varepsilon\). Off \(A\), every \(k\) obeys \(|f_n-f|\le1/k\) for all \(n\ge m_k\).
\end{proof}

Egorov's theorem is Littlewood's third principle: almost everywhere convergence is uniform convergence off a set you are allowed to make small. None of the four implications reverses in general. The typewriter sequence of Exercise~\ref{ex:typewriter} converges in measure and in \(L^1\), and the sequence of values \((f_n(x))\) converges at no \(x\). The spikes \(n\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}\) converge at every point and fail to converge in \(L^1\).

\section{Vitali and uniform integrability}\label{sec:ui}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch07-escape}
\caption{Left, the spikes \(f_n=n\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}\) for \(n=1,2,4,8\). Each has integral \(1\), and the pointwise limit \(0\) has integral \(0\): the mass escapes into a thinner spike. Right, \(g_n=\sqrt n\,\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}\), which also tends to \(0\). If \(\sqrt n>M\) the integral past height \(M\) equals \(1/\sqrt n\); if the spike never reaches \(M\), that tail is \(0\). In either case the tail is at most \(1/M\). The family is uniformly integrable, and the integrals tend to \(0\).}
\label{fig:escape}
\end{figure}

The right-hand family in Figure~\ref{fig:escape} is the model of the definition. The tails, past any fixed height \(M\), become uniformly small.

\begin{definition}
Let \(\mu\) be finite. A family \(\mathcal F\) of integrable functions is \emph{uniformly integrable} when
\[\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\int_{\{|f|>M\}}|f|\,d\mu\longrightarrow0\qquad\text{as }M\to\infty.\]
\end{definition}

A single integrable \(f\) forms a uniformly integrable family. Indeed \(f\) is finite almost everywhere by Proposition~\ref{prop:markov}(c), so
\[
|f|\mathbf 1_{\{|f|>M\}}\to0
\]
almost everywhere, and the functions are dominated by the integrable function \(|f|\). Dominated convergence sends the tails to \(0\). A finite union of uniformly integrable families is uniformly integrable: take the worst tail. A family dominated by one integrable \(g\) is uniformly integrable as well. If \(|f|\le g\), then \(\{|f|>M\}\subseteq\{|g|>M\}\) and the tail of \(f\) is at most the tail of \(g\). Any uniformly integrable family is bounded in \(L^1\). Choose \(M\) so that every tail is at most \(1\). Then \(\int|f|\le M\mu(E)+1\). On a probability space the definition reads \(\sup_X\mathbb E\bigl[|X|\mathbf 1_{\{|X|>M\}}\bigr]\to0\): no mass is allowed to hide in the far tails.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:abscont}
If \(f\) is integrable, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(\delta>0\) such that \(\mu(A)<\delta\) implies \(\int_A|f|<\varepsilon\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split the integral at a height: \(\int_A|f|\le M\mu(A)+\int_{\{|f|>M\}}|f|\). First choose \(M\) so that the second term is less than \(\varepsilon/2\). Then set \(\delta=\varepsilon/(2M)\), or \(\delta=1\) if \(M=0\).
\end{proof}

Giuseppe Vitali, in 1907, named the exact extra hypothesis. The paper is the one cited in the sources. It is the same Vitali who had built a set that carries no translation-invariant measure. His convergence theorem says that the integrals follow the functions precisely when no mass escapes.

\begin{theorem}[Vitali's convergence theorem]\label{thm:vitali7}
Let \(\mu\) be finite, \(f_n\) integrable and \(f_n\to f\) in measure. Then \(f\) is integrable and \(\int|f_n-f|\,d\mu\to0\) iff \((f_n)\) is uniformly integrable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First suppose \((f_n)\) is uniformly integrable. The \(L^1\) bound recorded above gives \(C=\sup_n\int|f_n|<\infty\). Proposition~\ref{prop:modes}(b) extracts a subsequence converging to \(f\) almost everywhere. Convergence in measure, as defined above, already requires \(f\) to be measurable, so Fatou's lemma applies to \(|f|\) and gives \(\int|f|\le C\).

Let \(\varphi_M(t)=\max(-M,\min(M,t))\). This truncation is \(1\)-Lipschitz, and \(|t-\varphi_M(t)|\le|t|\mathbf 1_{\{|t|>M\}}\). Split
\[\begin{aligned}\int|f_n-f|&\le\int|f_n-\varphi_M(f_n)|+\int|\varphi_M(f_n)-\varphi_M(f)|\eqbr+\int|\varphi_M(f)-f|.\end{aligned}\]
The first term is at most \(\sup_n\int_{\{|f_n|>M\}}|f_n|\), and the third is at most \(\int_{\{|f|>M\}}|f|\). Uniform integrability of the sequence, and the single-function case for \(f\), make both smaller than \(\varepsilon\) once \(M\) is large. Freeze that \(M\). The middle integrand \(h_n\) satisfies \(h_n\le|f_n-f|\) and \(h_n\le2M\). Split the domain into \(\{|f_n-f|\le\varepsilon\}\) and its complement:
\[
\int h_n\le\varepsilon\,\mu(E)+2M\,\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon).
\]
Convergence in measure makes the second term smaller than \(\varepsilon\) for large \(n\), so \(\int h_n<\varepsilon(\mu(E)+1)\). Therefore \(\limsup_n\int|f_n-f|\le\varepsilon(\mu(E)+3)\). The number \(\varepsilon>0\) was arbitrary, so the limsup is \(0\).

Conversely, suppose \(\int|f_n-f|\to0\). The numbers \(\int|f_n|\) stay bounded, because they form a convergent sequence after adding the fixed \(\int|f|\); call the bound \(C\). Fix \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(N\) so that \(n\ge N\) implies \(\int|f_n-f|<\varepsilon\). Lemma~\ref{lem:abscont} gives a \(\delta>0\) for this \(f\) and this \(\varepsilon\). Markov's inequality yields \(\mu(|f_n|>M)\le C/M\), so one \(M\) makes the inequality \(< \delta\) for every \(n\). Then, for \(n\ge N\),
\[\int_{\{|f_n|>M\}}|f_n|\le\int|f_n-f|+\int_{\{|f_n|>M\}}|f|<2\varepsilon.\]
The finitely many functions \(f_n\) with \(n<N\) form a uniformly integrable family, so a possibly larger \(M\) brings their tails under \(2\varepsilon\) as well.
\end{proof}

In probability language: if \(X_n\to X\) in probability, then \(\mathbb E|X_n-X|\to0\) if and only if \((X_n)\) is uniformly integrable, and in that case \(\mathbb E[X_n]\to\mathbb E[X]\). Dominated convergence is the special case in which one integrable function bounds the whole family. Exercise~\ref{ex:vallee} gives a practical sufficient condition, \(\sup_n\mathbb E[|X_n|^p]<\infty\) for some \(p>1\). Volume~11 uses the theorem for martingales; nothing proved here depends on that later use.

\section{Integrals depending on a parameter}

Differentiating under the integral sign needs one integrable bound on the \(t\)-derivative, the same bound for every \(t\) you are using. Dominated convergence then moves the limit inside.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:parameter}
Let \(T\) be a metric space (for (b), an open interval of \(\R\)), and \(h:E\times T\to\R\) (or \(\C\)) with \(h(\cdot,t)\) integrable for each \(t\). Let \(H(t)=\int h(x,t)\,d\mu(x)\).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Continuity) If \(t\mapsto h(x,t)\) is continuous at \(t_0\) for a.e.\ \(x\), and \(|h(x,t)|\le g(x)\) for all \(t\) near \(t_0\) with \(g\) integrable, then \(H\) is continuous at \(t_0\).
\item (Differentiation) If \(t\mapsto h(x,t)\) is differentiable on \(T\) for a.e.\ \(x\), and \(|\partial_th(x,t)|\le g(x)\) for all \(t\in T\) with \(g\) integrable, then \(H\) is differentiable on \(T\) and
\[H'(t)=\int\partial_th(x,t)\,d\mu(x).\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) If \(t_n\to t_0\), the functions \(h(\cdot,t_n)\) converge almost everywhere to \(h(\cdot,t_0)\) and are dominated by \(g\). Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6} gives \(H(t_n)\to H(t_0)\).

(b) Fix \(t\) and a sequence \(t_n\to t\) with \(t_n\ne t\). The difference quotients
\[
q_n(x)=\frac{h(x,t_n)-h(x,t)}{t_n-t}
\]
converge to \(\partial_th(x,t)\) for almost every \(x\). Write \(h=u+iv\) in real and imaginary parts, allowing \(v=0\) when \(h\) is real. The mean value theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:mvt}) supplies a point between \(t\) and \(t_n\) at which the difference quotient of \(u\) equals \(\partial_tu\). That partial is at most \(g\) in absolute value, because \(|\partial_tu|\le|\partial_th|\). The same bound holds for \(v\). Hence \(|q_n|\le2g\). The factor \(2\) is the sum of the two parts; a real integrand only needs the factor \(1\), and \(2g\) still dominates it.

The functions \(q_n\) are measurable. Their pointwise limit off the null set, redefined as \(0\) on that null set, is a measurable function \(\psi\) with \(|\psi|\le2g\), and \(\psi=\partial_th(\cdot,t)\) almost everywhere on the set where the derivative exists. Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6} yields
\[
\frac{H(t_n)-H(t)}{t_n-t}=\int q_n\,d\mu\to\int\psi\,d\mu.
\]
Every sequence \(t_n\to t\) gives the same limit, so \(H\) is differentiable at \(t\) and the derivative equals that integral. The integrand in the statement is this measurable version of the partial derivative.
\end{proof}

The bound has to be independent of \(t\), but only on a neighbourhood of the point where you differentiate. In practice one restricts \(t\) to a compact subinterval, finds a dominant there, and concludes. Differentiability is a local property, so the local conclusions cover \(T\). The continuous case used in Volume~5, Chapter~4, is the special case in which \(h\) and the dominant are continuous. Exercises~\ref{ex:gausscos} and~\ref{ex:dirichlet} are the standard illustrations.

\begin{beauty}
Sorting the values, rather than the variable, looks like a change of bookkeeping. It lets a limit pass under the integral when the functions rise, or when one integrable function sits on top of them. The same definition serves for lengths, probabilities, counting measure, and sums of series. Chapter~\ref{ch:lp} will show that the integrable functions are complete in the \(L^1\) metric. Volume~8 uses the same integral as Haar measure on a group. The coin count was the right count.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Every nonnegative measurable function on every measure space now has an integral in \([0,+\infty]\), and every integrable function has a finite integral. Three theorems move a limit through the integral sign: monotone convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:mct6}), Fatou's lemma (Theorem~\ref{thm:fatou6}), and dominated convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6}). On a bounded interval, the new integral agrees with Riemann's whenever Riemann's exists, and Lebesgue's criterion (Theorem~\ref{thm:lebesguecriterion}) says when that happens. On a finite measure space, Vitali's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:vitali7}) is the exact condition for convergence in \(L^1\). Continuity and differentiability in a parameter (Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}) are dominated convergence applied to the integrands and to the difference quotients. Each proof in the chapter starts from the measure constructed in Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}, or from Heine's theorem.

The variable of integration may already be a point of \(\R^d\). The formulas calculus reaches for next still involve two genuine variables: integrate in \(x\) and then in \(y\), or in the other order, or pass to polar coordinates. Volumes~4 and~5 interchanged integrals and sums of nonnegative terms by quoting a theorem left to this volume. Chapter~\ref{ch:products} proves it.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:fatoustrict}
Find measurable \(f_n\ge0\) on \((\R,\lambda)\) with (a) \(f_n\) decreasing to \(0\) but \(\int f_n=+\infty\) for all \(n\); (b) \(f_n\to0\) everywhere, \(\int f_n=1\), and all \(f_n\) bounded by \(1\). For each sequence, name the theorem that does not apply and the hypothesis that is absent.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:typewriter}
\emph{The typewriter.} Write \(n=2^k+j\) with \(0\le j<2^k\) and let \(f_n=\mathbf 1_{[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k}]}\) on \([0,1]\). Show that \(f_n\to0\) in \(L^1\) and in measure, but that \((f_n(x))\) converges for no \(x\). Find a
subsequence converging a.e.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:riemannalgebra}
Show, with Lebesgue's criterion, that the Riemann integrable functions on \([a,b]\) form an algebra stable under \(|\cdot|\), \(\max\) and \(\min\), and that \(g\circ f\) is Riemann integrable
when \(f\) is and \(g\) is continuous. Is \(f\circ g\) always Riemann integrable when \(f\) is Riemann integrable and \(g\) continuous?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:layercake}
\emph{The layer cake formula.} Let \(f\ge0\) be measurable. Show that \(\int f\,d\mu=\int_0^\infty\mu(f>t)\,dt\). (First for simple \(f\), then by monotone convergence.) In probability,
\(\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X>t)\,dt\) for \(X\ge0\), and \(\mathbb E[X^p]=\int_0^\infty pt^{p-1}\mathbb P(X>t)\,dt\) for \(p>0\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:scheffe}
\emph{Scheff\'e's lemma.} Let \(f_n,f\ge0\) be integrable with \(f_n\to f\) a.e.\ and \(\int f_n\to\int f\). Show that \(\int|f_n-f|\to0\). Deduce that if densities \(p_n\) of laws on \(\R\) converge a.e.\ to a
density \(p\), the laws converge in total variation: \(\sup_B|\int_Bp_n-\int_Bp|\to0\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:vallee}
\emph{De la Vall\'ee Poussin's criterion.} Let \(\mu\) be finite. Show that a family \(\mathcal F\) is uniformly integrable if \(\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\int\Phi(|f|)\,d\mu<\infty\) for some \(\Phi:[0,\infty[\to[0,\infty[\) with
\(\frac{\Phi(t)}t\to\infty\); for instance \(\Phi(t)=t^p\), \(p>1\), or \(t\log^+t\). (The converse is true as well; it is not asked for here.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:gausscos}
Let \(I(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\cos(tx)\,dx\). Justify \(I'(t)=-\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}\sin(tx)\,dx\), integrate by parts to reach \(I'=-\frac t2I\), and deduce \(I(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2e^{-t^2/4}\). What does the formula say about
the characteristic function of a standard Gaussian, as computed in Volume~4?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rlemma}
\emph{The Riemann--Lebesgue lemma.} Let \(f\) be integrable on \(\R\). Show that \(\int f(x)e^{i\xi x}\,dx\to0\) as \(|\xi|\to\infty\). (Approximate \(f\) in \(L^1\) by a step function, using the regularity of
\(\lambda\) from Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}.) Volume~1 proved the lemma only for \(C^1\) functions.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:dirichlet}
\emph{The Dirichlet integral.} Let \(F(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}x\,dx\) for \(t>0\). (a) Show that \(F\) is differentiable on \(]0,\infty[\) with \(F'(t)=-\frac1{1+t^2}\), and that \(F(t)\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\); deduce
\(F(t)=\frac\pi2-\arctan t\). (b) Show that \(\int_0^R\frac{\sin x}x\,dx\to\frac\pi2\) as \(R\to\infty\), by proving that the improper integral is continuous at \(0\): bound
\(\bigl|\int_R^\infty e^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}x\,dx\bigr|\) uniformly in \(t\ge0\) by an integration by parts.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:limits}
\emph{Two limits.} Compute
\[\text{(a)}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^n\Bigl(1-\frac xn\Bigr)^ne^{x/2}\,dx,\qquad\text{(b)}\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty\frac{n\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx.\]
Name the theorem that justifies each limit, and write down a function that dominates every integrand.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v7c08a}
Let \(s=2\mathbf 1_{[0,1)}+5\mathbf 1_{[1,3]}\) on \(\R\), and let \(\lambda\) be Lebesgue measure. Compute \(\int s\,d\lambda\) from the disjoint representation. Then, for each \(t\ge0\), compute \(\lambda(s>t)\), and evaluate \(\int_0^\infty\lambda(s>t)\,dt\) by splitting the \(t\)-axis at \(2\) and at \(5\). The two numbers should agree.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c08b}
On \([0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure, justify the equality
\[
\int_0^1\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{x^n}{2^n}\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\int_0^1\frac{x^n}{2^n}\,dx
\]
and compute the common value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c08c}
Let \(f_n(x)=ne^{-nx}\) for \(x\ge0\). Show that \(f_n\to0\) almost everywhere, that \(\int_0^\infty f_n=1\) for every \(n\), and that every measurable \(g\ge f_n\) for all \(n\) has \(\int g=\infty\). Say what Fatou's lemma reports about these numbers, and why monotone convergence does not apply. Then set \(h_n(x)=e^{-nx}\) on \([0,1]\). Show that dominated convergence does apply to \((h_n)\), and compute \(\lim_n\int_0^1 h_n\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c08d}
Let \(f(x)=x^{-1/2}\) on \(]0,1]\), with Lebesgue measure. Show that \(\int_{]0,1]}f\,d\lambda=2\). Then produce a number \(\delta>0\) such that \(\lambda(A)<\delta\) implies \(\int_A f\,d\lambda<\frac1{10}\). Use the split in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:abscont}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:fatoustrict}
\idea A downward limit needs a finite integral at the start. A dominant has to be integrable, and on an infinite space the constant \(1\) is not.
\writeup (a) Let \(f_n=\mathbf 1_{[n,\infty[}\). The sequence decreases pointwise to \(0\), and \(\int f_n=\infty\) for every \(n\). Monotone convergence is proved for increasing sequences. The downward companion needs a finite first integral. If \(0\le f_{n+1}\le f_n\) and \(\int f_1<\infty\), then \(f_1-f_n\) increases to \(f_1-\lim f_n\), and \(f_1\) dominates, so dominated convergence gives \(\int f_n\to\int\lim f_n\). Here \(\int f_1\) is already infinite, and that route is closed.

(b) Let \(f_n=\mathbf 1_{[n,n+1]}\). Each \(x\) belongs to at most one of these intervals, so \(f_n(x)\to0\). The integral equals \(1\). Also \(0\le f_n\le1\). Lebesgue measure of \(\R\) is infinite, so the constant function \(1\) is not integrable, and neither is \(\sup_nf_n=\mathbf 1_{[1,\infty[}\). Dominated convergence does not apply. Fatou's lemma does apply, and \(0\le1\) is a strict inequality.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:typewriter}
\idea The bump sweeps \([0,1]\) over and over, half as wide at each generation. It is small in integral, and it returns to every point once per sweep.

\begin{solution}{ex:riemannalgebra}
\idea The criterion turns Riemann integrability into a statement about the discontinuity set. Those sets behave well under the algebra operations.
\writeup Let \(D_f\) be the discontinuity set of \(f\). If \(f\) and \(g\) are both continuous at \(x\), then so are \(f+g\), \(fg\), \(|f|\), \(\max(f,g)\), \(\min(f,g)\), and \(h\circ f\) for any continuous \(h\). The discontinuities of each combination therefore sit inside \(D_f\cup D_g\). When \(f\) and \(g\) are Riemann integrable that union is null, the combination is bounded, and Theorem~\ref{thm:lebesguecriterion} applies.

The opposite order can fail. Let \(K\subseteq[0,1]\) be a fat Cantor set of measure \(\frac12\) (Exercise~\ref{ex:fatcantor}), and set \(g(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\). Then \(g\) is continuous, and \(g(x)=0\) exactly on \(K\), because \(K\) is closed. Let \(f=\mathbf 1_{]0,\infty[}\). On any segment, \(f\) has one discontinuity, so \(f\) is Riemann integrable. The composition \(f\circ g=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]\setminus K}\) equals \(0\) on \(K\) and equals \(1\) off \(K\). Since \(K\) has empty interior, every neighbourhood of a point of \(K\) meets the complement, so \(f\circ g\) is discontinuous at every point of \(K\). That set has measure \(\frac12\). Theorem~\ref{thm:lebesguecriterion} says \(f\circ g\) is not Riemann integrable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:layercake}
\idea Integrating \(\mu(f>t)\) in the height variable \(t\) stacks the horizontal slices under the graph. Monotone convergence assembles the slices; a later change-of-variables theorem is not required.
\writeup Let \(s=\sum_{i\le m}a_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\) with \(0=a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_m\) and the \(A_i\) disjoint. For \(a_{k-1}\le t<a_k\) one has \(\mu(s>t)=\sum_{i\ge k}\mu(A_i)\), so
\[\int_0^\infty\mu(s>t)\,dt=\sum_{k\le m}(a_k-a_{k-1})\sum_{i\ge k}\mu(A_i)=\sum_i\mu(A_i)\sum_{k\le i}(a_k-a_{k-1})=\sum_ia_i\mu(A_i).\]
For a general measurable \(f\ge0\), Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(c) gives simple \(s_n\uparrow f\). If \(f(x)>t\), then \(s_n(x)>t\) for large \(n\), so \(\{s_n>t\}\) increases to \(\{f>t\}\) and \(\mu(s_n>t)\uparrow\mu(f>t)\). The map \(t\mapsto\mu(s_n>t)\) is non-increasing, hence Borel: its strict superlevel set is an interval. Monotone convergence in the \(t\)-variable, and monotone convergence for \(\int s_n\), give
\[
\int f\,d\mu=\lim_n\int s_n\,d\mu=\lim_n\int_0^\infty\mu(s_n>t)\,dt=\int_0^\infty\mu(f>t)\,dt.
\]
On a probability space, apply this to the random variable \(X\ge0\). That is the formula for \(\mathbb E[X]\).

For \(p>0\), the same formula applied to \(X^p\) reads \(\mathbb E[X^p]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X^p>s)\,ds\). Write \(\varphi(s)=\mathbb P(X^p>s)\). This \(\varphi\) is non-increasing. The simple functions \(\sigma_n=\min\bigl(n,2^{-n}\lfloor2^n\varphi\rfloor\bigr)\) increase to \(\varphi\), and each \(\sigma_n\) is a finite sum \(\sum_kc_k\mathbf 1_{[0,a_k)}\) (a single point does not affect the integral). For \(\psi=\mathbf 1_{[0,a)}\) with \(a<\infty\),
\[
\int_0^\infty\psi(s)\,ds=a.
\]
The substituted integral is handled by monotone convergence as well. On \([\frac1n,a^{1/p}]\) the function \(pt^{p-1}\) is continuous, and
\[
\int_{1/n}^{a^{1/p}}pt^{p-1}\,dt=a-n^{-p}.
\]
The integrands increase to \(pt^{p-1}\mathbf 1_{]0,a^{1/p}]}\), so monotone convergence gives \(\int_0^\infty pt^{p-1}\psi(t^p)\,dt=a\). If \(a=\infty\), both sides are infinite. Linearity extends the identity from \(\psi\) to each \(\sigma_n\). A further monotone convergence, now in the variable \(t\), passes it to \(\varphi\):
\[
\int_0^\infty\varphi(s)\,ds=\int_0^\infty pt^{p-1}\varphi(t^p)\,dt.
\]
For \(t\ge0\) and \(X\ge0\), the inequality \(X^p>t^p\) is the same as \(X>t\), because \(u\mapsto u^p\) is strictly increasing on \([0,\infty[\). That is the formula for \(\mathbb E[X^p]\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:scheffe}
\idea Once the integrals converge and the functions converge pointwise, the mass above \(f\) is paid for by the mass missing below \(f\). The part below \(f\) is dominated by \(f\).
\writeup The identity \(|f_n-f|=f_n+f-2\min(f_n,f)\) is a check at each \(x\): the minimum is the smaller height, and the absolute difference is the sum of the two heights minus twice that smaller height. One has \(0\le\min(f_n,f)\le f\) and \(\min(f_n,f)\to f\) almost everywhere, so dominated convergence yields \(\int\min(f_n,f)\to\int f\). Therefore
\[
\int|f_n-f|\to\int f+\int f-2\int f=0.
\]
For densities, \(\bigl|\int_Bp_n-\int_Bp\bigr|\le\int|p_n-p|\) for every measurable \(B\), and the right-hand side tends to \(0\) with no dependence on \(B\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:gausscos}
\idea One integrable bound on the \(t\)-derivative, the same bound for every \(t\), is the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}. Integration by parts then brings \(I\) back.
\writeup The integrand \(h(x,t)=e^{-x^2}\cos(tx)\) satisfies \(|\partial_th(x,t)|=|xe^{-x^2}\sin(tx)|\le xe^{-x^2}\), and \(xe^{-x^2}\) is integrable on \([0,\infty[\) because it is the derivative of \(-\frac12e^{-x^2}\) up to a sign, and the improper integral equals \(\frac12\). Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}(b) therefore applies on the whole line, and \(I'(t)=-\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}\sin(tx)\,dx\).

Integrate by parts on \([0,R]\) with \(u=\sin(tx)\) and \(v'=-xe^{-x^2}\), so \(v=\frac12e^{-x^2}\). Let \(R\to\infty\). The boundary term vanishes at both ends, and
\[\begin{aligned}I'(t)&=\Bigl[\tfrac12e^{-x^2}\sin(tx)\Bigr]_0^\infty-\frac t2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\cos(tx)\,dx\eqbr=-\frac t2I(t).\end{aligned}\]
Differentiate the product \(e^{t^2/4}I(t)\). The two contributions \(e^{t^2/4}\cdot(t/2)I\) and \(e^{t^2/4}\cdot I'\) cancel, so the product is constant. Volume~1 gives \(I(0)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2\), and therefore \(I(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2e^{-t^2/4}\).

The function \(e^{-x^2}\sin(tx)\) is odd and \(e^{-x^2}\cos(tx)\) is even, so
\[
\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}e^{itx}\,dx=2I(t)=\sqrt\pi\,e^{-t^2/4}.
\]
Substitute \(x=y/\sqrt2\), hence \(dx=dy/\sqrt2\). With \(s=t/\sqrt2\), so that \(t^2/4=s^2/2\),
\[
\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-y^2/2}e^{isy}\,dy=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-s^2/2}.
\]
Divide by \(\sqrt{2\pi}\). The standard Gaussian density \((2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/2}\) has characteristic function \(e^{-s^2/2}\). Volume~4 reached the same function by differentiating under the integral in the continuous setting.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rlemma}
\idea On a single interval the oscillation produces a factor \(1/|\xi|\). Step functions are dense in \(L^1\), and \(f\mapsto\int fe^{i\xi x}\) does not increase the \(L^1\) norm.
\writeup For \(f=\mathbf 1_{]a,b]}\),
\[
\Bigl|\int_a^be^{i\xi x}\,dx\Bigr|=\Bigl|\frac{e^{i\xi b}-e^{i\xi a}}{i\xi}\Bigr|\le\frac2{|\xi|},
\]
which tends to \(0\) as \(|\xi|\to\infty\). Finite linear combinations of such indicators, the step functions, obey the same limit.

Now take \(f\in\mathcal L^1(\R)\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). Splitting into real and imaginary parts, and then into positive and negative parts, reduces the claim to the case \(f\ge0\). Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(c) and monotone convergence give a simple function \(s=\sum_{i\le m}a_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\le f\) with \(\int(f-s)<\varepsilon\). Each \(A_i\) with \(a_i>0\) has finite measure. Exercise~\ref{ex:completion} puts inside \(A_i\) a Borel set of the same measure. The paragraph after Proposition~\ref{prop:regularity} approximates that Borel set, up to measure less than \(\varepsilon/(m|a_i|)\), by a finite union of intervals. The resulting step function \(\phi\) satisfies \(\int|s-\phi|<\varepsilon\), hence \(\int|f-\phi|<2\varepsilon\). Then
\[\Bigl|\int fe^{i\xi x}\Bigr|\le\int|f-\phi|+\Bigl|\int\phi e^{i\xi x}\Bigr|<2\varepsilon+\Bigl|\int\phi e^{i\xi x}\Bigr|.\]
The last term tends to \(0\) as \(|\xi|\to\infty\), so
\[\limsup_{|\xi|\to\infty}\Bigl|\int fe^{i\xi x}\Bigr|\le2\varepsilon\]
for every \(\varepsilon>0\). The limsup is \(0\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:dirichlet}
\idea For \(t>0\) the factor \(e^{-tx}\) makes the integral absolutely convergent, so Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter} computes \(F\) exactly. At \(t=0\) the function \(\sin x/x\) is not Lebesgue integrable. An integration by parts shows that the tails are small uniformly in \(t\), and that is what lets \(t\) reach \(0\).
\writeup (a) For \(t\ge t_0>0\),
\[
\Bigl|\partial_t\Bigl(e^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}x\Bigr)\Bigr|=|e^{-tx}\sin x|\le e^{-t_0x},
\]
which is integrable on \([0,\infty[\). Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}(b) gives differentiability on \(]t_0,\infty[\) for every \(t_0>0\). The derivative is \(F'(t)=-\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\sin x\,dx\).

The function \(\psi(x)=e^{-tx}(-t\sin x-\cos x)/(1+t^2)\) has derivative \(e^{-tx}\sin x\). Indeed, with \(u=e^{-tx}\) and \(v=(-t\sin x-\cos x)/(1+t^2)\),
\begin{align*}
u'v+uv'
&=\frac{e^{-tx}}{1+t^2}\Bigl(t(t\sin x+\cos x)+(-t\cos x+\sin x)\Bigr)\\
&=e^{-tx}\sin x.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\int_0^b e^{-tx}\sin x\,dx=\psi(b)-\psi(0)\). As \(b\to\infty\) the factor \(e^{-tb}\) sends \(\psi(b)\) to \(0\), and \(\psi(0)=-1/(1+t^2)\), so the improper integral equals \(1/(1+t^2)\). Therefore \(F'(t)=-1/(1+t^2)\).

Also \(|\sin x/x|\le1\) for \(x>0\), the integrand at \(0\) being read as \(1\), so \(|F(t)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\,dx=1/t\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\). The function \(\frac\pi2-\arctan t\) has the same derivative and the same limit \(0\) at infinity, so \(F(t)=\frac\pi2-\arctan t\).

(b) For \(t\ge0\) and \(0\le x\le R\), the integrand of \(G_R(t)=\int_0^Re^{-tx}(\sin x)/x\,dx\) is bounded by \(1\). Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}(a) makes \(G_R\) continuous on \([0,\infty[\). Use the same primitive \(\psi_t(x)=-e^{-tx}(t\sin x+\cos x)/(1+t^2)\) of \(e^{-tx}\sin x\). For \(t\ge0\),
\[
|\psi_t(x)|\le\frac{t+1}{1+t^2}.
\]
The quadratic \(2t^2-t+1=2\bigl(t-\frac14\bigr)^2+\frac78\) is positive, so \(t+1\le2(1+t^2)\) and \(|\psi_t|\le2\). Integrate by parts on \([R,S]\), differentiating \(1/x\):
\[\int_R^Se^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}x\,dx=\Bigl[\frac{\psi_t(x)}x\Bigr]_R^S+\int_R^S\frac{\psi_t(x)}{x^2}\,dx.\]
The absolute value is at most \(2/S+2/R+2\int_R^\infty x^{-2}\,dx\le2/S+2/R+2/R\le6/R\), for every \(S>R\) and every \(t\ge0\). The improper integrals \(\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}(\sin x)/x\,dx\) therefore exist for all \(t\ge0\). For \(t>0\) they equal \(F(t)\). Writing \(F(0)\) for the improper integral at \(t=0\), one has \(|F(t)-G_R(t)|\le6/R\) for every \(t\ge0\). A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so \(F\) is continuous at \(0\):
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x\,dx=F(0)=\lim_{t\to0^+}\Bigl(\frac\pi2-\arctan t\Bigr)=\frac\pi2.
\]
\end{solution}

\int_{1/n}^1x^{-1/2}\,dx=\bigl[2x^{1/2}\bigr]_{1/n}^1=2-2n^{-1/2}.
\]
The functions \(f\mathbf 1_{[1/n,1]}\) increase pointwise to \(f\) on \(]0,1]\). Monotone convergence yields \(\int_{]0,1]}f\,d\lambda=2\).

The same computation on \(]0,1/M^2]\) gives \(\int_{\{f>M\}}f=2/M\) for \(M>0\), because \(\{f>M\}=\,]0,1/M^2[\). Choose \(M=40\). Then \(2/M=\frac1{20}\). The split in Lemma~\ref{lem:abscont} reads
\[
\int_Af\le M\lambda(A)+\int_{\{f>M\}}f.
\]
If \(\lambda(A)<\frac1{800}\), the first term is strictly less than \(40\cdot\frac1{800}=\frac1{20}\). Adding the tail,
\[
\int_Af<\frac1{20}+\frac1{20}=\frac1{10}.
\]
The number \(\delta=\frac1{800}\) has the property asked for.
\end{solution}

\section*{Sources and further reading}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Sources and further reading}

\begin{itemize}
\item H.~Lebesgue, \emph{Le\c cons sur l'int\'egration et la recherche des fonctions primitives}, Gauthier-Villars, 1904, is where the integral is built and where the criterion for Riemann integrability is proved. The two merchants are his picture in the lecture ``Sur le d\'eveloppement de la notion d'int\'egrale'', \emph{Matematisk Tidsskrift B} (1926), 54--74, delivered in Copenhagen on 8~May 1926.
\item H.~Lebesgue, ``Sur la m\'ethode de M.~Goursat pour la r\'esolution de l'\'equation de Fredholm'', \emph{Bulletin de la Soci\'et\'e math\'ematique de France} 36 (1908), 3--19, passes a limit under the integral by cutting off a set whose integral is smaller than a chosen \(\varepsilon\).
\item G.~B.~Folland, \emph{Real Analysis}, 2nd ed., Wiley, 1999, Chapter~2, treats the integral, convergence in measure, and Egorov's theorem.
\item W.~Rudin, \emph{Real and Complex Analysis}, 3rd ed., McGraw--Hill, 1987, Chapter~1, gives the integral and the convergence theorems in a short form.
\item P.~Billingsley, \emph{Probability and Measure}, 3rd ed., Wiley, 1995, Sections~15--16, develops uniform integrability and Vitali's theorem on a probability space.
\item D.~Williams, \emph{Probability with Martingales}, Cambridge University Press, 1991, Chapters~5--6 and~13, arranges monotone convergence, Fatou's lemma, dominated convergence, and uniform integrability for later use with martingales.
\item G.~Vitali, \emph{Sull'integrazione per serie}, Rendiconti del Circolo Matematico di Palermo 23 (1907), 137--155, proves the convergence theorem under uniform absolute continuity of the integrals.
\item \emph{For the history named in the opening.} B.~Riemann, \emph{\"Uber die Darstellbarkeit einer Function durch eine trigonometrische Reihe}, Habilitationsschrift, G\"ottingen, 1854. B.~Levi, ``Sopra l'integrazione delle serie'', \emph{Rend.\ Ist.\ Lombardo} 39 (1906), 775--780. P.~Fatou, ``S\'eries trigonom\'etriques et s\'eries de Taylor'', \emph{Acta Math.} 30 (1906), 335--400. The full development is in T.~Hawkins, \emph{Lebesgue's Theory of Integration: Its Origins and Development}, 2nd ed., Chelsea, 1975.
\end{itemize}
