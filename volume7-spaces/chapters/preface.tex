\chapter*{Preface}
What is a number, a limit, a length? Every earlier volume used these words, and they worked. This volume says
what they are. It also pays what the earlier volumes owed: each of them left a few facts without proof, said so on
the spot, and named this volume as the one that proves them. The table below lists those facts and the theorems that prove them.

The real line comes first (Chapter~\ref{ch:sets}). It is built from sets: the natural numbers as the smallest set closed under
``add one'', the integers and the rationals as pairs, and the reals as limits of rational Cauchy sequences. The least upper
bound property, an axiom in Volume~1, becomes a theorem, and the reals turn out to be the only complete ordered field. Cantor's diagonal
argument then shows that there are more reals than rationals, so that infinity comes in sizes.

Limits come next, freed from coordinates. A distance is enough to speak of convergence, completeness and fixed
points (Chapter~\ref{ch:metric}), and one theorem, Banach's, solves equations, draws fractals and ranks web pages. The same chapter completes the
rationals a second time, for a different distance, and finds the $p$-adic numbers. Forgetting even the
distance leaves a topology (Chapter~\ref{ch:topology}), where continuity and connectedness still make sense. Compactness
(Chapter~\ref{ch:compact}) lets an infinite set be handled through finitely many pieces; Tychonoff's theorem, the Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem and the
Stone--Weierstrass theorem are its main results. Baire's theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:baire}) brings a surprise: in a complete space the typical object
is strange, and most continuous functions have a derivative nowhere. The first part ends with the Jordan curve theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:jordan}):
a closed curve that does not cross itself has an inside and an outside. The proof goes through polygons first, then uses the winding number of a
continuous curve and the fixed-point theorem on a disc.

The second part measures. Lebesgue's idea is to give a length to every reasonable set (Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}). It cannot be
done for all sets: a set without a length exists, and a measurable set can meet every interval in positive length while its complement does too.
From lengths comes the integral (Chapter~\ref{ch:integral}), built by slicing the values of the function and not the axis. Its convergence theorems
say when a limit may pass under the integral sign, and their hypotheses are easy to check. Volumes are
computed by slices and change by the determinant under a linear map (Chapter~\ref{ch:products}); the volume of the unit ball in dimension $d$
comes out in closed form and tends to zero as $d$ grows. Functions become points of the spaces $L^p$, which are complete (Chapter~\ref{ch:lp}).
A density is the derivative of one measure with respect to another, and conditional expectation is a theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:radon}). Measures on metric spaces
carry weak convergence and the dimension of fractals (Chapter~\ref{ch:metricmeasures}): the Cantor set on the cover has dimension $\frac{\log2}{\log3}$, strictly between zero and one.
The last chapter is a set of problems on one theme, what almost every real number looks like, and its centre is Khinchin's theorem.

None of these proofs uses the result that borrowed the fact, and none uses a later volume. Volume~4 is a source of examples here and not a premise:
no proof in this volume uses a theorem of probability. The other direction is different. The strong law of large numbers for coin tosses, the existence of infinitely many
independent coins and conditional expectation are proved here from measure theory, and Volumes~11 and~12 build on them.

The volume assumes the calculus of Volume~1; Volume~2 supplies the continued fractions of the last chapter, and Volumes~5 and~6 are cited in a few
problems there. Chapters~\ref{ch:sets} to~\ref{ch:jordan} are about sets, distances and topology, Chapters~\ref{ch:measures} to~\ref{ch:metricmeasures} about measure and
integration, and each part can be read for its own sake.

Every theorem, proposition and lemma is proved. A result quoted only for its interest is marked \emph{quoted}, with a
reference, and nothing proved here depends on it. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a good
afternoon and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} for the hardest, and every one has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its
chapter, beginning with \emph{How to find it} before the \emph{Write-up}. ``Pause and think'' boxes ask you to guess
before you read on.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random yet obey laws. This floor is the rooms in which both
are measured: a limit, a length, Lebesgue's integral, and the meaning of ``almost every''. The next floor, \emph{Analysis}, puts functions in those rooms and
takes them apart into frequencies.
