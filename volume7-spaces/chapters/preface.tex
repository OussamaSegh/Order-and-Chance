\chapter*{Preface}
A number, a limit, a length. The earlier volumes used the three words, and the arguments went through. This volume gives the definitions. It also pays the debts those volumes left in the open: each time a fact was used without proof, the text said so and named this volume. The table records the debt and the theorem that clears it.

The real line is built first (Chapter~\ref{ch:sets}). Sets are the only material. The natural numbers are the smallest set that contains $0$ and is closed under ``add one''. The integers and the rationals are equivalence classes of pairs. The reals are limits of rational Cauchy sequences. In Volume~1 the least upper bound property is an axiom. Here it is a theorem, and a second theorem says that any two complete ordered fields are the same. Cantor's diagonal argument then shows there are more reals than rationals. Infinity has more than one size.

A limit does not need coordinates. A distance is enough for convergence, for completeness, and for fixed points (Chapter~\ref{ch:metric}). Banach's theorem is the one fixed-point result, and it solves equations, draws fractals, and ranks pages on the web. The same chapter runs the completion a second time, with a different distance on $\Q$, and the $p$-adic numbers appear. Drop the distance as well and a topology remains (Chapter~\ref{ch:topology}): continuity and connectedness still make sense. Compactness (Chapter~\ref{ch:compact}) lets you handle an infinite set by finitely many pieces. Tychonoff's theorem, the Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem, and the Stone--Weierstrass theorem are the three results that get used later. Baire's theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:baire}) is the surprise of the first part: in a complete space the typical point is strange, and a typical continuous function has a derivative at no point. The part closes with the Jordan curve theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:jordan}). A closed curve that does not cross itself has an inside and an outside. Polygons come first. The winding number of a continuous curve, and the fixed-point theorem on a disc, finish the argument.

The second part gives sets a length. Lebesgue's idea is to assign a length to every reasonable set (Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}). The assignment cannot cover every set: there is a set with no length, and there is a measurable set that meets every interval in positive length, as does its complement. The integral is built from that length (Chapter~\ref{ch:integral}), by slicing the values of the function rather than the axis. The convergence theorems say when a limit may pass under the integral sign, and the hypotheses are the ones you can check. Volumes are computed by slices, and a linear map multiplies volume by the determinant (Chapter~\ref{ch:products}). The volume of the unit ball in dimension $d$ has a closed form, and it tends to $0$ as $d$ grows. Functions are then points of the spaces $L^p$, and those spaces are complete (Chapter~\ref{ch:lp}). A density is the derivative of one measure with respect to another, and conditional expectation is a theorem (Chapter~\ref{ch:radon}). Measures on metric spaces carry weak convergence and the dimension of a fractal (Chapter~\ref{ch:metricmeasures}). The Cantor set drawn on the cover has dimension $\frac{\log2}{\log3}$, strictly between $0$ and $1$. The last chapter is a set of problems with one subject, the look of almost every real number, and the centre of that chapter is Khinchin's theorem.

None of these proofs calls on the result that borrowed the fact, and none calls on a later volume. Volume~4 supplies examples and is not a premise: no proof in this volume uses a theorem of probability. The other direction runs the other way. The strong law for coin tosses, the existence of infinitely many independent coins, and conditional expectation are proved here from measure, and Volumes~11 and~12 build on them.

The volume assumes the calculus of Volume~1. Volume~2 supplies the continued fractions used in the last chapter, and Volumes~5 and~6 are cited in a few problems there. Chapters~\ref{ch:sets} to~\ref{ch:jordan} are sets, distances, and topology. Chapters~\ref{ch:measures} to~\ref{ch:metricmeasures} are measure and integration. Either part can be read on its own.

Every theorem, proposition, and lemma is proved. A result quoted only for its interest is marked \emph{quoted}, with a reference, and nothing proved here depends on it. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a good afternoon, and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} for the hardest. Each one has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its chapter. The solution begins with \emph{How to find it} and then gives the \emph{Write-up}. A ``Pause and think'' box asks you to guess before you read on.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random while they obey laws. This volume is the set of rooms in which both are measured: a limit, a length, Lebesgue's integral, and the meaning of ``almost every''. The next volume, \emph{Analysis}, puts functions in those rooms and takes them apart into frequencies.
