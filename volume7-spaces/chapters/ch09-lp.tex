\chapter{\texorpdfstring{$L^p$}{Lp} Spaces}\label{ch:lp}

\begin{center}\itshape
Put two functions side by side and ask how far apart they are. The largest gap, the average gap, and the square root of the average squared gap are three different answers, and each answer builds its own geometry.
\end{center}

On $[0,1]$, let $f_n=n\,\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}$. The area under $f_n$ is $1$, so the average size stays fixed. The largest value is $n$. The $L^2$ norm is $\bigl(\int_0^{1/n}n^2\,dx\bigr)^{1/2}=\sqrt n$. One spike, three sizes, and only the first refuses to grow. The spaces $L^p$ are the family that holds these sizes together, one space for each exponent $1\le p\le\infty$.

The two-number inequality underneath the family is older than the spaces. Rogers published it for finite sums in the \emph{Messenger of Mathematics} in 1888. Hölder's paper in the Göttingen \emph{Nachrichten} of 1889 gave a shorter proof, and the inequality took his name. Jensen's inequality for convex functions is the 1906 paper in \emph{Acta Mathematica}. Jensen worked as an engineer at the Copenhagen Telephone Company and held no university post; the biography in the MacTutor archive records that career. In one volume of the \emph{Comptes rendus} for 1907, Riesz and Fischer treated square-integrable functions separately: Fischer proved convergence in the mean, and Riesz proved the matching statement for orthonormal series. Riesz's 1910 paper in \emph{Mathematische Annalen} then built the spaces for a general exponent. The argument below follows that later pattern, and it uses only the integral, the measure, and the metric facts already proved.

Volume~1, Theorem~9.5, is Young's inequality for two numbers, and Volume~1, Theorem~9.6, is Hölder for finite sums. Here the lists become functions and the sums become integrals. The pattern of the proof does not change. Minkowski's inequality is the triangle inequality for the resulting size. The main theorem, Riesz--Fischer, says that a Cauchy sequence of functions has a limit in the same space. Riemann's integral cannot offer that: Exercise~\ref{ex:L1incomplete} builds a Cauchy sequence of continuous functions with no continuous limit for the distance $\int|f-g|$. After completeness come three approximation theorems, by simple functions, by continuous functions of compact support, and by smooth ones. The smoothing is convolution against a small bump.

\section{Young, H\"older, Minkowski}

Two exponents $p,q>1$ are \emph{conjugate} when $\frac1p+\frac1q=1$. Solving for $q$ gives $q=\frac p{p-1}$. You should keep four pairs in reach: $2$ with $2$, $3$ with $\frac32$, $4$ with $\frac43$, and, by the same algebra with the obvious limits, $1$ with $\infty$.

\begin{lemma}[Young's inequality]\label{lem:young}
Let $1<p<\infty$ and let $q$ be conjugate to $p$. For all $a,b\ge0$,
\[ab\le\frac{a^p}p+\frac{b^q}q,\]
with equality if and only if $a^p=b^q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Check the axes first. If $a=0$, the claim is $0\le b^q/q$, and both sides agree precisely when $b=0$, which is $a^p=b^q$. The case $b=0$ is the same with the letters swapped.

Now take $a>0$ and define $\varphi(b)=a^p/p+b^q/q-ab$ for $b\ge0$. Then $\varphi'(b)=b^{q-1}-a$ and $\varphi''(b)=(q-1)b^{q-2}$ for $b>0$. The second derivative is positive, since $q>1$. On any compact subinterval of $]0,\infty[$, Theorem~\ref{thm:mvt}(c) applied to $\varphi'$ gives $\varphi'(b_2)-\varphi'(b_1)=\varphi''(c)(b_2-b_1)>0$. So $\varphi'$ is strictly increasing on $]0,\infty[$. It has a single zero, where $b^{q-1}=a$. Because $q-1=1/(p-1)$, that zero is $b=a^{p-1}$. Thus $\varphi'$ is negative before $a^{p-1}$ and positive after, and $\varphi$ has a strict minimum there.

The minimum value is zero. Indeed $(p-1)q=p$, so
\[\varphi(a^{p-1})=\frac{a^p}p+\frac{a^{(p-1)q}}q-a\cdot a^{p-1}=a^p\Bigl(\frac1p+\frac1q-1\Bigr)=0.\]
Therefore $\varphi(b)\ge0$, with equality only at $b=a^{p-1}$. Since $(p-1)q=p$, raising $b=a^{p-1}$ to the power $q$ gives $b^q=a^p$.
\end{proof}

Try $p=q=2$, $a=b=2$: both sides equal $4$. Move $b$ to $4$ and the right side becomes $2+8=10$, while the product is $8$. Equality really does need $a^2=b^2$.

Throughout this chapter, $(E,\mathcal A,\mu)$ is a measure space. For a measurable $f$, real or complex, and for $1\le p<\infty$,
\[\|f\|_p=\Bigl(\int|f|^p\,d\mu\Bigr)^{1/p}\in[0,+\infty].\]
The absolute value means that a complex function is estimated by the same integrals as $|f|$.

\begin{theorem}[H\"older's inequality]\label{thm:holder}
Let $1<p<\infty$ and let $q$ be conjugate to $p$. For any measurable $f$ and $g$,
\[\|fg\|_1\le\|f\|_p\,\|g\|_q.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\|f\|_p=0$, then $f=0$ almost everywhere, so $fg=0$ almost everywhere and the left side vanishes. The product of the norms is read as $0$ as well, including the case $\|g\|_q=\infty$, by the convention $0\cdot\infty=0$ already fixed for the integral. The symmetric case $\|g\|_q=0$ is identical.

If one norm is infinite and the other is positive, the right side is infinite and there is nothing to prove. The remaining case is $0<\|f\|_p,\|g\|_q<\infty$. Divide $f$ and $g$ by their norms, which does not change the inequality we want, and assume from here that both norms equal $1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:young} at the numbers $|f(x)|$ and $|g(x)|$ gives
\[|f||g|\le\frac{|f|^p}p+\frac{|g|^q}q.\]
Integrate. The right side becomes $\frac1p+\frac1q=1$, and the left side is $\|fg\|_1$.
\end{proof}

The case $p=q=2$ is the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for integrals: $\int|fg|\,d\mu\le\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$. Replacing $g$ by a function with the same modulus does not change either side, so
\[\Bigl|\int f\bar g\,d\mu\Bigr|\le\|f\|_2\|g\|_2.\]
Counting measure on a finite set turns Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} back into Volume~1, Theorem~9.6. The same counting measure with $p=2$ is Volume~1, Theorem~9.4.

On $[0,1]$, take $f=1$ and $g(x)=x$. Then $\int fg=\frac12$, while $\|f\|_2\|g\|_2=1/\sqrt3$. Squaring both numbers gives $\frac14<\frac13$, so the inequality is strict, and you can see the gap without a calculator.

\begin{theorem}[Minkowski's inequality]\label{thm:minkowski}
For $1\le p<\infty$ and measurable $f,g$,
\[\|f+g\|_p\le\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If either norm on the right is infinite, the inequality holds because the right side is infinite. Assume both are finite.

For $p=1$, integrate the pointwise bound $|f+g|\le|f|+|g|$.

For $p>1$, first check that $f+g$ has a finite norm. The map $t\mapsto t^p$ on $[0,\infty)$ has second derivative $p(p-1)t^{p-2}\ge0$ for $t>0$, so it is convex. The chord form at $|f|$ and $|g|$ reads
\[\Bigl(\frac{|f|+|g|}2\Bigr)^p\le\frac{|f|^p+|g|^p}2.\]
Multiply by $2^p$ and use $|f+g|\le|f|+|g|$:
\[|f+g|^p\le 2^{p-1}\bigl(|f|^p+|g|^p\bigr).\]
Integrate. The right side is finite, so $\|f+g\|_p<\infty$. The factor $2^{p-1}$ is only this preliminary bound. The triangle inequality itself will have constant $1$.

Now write $|f+g|^p=|f+g|\,|f+g|^{p-1}\le|f|\,|f+g|^{p-1}+|g|\,|f+g|^{p-1}$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} to each product. The conjugate exponent satisfies $(p-1)q=p$, so
\[\bigl\||f+g|^{p-1}\bigr\|_q=\Bigl(\int|f+g|^{(p-1)q}\,d\mu\Bigr)^{1/q}=\|f+g\|_p^{p/q}=\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}.\]
Adding the two estimates,
\[\|f+g\|_p^p\le\bigl(\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p\bigr)\,\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}.\]
If $\|f+g\|_p=0$ we are done. If not, divide by $\|f+g\|_p^{p-1}$.
\end{proof}

The constant is sharp for a trivial reason: $\|2f\|_p=2\|f\|_p$. It is not always an equality for unrelated functions. Let $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}$ and $g=\mathbf 1_{[1,2]}$ on $\R$. Then $|f+g|$ is the indicator of a set of measure $2$, so $\|f+g\|_p=2^{1/p}$, while $\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p=2$. For every $p>1$ one has $2^{1/p}<2$.

\section{The spaces \texorpdfstring{$L^p$}{Lp}}

Let $\mathcal L^p$ be the set of measurable functions, real or complex, with $\|f\|_p<\infty$. Theorem~\ref{thm:minkowski} says that $\mathcal L^p$ is a vector space and that $\|\cdot\|_p$ is a seminorm on it. The seminorm vanishes exactly when $f=0$ almost everywhere. On $[0,1]$, the indicator of the rationals is a concrete example: it is not the zero function, and every integral of a power of it is zero. The quotient by that kernel repairs the seminorm. Set
\[L^p(\mu)=\mathcal L^p\big/\bigl\{f:f=0\text{ almost everywhere}\bigr\}.\]
Two functions determine the same point of $L^p$ when they agree off a null set. Integration cannot separate them, so the usual shorthand is harmless: one says ``$f\in L^p$'' and means the class of $f$.

For $p=\infty$ the size is the \emph{essential supremum}
\[\|f\|_\infty=\inf\{M\ge0:|f|\le M\text{ almost everywhere}\}.\]
The infimum is attained. The set $\{|f|>\|f\|_\infty\}$ equals $\bigcup_n\{|f|>\|f\|_\infty+1/n\}$, a countable union of null sets, so $|f|\le\|f\|_\infty$ almost everywhere. Then $L^\infty(\mu)$ is the space of classes with $\|f\|_\infty<\infty$. It is a normed space, because $|f+g|\le\|f\|_\infty+\|g\|_\infty$ almost everywhere. Hölder's inequality at the endpoint is immediate from the same bound:
\[\|fg\|_1\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_\infty,\]
and likewise with $f$ and $g$ exchanged. Counting measure on $\N$ specialises every one of these spaces to the sequence space $\ell^p$.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz--Fischer]\label{thm:rieszfischer}
Every space $L^p(\mu)$ with $1\le p\le\infty$ is a Banach space. If $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$ for some finite $p$, a subsequence converges to $f$ almost everywhere.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $p<\infty$ first, and let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy in $L^p$.

\emph{A fast subsequence.} For each $k\ge1$ the Cauchy property supplies an index $N_k$ such that $m,n\ge N_k$ implies $\|f_m-f_n\|_p\le2^{-k}$. Choose the indices inductively so that $n_k\ge N_k$ and $n_{k+1}>n_k$. Then $\|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}\|_p\le2^{-k}$.

\emph{A pointwise limit.} Define
\[G_N=|f_{n_1}|+\sum_{k=1}^N|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}|,\qquad G=\lim_{N\to\infty}G_N.\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:minkowski} and the geometric sum give $\|G_N\|_p\le\|f_{n_1}\|_p+\sum_{k=1}^N2^{-k}\le\|f_{n_1}\|_p+1$. The integrands $G_N^p$ increase to $G^p$, so monotone convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:mct6}) yields $\|G\|_p\le\|f_{n_1}\|_p+1$. A function of finite $L^p$ norm is finite almost everywhere, hence $G<\infty$ almost everywhere. Off that null set the telescoping series $f_{n_1}+\sum_k(f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k})$ converges absolutely, and its partial sums are the functions $f_{n_N}$. Define $f$ to be this pointwise limit where $G<\infty$, and define $f=0$ on the null set. Then $f$ is measurable, $|f|\le G$, and the remainder of the series gives
\[|f-f_{n_N}|\le\sum_{k\ge N}|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}|\le G.\]

\emph{The norm limit.} The bound $|f-f_{n_N}|^p\le G^p$ is integrable, and the left side tends to $0$ almost everywhere. Dominated convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6}) gives $\|f-f_{n_N}\|_p\to0$.

\emph{The whole sequence.} Chapter~\ref{ch:metric} records that a Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit. Thus $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$. In particular $f\in L^p$, and the class of $f$ is the limit in the quotient.

Now suppose $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$, with $p<\infty$. Then $g_n=f_n-f$ tends to $0$ in $L^p$, so $(g_n)$ is Cauchy. The construction just given produces a subsequence $g_{n_j}$ and a limit $g\in L^p$ with $g_{n_j}\to g$ almost everywhere and $\|g_{n_j}-g\|_p\to0$. But $\|g_{n_j}\|_p\to0$, so $\|g\|_p=0$ and $g=0$ almost everywhere. Therefore $f_{n_j}\to f$ almost everywhere.

\emph{The case $p=\infty$.} A Cauchy sequence is bounded: past some index, every term lies within distance $1$ of a fixed term, and a finite set of norms is bounded. Let
\[N=\bigcup_{m,n}\bigl\{|f_m-f_n|>\|f_m-f_n\|_\infty\bigr\}.\]
Each set in the union is null, so $N$ is null. Off $N$ one has $|f_m-f_n|\le\|f_m-f_n\|_\infty$, and the right side is small for all large $m$ and $n$, independently of the point. The sequence is uniformly Cauchy on the complement of $N$, so it converges uniformly there to a bounded function. Call the limit $f$ off $N$, and set $f=0$ on $N$. Then $f$ equals the pointwise limit of the measurable functions $f_n\mathbf 1_{E\setminus N}$, so $f$ is measurable and essentially bounded. For every large $n$, $|f_n-f|\le\varepsilon$ off $N$, hence $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
Can a sequence tend to $0$ in $L^2([0,1])$ and still diverge at every point? Can it tend to $0$ at every point and keep a fixed positive $L^2$ norm?
\end{think}

Both happen, and the examples are small enough to compute. The typewriter sequence of Exercise~\ref{ex:typewriter} runs through the dyadic intervals of $[0,1]$: if $n=2^k+j$ with $0\le j<2^k$, the function $f_n$ is the indicator of $[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k}]$. Its $L^1$ norm is $2^{-k}$. Since $f_n$ is an indicator, $f_n^2=f_n$, so the square of the $L^2$ norm equals the $L^1$ norm and the $L^2$ norm itself is $2^{-k/2}$. Both tend to $0$. Every $x\in[0,1]$ lies in infinitely many of these intervals and in infinitely many of their complements, so $(f_n(x))$ does not converge. The other direction is the spike $\sqrt n\,\mathbf 1_{]0,1/n[}$. At each fixed $x>0$ the spike is eventually $0$, and the value at $0$ is $0$, so the sequence tends to $0$ everywhere. Its $L^2$ norm equals $1$ for every $n$, while its $L^1$ norm equals $n^{-1/2}$ and tends to $0$. Convergence in $L^p$ and convergence almost everywhere are therefore not comparable in general. The subsequence in Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszfischer} cannot be omitted. For $p=1$, on a finite measure space, Vitali's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:vitali7}) names the extra condition that turns convergence in measure into convergence of the integrals.

\emph{Inclusions.} Suppose $\mu(E)<\infty$ and $1\le p<q<\infty$. Hölder's inequality with the conjugate pair $q/p$ and $q/(q-p)$, applied to the product $|f|^p\cdot 1$, gives
\[\int|f|^p\,d\mu\le\Bigl(\int|f|^q\,d\mu\Bigr)^{p/q}\mu(E)^{(q-p)/q}.\]
Take $p$th roots:
\[\|f\|_p\le\mu(E)^{\frac1p-\frac1q}\|f\|_q.\]
The endpoint $q=\infty$ is the estimate $\int|f|^p\le\|f\|_\infty^p\,\mu(E)$, which is the same formula with the convention $1/\infty=0$. On a finite measure space, a larger exponent gives a smaller space: $L^q\subset L^p$.

For sequences the inclusion runs the other way. Let $1\le p<q\le\infty$ and let $a\in\ell^p$ with $a\ne0$. The normalised sequence $b=a/\|a\|_p$ satisfies $\|b\|_p=1$, so each coordinate obeys $|b_n|\le1$. If $q<\infty$, then $|b_n|^q\le|b_n|^p$, and summing yields $\|b\|_q\le1$. If $q=\infty$, the same bound $|b_n|\le1$ is $\|b\|_\infty\le1$. In both cases $\|a\|_q\le\|a\|_p$, and therefore $\ell^p\subset\ell^q$.

On $\R$ neither inclusion survives. A large exponent forbids spikes. A small exponent forbids a tail that decays too slowly.

\begin{think}
For which $p$ does $x\mapsto x^{-1/2}$ lie in $L^p(]0,1[)$? In $L^p(]1,\infty[)$? In $L^p(]0,\infty[)$?
\end{think}

Compute the two improper integrals separately. The antiderivative of $x^{-p/2}$ is a power when $p\ne2$, and a logarithm when $p=2$. On $]0,1]$ the integral $\int_0^1 x^{-p/2}\,dx$ converges if and only if $p/2<1$, that is $p<2$. In particular $\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx=[2x^{1/2}]_0^1=2$, so $x^{-1/2}$ belongs to $L^1(]0,1])$, while $\int_0^1 x^{-1}\,dx$ diverges, so it does not belong to $L^2(]0,1])$. On $[1,\infty)$ the integral $\int_1^\infty x^{-p/2}\,dx$ converges if and only if $p>2$. The function is bounded by $1$ there, so it also belongs to $L^\infty(]1,\infty[)$. At $p=2$ both ends produce a logarithm, and a logarithm tends to infinity at $0$ and at $\infty$. Thus $x^{-1/2}$ lies in no space $L^p(]0,\infty[)$.

The reverse inclusion needs the other end of the line. The function $x^{-1}$ on $[1,\infty)$ has $\int_1^\infty x^{-1}\,dx=\infty$ and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx=1$, so it lies in $L^2$ and not in $L^1$. Figure~\ref{fig:rwsplit} draws both failures.

\begin{theorem}[Jensen's inequality]\label{thm:jensen}
Let $\mu$ be a probability measure, let $I$ be an open interval, and let $\varphi:I\to\R$ be convex. If $f\in L^1(\mu)$ takes its values in $I$, then $\int f\,d\mu$ lies in $I$, the negative part of $\varphi\circ f$ is integrable, and
\[\varphi\Bigl(\int f\,d\mu\Bigr)\le\int\varphi\circ f\,d\mu.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $m=\int f\,d\mu$.

\emph{The mean stays in the interval.} Suppose $I$ has a finite left endpoint $a$. Then $g=f-a>0$ at every point, so $\{g>0\}=E$. The set $\{g>0\}$ is the union of the sets $\{g\ge1/n\}$. If each of those were null, then $\{g>0\}$ would be null, and that set is $E$, contradicting $\mu(E)=1$. Some set $\{g\ge1/n\}$ therefore has positive measure, and $\int g\ge n^{-1}\mu(g\ge1/n)>0$. Hence $m>a$. A finite right endpoint is the same argument applied to $-f$. If an endpoint is infinite there is nothing to prove on that side. In every case $m\in I$.

\emph{A line under the graph.} Convexity is the chord inequality. For $s<m<t$ in $I$,
\[m=\frac{t-m}{t-s}\,s+\frac{m-s}{t-s}\,t,\]
so $\varphi(m)\le\bigl((t-m)\varphi(s)+(m-s)\varphi(t)\bigr)/(t-s)$. Rearrangement gives $(t-m)(\varphi(m)-\varphi(s))\le(m-s)(\varphi(t)-\varphi(m))$. Divide by the positive number $(t-m)(m-s)$:
\[\frac{\varphi(m)-\varphi(s)}{m-s}\le\frac{\varphi(t)-\varphi(m)}{t-m}.\]
Let $c$ be the supremum of the left-hand side over all $s\in I$ with $s<m$. The set of such $s$ is nonempty because $I$ is open and $m\in I$. Any single right-hand slope is an upper bound, so $c$ is finite. The comparison then says that every right-hand slope is at least $c$, and the definition of $c$ says that every left-hand slope is at most $c$. Both rearrange into $\varphi(t)\ge\varphi(m)+c(t-m)$. The affine function $\ell(t)=\varphi(m)+c(t-m)$ lies under $\varphi$ on $I$.

\emph{Continuity, then the integral.} On a compact subinterval $[\alpha,\beta]\subset I$, pick $\alpha'<\alpha$ and $\beta'>\beta$ still in $I$. Slopes between points of $[\alpha,\beta]$ are trapped between the slope from $\alpha'$ to $\alpha$ and the slope from $\beta$ to $\beta'$, again by the three-slope comparison. Those slopes are bounded by some constant $M$, so $|\varphi(t)-\varphi(s)|\le M|t-s|$ on $[\alpha,\beta]$. A convex function on an open interval is therefore continuous, and $\varphi\circ f$ is measurable because a continuous function of a measurable function is measurable (Chapter~\ref{ch:integral}). Since $\varphi\circ f\ge\ell\circ f$ and $\ell\circ f$ is integrable, the negative part of $\varphi\circ f$ is at most the negative part of an integrable function, hence integrable. The integral $\int\varphi\circ f$ is well defined in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, and
\[\int\varphi\circ f\,d\mu\ge\int\ell\circ f\,d\mu=\varphi(m)+c\int(f-m)\,d\mu=\varphi(m).\]
\end{proof}

Here is a case where the hypotheses are easy to see. On the probability space $[0,1]$, the map $\varphi(t)=t^2$ is convex on the open interval $\R$, and $f(x)=x$ takes values in $\R$. Jensen says $\bigl(\int_0^1 x\,dx\bigr)^2\le\int_0^1 x^2\,dx$, that is $\frac14\le\frac13$.

On a probability space, $\|f\|_p\le\|f\|_q$ for $p<q$ is the case $\mu(E)=1$ of the Hölder inclusion above. Jensen recovers the same bound by a second route. The map $t\mapsto t^{q/p}$ is convex on $[0,\infty)$, while Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen} asks for an open interval, and $|f|^p$ may vanish. The theorem also asks for an integrable input, so an infinite $\|f\|_p$ cannot be fed back into the inequality. Truncate. For $n\ge1$ and $0<\varepsilon\le1$, the function $\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon$ is bounded, hence integrable on a probability space, and its values lie in $]0,\infty[$. If $f\in L^q$, then $\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon\le|f|^p+\varepsilon$. For $u,v\ge0$ one has $(u+v)^{q/p}\le2^{q/p}(u^{q/p}+v^{q/p})$, and $\varepsilon\le1$ forces $\varepsilon^{q/p}\le1$, so
\[
\bigl(\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon\bigr)^{q/p}\le2^{q/p}(|f|^q+1).
\]
The right side is integrable. Jensen applies:
\[
\Bigl(\int\bigl(\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon\bigr)\,d\mu\Bigr)^{q/p}\le\int\bigl(\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon\bigr)^{q/p}\,d\mu\le2^{q/p}\int(|f|^q+1)\,d\mu.
\]
The right side does not depend on $n$, so the integrals of $\min(|f|^p,n)+\varepsilon$ stay bounded as $n$ grows. Monotone convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:mct6}) gives $\int|f|^p<\infty$, and therefore $\|f\|_p<\infty$. Now $|f|^p+\varepsilon$ itself is an allowed input. The same comparison, without the truncation, gives
\[
\Bigl(\int(|f|^p+\varepsilon)\,d\mu\Bigr)^{q/p}\le\int(|f|^p+\varepsilon)^{q/p}\,d\mu.
\]
Along $\varepsilon=1/m$ the left side equals $\bigl(\|f\|_p^p+\varepsilon\bigr)^{q/p}$, which tends to $\|f\|_p^q$. On the right, the integrands decrease to the measurable function $|f|^q$ and are dominated by the integrable function $2^{q/p}(|f|^q+1)$. Dominated convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6}) sends the right side to $\|f\|_q^q$. Exercise~\ref{ex:jensen} specialises the theorem to the exponential, to $t^2$, and to the logarithm. In the language of probability the inequality is $\varphi(\mathbb E[X])\le\mathbb E[\varphi(X)]$. Volume~11 extends the same comparison to conditional expectations; that extension is not used here.

\section{Approximation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:simpledense}
For $1\le p\le\infty$, the simple functions that lie in $L^p$ are dense in $L^p$. For $p<\infty$ those simple functions are exactly the finite sums $\sum_ia_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}$ with $\mu(A_i)<\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $f\ge0$ belong to $L^p$, and let $s_n=\min\bigl(n,2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n f\rfloor\bigr)$ be the simple functions of Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}. They satisfy $0\le s_n\uparrow f$.

If $p<\infty$, then $|f-s_n|^p\le f^p$, and $f^p$ is integrable. Dominated convergence gives $\|f-s_n\|_p\to0$. Also $s_n^p\le f^p$. For each height $a>0$ one has $a^p\mu(s_n\ge a)\le\int s_n^p\,d\mu<\infty$, so every level set that carries a positive value has finite measure.

If $p=\infty$, change $f$ on a null set so that $0\le f\le\|f\|_\infty$. The same simple functions then converge uniformly, by Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(c). A general real function is the difference of its positive and negative parts. A complex function is a pair of real functions.
\end{proof}

On $\R^d$, a \emph{step function} is a finite linear combination of indicators of boxes. The symbol $C_c(\R^d)$ denotes the continuous functions of compact support.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ccdense}
For $1\le p<\infty$, the step functions are dense in $L^p(\R^d)$, and so are the functions in $C_c(\R^d)$. Neither class is dense in $L^\infty(\R^d)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:simpledense} it is enough to approximate $\mathbf 1_A$ for a Borel set $A$ with $\lambda_d(A)<\infty$. Proposition~\ref{prop:regularity} supplies a compact set $K$ and an open set $U$ with $K\subset A\subset U$ and $\lambda_d(U\setminus K)<\varepsilon$. Intersecting $U$ with a large open ball keeps $U$ bounded and costs nothing off $A$.

\emph{Continuous functions.} The quotient
\[g(x)=\frac{d(x,U^c)}{d(x,U^c)+d(x,K)}\]
is the Urysohn function of Proposition~\ref{prop:urysohn}. It is continuous, it equals $1$ on $K$, it equals $0$ off $U$, and its values lie in $[0,1]$. Its support sits inside $\overline U$, which is compact. The difference $|\mathbf 1_A-g|$ is at most $\mathbf 1_{U\setminus K}$, so $\|\mathbf 1_A-g\|_p^p\le\varepsilon$.

\emph{Step functions.} Step~2 in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cov} writes a bounded open set as a countable union of closed dyadic cubes $Q_k$ with disjoint interiors. The boundaries are null, and $\sum_k\lambda_d(Q_k)=\lambda_d(U)<\infty$. Choose $N$ so that the tail $\sum_{k>N}\lambda_d(Q_k)<\varepsilon$, and let $h$ be the indicator of $Q_1\cup\cdots\cup Q_N$. Up to a null set, $h$ is a step function, and
\[|\mathbf 1_A-h|\le\mathbf 1_{U\setminus K}+\mathbf 1_{\bigcup_{k>N}Q_k}.\]
Therefore $\|\mathbf 1_A-h\|_p^p\le2\varepsilon$.

\emph{The endpoint $p=\infty$.} Let $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}$ on $\R$. Suppose $g$ is continuous and $\|f-g\|_\infty=c<\frac12$. Then $g\ge1-c$ almost everywhere on $]0,1[$. The set where this holds is dense, and $g$ is continuous, so $g\ge1-c$ everywhere on $[0,1]$, and in particular $g(0)\ge1-c$. The same reasoning on $]-1,0[$ gives $g\le c$ there, so $g(0)\le c$. But $c<1-c$, and one point cannot satisfy both bounds. No continuous function is strictly closer than $\frac12$ to $f$. The constant function $\frac12$ meets the distance $\frac12$, so that number is sharp for the class of all continuous functions.

Compact support makes the distance larger. A function in $C_c(\R)$, and likewise a step function, vanishes outside some bounded interval. Far to the right, $f$ equals $1$ and the approximant equals $0$, so the essential supremum of the difference is at least $1$. The zero function is both a step function and an element of $C_c(\R)$, and $\|f\|_\infty=1$, so the distance from $f$ to each of those two classes is exactly $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[continuity of translation]\label{prop:translation}
Let $f\in L^p(\R^d)$ with $1\le p<\infty$, and write $\tau_hf(x)=f(x-h)$. Then $\|\tau_hf-f\|_p\to0$ as $h\to0$. The same statement is false in $L^\infty$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
First take $g\in C_c(\R^d)$, supported in a ball $B$. Uniform continuity gives $\sup|\tau_hg-g|\to0$ as $h\to0$. For $|h|\le1$ the difference $\tau_hg-g$ is supported in the $1$-neighbourhood $B'$ of $B$, a set of finite measure. Hence
\[\|\tau_hg-g\|_p\le\lambda_d(B')^{1/p}\sup|\tau_hg-g|\to0.\]
For a general $f\in L^p$ and a given $\varepsilon>0$, Theorem~\ref{thm:ccdense} supplies $g\in C_c$ with $\|f-g\|_p<\varepsilon$. Translation does not change Lebesgue measure, so $\|\tau_hf-\tau_hg\|_p=\|f-g\|_p$. The triangle inequality yields
\[\|\tau_hf-f\|_p\le2\varepsilon+\|\tau_hg-g\|_p,\]
which is less than $3\varepsilon$ for all sufficiently small $h$.

In $L^\infty$ the indicator $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}$ satisfies $\|\tau_hf-f\|_\infty=1$ for every $h\ne0$. Shifting the jump leaves a whole interval on which the two functions differ by $1$.
\end{proof}

Exercise~\ref{ex:v7c10d} computes the exact rate for this indicator: the $L^p$ norm of the difference is $(2|h|)^{1/p}$ while $|h|\le1$. The proposition is sharp in that scale.

\section{Convolution}

For measurable $f$ and $g$ on $\R^d$, the \emph{convolution} is
\[(f*g)(x)=\int_{\R^d}f(x-y)\,g(y)\,dy,\]
at every $x$ where the integral converges absolutely. The formula needs a representative for which $(x,y)\mapsto f(x-y)g(y)$ is measurable on the product. Exercise~\ref{ex:completion} describes a Lebesgue measurable set as a Borel set altered by a null set. Starting from the simple functions of Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}, replace each level set by a Borel subset of the same measure, and collect the discrepancies into one Borel null set $Z$. Off $Z$ those simple functions increase to $f$, and redefining the limit to be $0$ on $Z$ produces a Borel function equal to $f$ almost everywhere. Use that representative for every class. If $f$ and $g$ are Borel, the map $(x,y)\mapsto f(x-y)$ is Borel because $x-y$ is continuous, and the product with $g(y)$ is Borel on $\R^{2d}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:borelproduct} identifies that $\sigma$-algebra with the product, so Tonelli (Theorem~\ref{thm:tonelli}) applies to the absolute value. Lebesgue measure is $\sigma$-finite, as Tonelli requires. Changing $f$ or $g$ on a null set does not change the integral. The substitution $y\mapsto x-y$, which preserves Lebesgue measure, gives $f*g=g*f$.

Chapter~\ref{ch:products} already met one case. If $f$ and $g$ are probability densities, $f*g$ is the density of the sum of two independent random variables with those densities.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:convolution}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $f\in L^1$ and $g\in L^p$ for some $1\le p\le\infty$, then $f*g(x)$ is defined for almost every $x$, and $\|f*g\|_p\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_p$.
\item If $f\in L^p$ and $g\in L^q$ with $p$ and $q$ conjugate, then $f*g(x)$ is defined for every $x$, the bound $|f*g(x)|\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ holds everywhere, and $f*g$ is uniformly continuous.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) If $\|f\|_1=0$, then $f=0$ almost everywhere and $f*g=0$. Assume $\|f\|_1>0$. For $p=\infty$,
\[|f*g(x)|\le\|g\|_\infty\int|f(x-y)|\,dy=\|f\|_1\|g\|_\infty,\]
where the integral equals $\|f\|_1$ for every $x$ by translation invariance. For $p=1$, Tonelli gives
\[\int\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|\,dy\Bigr)dx=\|f\|_1\|g\|_1,\]
so the inner integral is finite for almost every $x$ and $\|f*g\|_1\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. Now take $1<p<\infty$ and let $q$ be conjugate to $p$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} to the functions $|f(x-\cdot)|^{1/p}|g|$ and $|f(x-\cdot)|^{1/q}$. Their product is $|f(x-y)||g(y)|$, and
\[\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|\,dy\le\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|^p\,dy\Bigr)^{1/p}\|f\|_1^{1/q}.\]
Raise both sides to the power $p$. Since $p/q=p-1$,
\[\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|\,dy\Bigr)^p\le\|f\|_1^{p-1}\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|^p\,dy.\]
Integrate in $x$ and apply Tonelli to the nonnegative integrand $|f(x-y)||g(y)|^p$. The inner integral in $x$ equals $\|f\|_1$ for each $y$, so
\[\int\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|\,dy\Bigr)^p dx\le\|f\|_1^p\|g\|_p^p.\]
The function $x\mapsto\int|f(x-y)||g(y)|\,dy$ therefore has finite $L^p$ norm, hence is finite for almost every $x$. At those points the integral defining $f*g(x)$ converges absolutely, and $\|f*g\|_p\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_p$.

(b) Write $f^\vee(y)=f(-y)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} and translation invariance give, for every $x$,
\[|f*g(x)|\le\|\tau_x f^\vee\|_p\|g\|_q=\|f\|_p\|g\|_q.\]
The integral converges absolutely everywhere, not merely almost everywhere. Conjugate exponents cannot both be infinite, so one of $p$ and $q$ is finite. If $p=\infty$, exchange $f$ and $g$, which does not change the convolution. With $p$ finite,
\[|f*g(x+h)-f*g(x)|\le\|\tau_{-h}f-f\|_p\|g\|_q,\]
because the difference of the integrands is a translate of $\tau_{-h}f-f$, multiplied by $g$. The right side does not depend on $x$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:translation} sends it to $0$ as $h\to0$.
\end{proof}

Exercise~\ref{ex:youngconv} proves the general Young inequality $\|f*g\|_r\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ whenever $\frac1p+\frac1q=1+\frac1r$. Part (a) is the case in which one of $p$ and $q$ equals $1$. Part (b) is the case $r=\infty$, where $p$ and $q$ are conjugate.

A concrete convolution makes the two bounds visible. Let $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}$ and $g=\mathbf 1_{[0,2]}$. For each fixed $x$, the integrand is the indicator of $[0,1]\cap[x-2,x]$, so $f*g(x)$ is the length of that intersection. The length equals $x$ on $[0,1]$, equals $1$ on $[1,2]$, equals $3-x$ on $[2,3]$, and equals $0$ elsewhere. The output is continuous, although both inputs jump. Its height is $1$ and its area is $\frac12+1+\frac12=2$. Since $f$ and $g$ are nonnegative, Tonelli gives $\int(f*g)=\bigl(\int f\bigr)\bigl(\int g\bigr)$ with no loss, and $1\cdot2=2$, so the $L^1$ bound of part (a) is an equality. Figure~\ref{fig:rwconv} is this calculation.

\emph{Approximate identities.} Let $\varphi\ge0$ be integrable, with $\int\varphi=1$, and set $\varphi_t(x)=t^{-d}\varphi(x/t)$ for $t>0$. The change of variables $y=x/t$ shows $\int\varphi_t=1$. The mass concentrates at the origin: for each fixed $\delta>0$,
\[\int_{|x|>\delta}\varphi_t(x)\,dx=\int_{|y|>\delta/t}\varphi(y)\,dy,\]
and the right side tends to $0$ as $t\to0$ because $\varphi$ is integrable. Convolving $f$ with $\varphi_t$ replaces the value $f(x)$ by an average of $f$ over a region of width about $t$.

\begin{theorem}[approximate identities]\label{thm:approxid}
Let $\varphi_t$ be the rescaled kernel defined above.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $f\in L^p(\R^d)$ and $1\le p<\infty$, then $\|f*\varphi_t-f\|_p\to0$ as $t\to0$.
\item If $f$ is bounded and measurable, then $f*\varphi_t(x)\to f(x)$ at every point where $f$ is continuous, and the convergence is uniform on every set where $f$ is uniformly continuous.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The substitution $y=tz$ rewrites the difference as an average against the fixed probability density $\varphi$:
\[f*\varphi_t(x)-f(x)=\int_{\R^d}\bigl(f(x-tz)-f(x)\bigr)\,\varphi(z)\,dz.\]

(a) For $p=1$ the absolute value of the average is at most the average of the absolute values. For $p>1$, Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} applied to $|f(x-t\,\cdot)-f(x)|\,\varphi^{1/p}$ and $\varphi^{1/q}$ gives the same comparison, because $\int\varphi=1$. In both cases
\[|f*\varphi_t(x)-f(x)|^p\le\int|f(x-tz)-f(x)|^p\,\varphi(z)\,dz.\]
Integrate in $x$ and use Tonelli:
\[\|f*\varphi_t-f\|_p^p\le\int\|\tau_{tz}f-f\|_p^p\,\varphi(z)\,dz.\]
For each fixed $z$ the integrand tends to $0$ as $t\to0$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:translation}. The map $z\mapsto\|\tau_{tz}f-f\|_p$ is continuous, since a further translation by $t(z-z_0)$ has norm tending to $0$ as $z\to z_0$, so the integrand is measurable. It is dominated by $2^p\|f\|_p^p\,\varphi(z)$, which is integrable. Dominated convergence, applied to the probability measure $\varphi(z)\,dz$, sends the integral to $0$.

(b) Let $|f|\le M$. Fix $x$ at which $f$ is continuous, and fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta>0$ so that $|y|<\delta$ implies $|f(x-y)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$. Split the average into $|y|<\delta$ and $|y|\ge\delta$:
\[|f*\varphi_t(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon\int\varphi_t+2M\int_{|y|\ge\delta}\varphi_t(y)\,dy.\]
The first integral equals $1$. The second tends to $0$ as $t\to0$. On a set where $f$ is uniformly continuous, one $\delta$ serves for every $x$ in the set, and the same estimate is uniform.
\end{proof}

\emph{Mollifiers.} The standard smooth step is built from the exponential. Define $\theta(s)=e^{-1/s}$ for $s>0$ and $\theta(s)=0$ for $s\le0$. On $]0,\infty[$ the derivatives have the form $\theta^{(k)}(s)=P_k(1/s)\,e^{-1/s}$ for a polynomial $P_k$. This holds for $k=0$ with $P_0=1$. If it holds for $k$, the product rule gives
\[\frac{d}{ds}\Bigl(P_k(1/s)\,e^{-1/s}\Bigr)=s^{-2}\bigl(P_k(1/s)-P_k'(1/s)\bigr)e^{-1/s},\]
and $P_k(u)-P_k'(u)$ is again a polynomial in $u$. Every derivative tends to $0$ as $s\to0^+$, because $u^m e^{-u}\to0$ as $u\to\infty$: the series for $e^u$ dominates any power, since $e^u\ge u^{m+1}/(m+1)!$. Extend each derivative to be $0$ for $s\le0$. The difference quotient at $0$ is $0$ from the left. From the right it equals $h^{-1}P_k(1/h)e^{-1/h}$. Set $u=1/h$. The quotient is $u\,P_k(u)\,e^{-u}$, which tends to $0$. By induction, $\theta$ is $C^\infty$ on $\R$, and every derivative vanishes on $]-\infty,0]$.

Consequently $\varphi(x)=c\,\theta(1-|x|^2)$, with $c$ chosen so that $\int\varphi=1$, is $C^\infty$, nonnegative, and supported in the closed unit ball. The constant $c$ is finite and positive because $\varphi$ is continuous, not identically zero, and compactly supported. Any such $\varphi$ is called a \emph{mollifier}. The rescaled functions $\varphi_t$ are approximate identities with an extra property: they are smooth.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:smoothdense}
Let $f\in L^p(\R^d)$ with $1\le p\le\infty$, and let $\varphi$ be a mollifier. Then $f*\varphi_t$ is $C^\infty$, and $\partial^\alpha(f*\varphi_t)=f*\partial^\alpha\varphi_t$ for every multi-index $\alpha$. If $p<\infty$, the space $C_c^\infty(\R^d)$ of smooth functions of compact support is dense in $L^p(\R^d)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The support of $\varphi_t$ is the ball of radius $t$, and the same is true of every derivative $\partial_i\varphi_t$. Fix $x_0$ and consider $x$ with $|x-x_0|<1$. For such $x$ the integrand $y\mapsto f(y)\,\partial_i\varphi_t(x-y)$ vanishes unless $|y-x_0|<1+t$. On that ball of finite measure, $f\in L^p$ is integrable, by the inclusion proved earlier in the chapter. The derivative $\partial_i\varphi_t$ is continuous and compactly supported, hence bounded. The dominating function $\sup|\partial_i\varphi_t|\,|f|\,\mathbf 1_{|y-x_0|\le1+t}$ is integrable and does not depend on $x$ near $x_0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter}(b), applied to one coordinate at a time, allows differentiation under the integral sign and gives $\partial_i(f*\varphi_t)=f*\partial_i\varphi_t$. Part (a) of the same theorem, with the same dominant, shows that this derivative is continuous. Iterate on the order of differentiation.

For the density statement, fix $f\in L^p$ with $p<\infty$ and fix $\varepsilon>0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:ccdense} supplies $g\in C_c$ with $\|f-g\|_p<\varepsilon$. The convolution $g*\varphi_t$ is smooth by the first half of the proof, and its support lies in the $t$-neighbourhood of the support of $g$, which is compact. Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid}(a) gives $\|g*\varphi_t-g\|_p<\varepsilon$ for all sufficiently small $t$. The triangle inequality yields $\|f-g*\varphi_t\|_p<2\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

Convolution against a mollifier therefore turns an arbitrary function in $L^p$, for $p<\infty$, into a smooth compactly supported function as close as one wishes. That is a practical way to prove an identity on all of $L^p$. Prove it on $C_c^\infty$, where ordinary calculus is available, and pass to the limit by the continuity of both sides. Volume~8 uses this reduction for the Fourier transform. Figure~\ref{fig:mollify} is the same mechanism applied to a single jump: the smooth graphs approach the step everywhere except at the two points where the step itself is discontinuous.

\begin{beauty}
The whole chapter sits on one comparison of two numbers, $ab\le a^p/p+b^q/q$. Integrate it and the result is Hölder. Apply Hölder to $|f+g|$ times a power of itself and the result is the triangle inequality. A fast Cauchy subsequence then has a summable majorant, and dominated convergence names the limit. When $p=2$, the same inequality bounds $\bigl|\int f\bar g\bigr|$. Volume~8 takes that integral as the inner product of square-integrable functions.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A measurable function is now a point of a normed space. Young's inequality (Lemma~\ref{lem:young}) compares a product of numbers with a sum of powers. Hölder's inequality (Theorem~\ref{thm:holder}) and Minkowski's inequality (Theorem~\ref{thm:minkowski}) turn that comparison into a norm $\|\cdot\|_p$. The Riesz--Fischer theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:rieszfischer}) says the normed space is complete. This is the limit promised in Chapter~\ref{ch:metric}: the completion of the continuous functions of compact support, for the distance $\int|f-g|$, is $L^1(\R^d)$, because those functions are dense (Theorem~\ref{thm:ccdense}). Jensen's inequality (Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen}) compares a convex function of an average with the average of the function. Convolution against an approximate identity returns the original function (Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid}), and convolution against a mollifier makes it smooth (Theorem~\ref{thm:smoothdense}). Every statement in this chapter was proved from the measure, the integral, and the metric results already in hand.

Up to now the integral has used one measure at a time. The next chapter puts two measures on the same space and asks when one of them is given by a density with respect to the other. That question is the Radon--Nikodym theorem. The same circle of ideas asks when a function is the integral of its derivative, and what the best guess of a random variable is when only part of the information is known.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:youngconv}
\emph{Young's inequality for convolutions.} (a) Prove the three-function form of Hölder: $\|fgh\|_1\le\|f\|_a\|g\|_b\|h\|_c$ whenever $\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=1$ and $a,b,c>1$. (b) Let $1\le p,q,r\le\infty$ satisfy $\frac1p+\frac1q=1+\frac1r$. Show that $\|f*g\|_r\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$. Treat the case $1<p,q<r<\infty$ first, by factoring $|f(x-y)g(y)|$ into three pieces and applying (a).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:approxid}
\emph{The heat kernel and the Poisson kernel.} (a) Show that
\[G_t(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-x^2/(4t)},\qquad P_t(x)=\frac{t}{\pi(t^2+x^2)}\]
are approximate identities on $\R$ as $t\to0$. Each should be written as $\varphi_s$ for an explicit $\varphi$ and an explicit scaling $s$ of $t$. (b) For $f\in L^p(\R)$ with $1\le p<\infty$, set $u(t,x)=(f*G_t)(x)$. Show that $\partial_tu=\partial_x^2u$ for $t>0$, and that $u(t,\cdot)\to f$ in $L^p$ as $t\to0$. The norm limit fails at $p=\infty$: if $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,\infty)}$, then $\|f*G_t-f\|_\infty=\frac12$ for every $t>0$. (c) Show that $G_s*G_t=G_{s+t}$ by completing the square in the exponent. The Poisson kernels obey $P_s*P_t=P_{s+t}$ as well. Volume~8 proves that identity with the Fourier transform. Nothing in this volume relies on it.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:logconvex}
\emph{One function, two exponents.} Let $1\le p_0<p_1\le\infty$, let $0<\theta<1$, and define $p_\theta$ by $\frac1{p_\theta}=\frac{1-\theta}{p_0}+\frac\theta{p_1}$. Show that $\|f\|_{p_\theta}\le\|f\|_{p_0}^{1-\theta}\|f\|_{p_1}^\theta$. Deduce that $L^{p_0}\cap L^{p_1}\subset L^p$ for every $p$ between $p_0$ and $p_1$, and that $s\mapsto\ln\|f\|_{1/s}$ is convex where it is finite.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:pinfty}
Suppose $f\in L^{p_0}\cap L^\infty$ for some finite $p_0$. Show that $\|f\|_p\to\|f\|_\infty$ as $p\to\infty$. Then give a function that lies in $L^\infty$ and in no $L^p$ with $p<\infty$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:hardy}
\emph{Hardy's inequality.} Let $p>1$, let $f\ge0$ lie in $L^p(]0,\infty[)$, and set $F(x)=\frac1x\int_0^xf(s)\,ds$. Show that $\|F\|_p\le\frac p{p-1}\|f\|_p$. Write $F(x)=\int_0^1 f(xu)\,du$, insert the weights $u^{\alpha}$ and $u^{-\alpha}$, apply Hölder in the variable $u$, integrate in $x$, and choose $\alpha$. Then show that the constant $\frac p{p-1}$ cannot be replaced by any smaller number, using $f(x)=x^{-1/p}\mathbf 1_{[1,N]}(x)$ and letting $N\to\infty$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:jensen}
Deduce the following three inequalities from Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen}. (a) For $x_i>0$, $(x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n}\le(x_1+\cdots+x_n)/n$. (b) $\mathbb E[X]^2\le\mathbb E[X^2]$. (c) If $\mu$ is a probability and $f>0$ is integrable, then $\int\ln f\,d\mu\le\ln\int f\,d\mu$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:linfinity}
(a) Show that $L^\infty([0,1])$ is not separable, by exhibiting an uncountable family of functions at mutual distance $1$. (b) Show that $L^p([0,1])$ is separable for every $p<\infty$. (c) Show that in $L^\infty(\R)$ the function $\sin$ lies at distance at least $1$ from every step function, and at distance at least $1$ from every function in $C_c(\R)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:tent}
\emph{Three norms, with numbers.} On $[0,1]$ let $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=x^2$. (a) Compute $\|f\|_p$ for $1\le p<\infty$, and check that the value tends to $\|f\|_\infty$. (b) Compare $\int_0^1 fg$ with $\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$. (c) Let $h=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}$ on $\R$. Compute $h*h$, sketch the graph, and compute the norms of $h*h$ in $L^1$, $L^2$ and $L^\infty$. Which bounds in Proposition~\ref{prop:convolution} are equalities for this pair?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v7c10a}

(a) Compute $\|(3,4)\|_1$, $\|(3,4)\|_2$ and $\|(3,4)\|_\infty$. (b) For the point $\bigl(\frac35,\frac45\bigr)$, compute the same three norms and say which of the drawn curves it lies on. \begin{solution}{ex:youngconv}
\idea The inequality interpolates three estimates already proved: $\|f*g\|_\infty\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$, $\|f*g\|_p\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_p$, and $\|f*g\|_1\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. The integrand is split so that one factor carries the $L^r$ integrability and the other two have norms independent of $x$.
\writeup (a) Let $a'=a/(a-1)$, so that $1/a+1/a'=1$. Theorem~\ref{thm:holder} gives $\|fgh\|_1\le\|f\|_a\|gh\|_{a'}$. The hypothesis $\frac1a+\frac1b+\frac1c=1$ rearranges to $\frac1b+\frac1c=\frac1{a'}$, and therefore $\frac{a'}b+\frac{a'}c=1$. A second application of Hölder, to the functions $|g|^{a'}$ and $|h|^{a'}$ with those conjugate exponents, yields
\[\|gh\|_{a'}^{a'}=\int|g|^{a'}|h|^{a'}\,d\mu\le\bigl\||g|^{a'}\bigr\|_{b/a'}\,\bigl\||h|^{a'}\bigr\|_{c/a'}=\|g\|_b^{a'}\|h\|_c^{a'}.\]
Take $a'$th roots and multiply by $\|f\|_a$.

(b) The case $r=\infty$ forces $\frac1p+\frac1q=1$, which is Proposition~\ref{prop:convolution}(b). If $p=1$, the relation forces $q=r$, and if $q=1$ it forces $p=r$; both are Proposition~\ref{prop:convolution}(a). Now assume $1<p,q,r<\infty$. Then $\frac1r=\frac1p+\frac1q-1<\frac1p$, because $q>1$, so $r>p$. The symmetric argument gives $r>q$. The exponents $s_1,s_2$ in the hint therefore lie in $(1,\infty)$, and
\[\frac1r+\frac1{s_1}+\frac1{s_2}=\frac1p+\frac1q-\frac1r=1.\]
Also $\frac pr+\frac p{s_1}=1$ and $\frac qr+\frac q{s_2}=1$, which is why the factorisation in the hint multiplies back to $|f(x-y)g(y)|$. Part (a), with exponents $r$, $s_1$ and $s_2$, gives
\[\int|f(x-y)g(y)|\,dy\le\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)|^p|g(y)|^q\,dy\Bigr)^{1/r}\|f\|_p^{p/s_1}\|g\|_q^{q/s_2}.\]
Raise to the power $r$ and integrate in $x$. Tonelli moves the integral through the product $|f(x-y)|^p|g(y)|^q$, and each inner integral produces one norm:
\[\int\Bigl(\int|f(x-y)g(y)|\,dy\Bigr)^r dx\le\|f\|_p^p\|g\|_q^q\cdot\|f\|_p^{rp/s_1}\|g\|_q^{rq/s_2}.\]
The exponent of $\|f\|_p$ simplifies because $p+rp/s_1=p\bigl(1+r(1/p-1/r)\bigr)=r$, and the exponent of $\|g\|_q$ does the same. Hence $f*g(x)$ is defined for almost every $x$, and $\|f*g\|_r\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:approxid}
\idea Both kernels are dilations of one fixed probability density, so Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid} applies as soon as the scaling is identified. The heat equation is an identity for $G_t$ itself, and differentiation under the integral carries it over to $f*G_t$.
\writeup (a) The function $G_1(x)=e^{-x^2/4}/\sqrt{4\pi}$ is nonnegative. Chapter~\ref{ch:products} gives $\int_\R e^{-v^2}\,dv=\sqrt\pi$. The substitution $v=x/2$ shows that $G_1$ integrates to $1$, so it is a probability density, the Gaussian of variance $2$. Scaling gives
\[s^{-1}G_1(x/s)=\frac1{s\sqrt{4\pi}}\exp\bigl(-x^2/(4s^2)\bigr)=G_{s^2}(x).\]
Thus $G_t=(G_1)_{\sqrt t}$. For the Poisson kernel, $P_1(x)=1/(\pi(1+x^2))$ and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty P_1=\frac1\pi[\arctan x]_{-\infty}^\infty=1$. The same scaling computation gives $t^{-1}P_1(x/t)=P_t(x)$. As $t\to0$ one has $\sqrt t\to0$, so both families are approximate identities.

(b) Write $G_t=c\,t^{-1/2}\exp(-x^2/(4t))$ with $c=(4\pi)^{-1/2}$. The logarithmic derivative in $t$ is $x^2/(4t^2)-1/(2t)$, so $\partial_tG_t=G_t\bigl(x^2/(4t^2)-1/(2t)\bigr)$. In $x$, $\partial_xG_t=G_t\cdot(-x/(2t))$, and one further derivative gives $G_t(x^2/(4t^2))-G_t/(2t)$. The two sides agree, so $\partial_tG_t=\partial_x^2G_t$. Fix $0<t_0<t_1$. On the interval $[t_0,t_1]$ the derivatives $\partial_tG_t$, $\partial_xG_t$ and $\partial_x^2G_t$ are bounded by $C(1+x^2)e^{-x^2/(4t_1)}$ for a constant $C$ depending only on $t_0$ and $t_1$. For $|x|\le R$, the same bound evaluated at $x-y$ is at most
\[k(y)=C\bigl(1+(|y|+R)^2\bigr)\exp\bigl(-((|y|-R)^+)^2/(4t_1)\bigr).\]
This function lies in every $L^q(\R)$. If $f\in L^p$, Hölder gives $|f|k\in L^1$, locally in the parameter as Theorem~\ref{thm:parameter} requires. Differentiation under the integral is therefore legitimate, and $\partial_tu=f*\partial_tG_t=f*\partial_x^2G_t=\partial_x^2u$. Part (a), together with Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid}, gives $u(t,\cdot)\to f$ in $L^p$ for $1\le p<\infty$.

(c) By the formula for $G_t$,
\[(G_s*G_t)(x)=\frac1{4\pi\sqrt{st}}\int_\R\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4s}-\frac{y^2}{4t}\Bigr)dy.\]
The exponent, written over the denominator $4st$, is
\[\frac{t(x-y)^2+sy^2}{4st}=\frac{s+t}{4st}\Bigl(y-\frac{tx}{s+t}\Bigr)^2+\frac{x^2}{4(s+t)}.\]
To see the identity, expand the right-hand side. The coefficients of $y^2$ and of $xy$ match at once. The coefficient of $x^2$ on the right is $t^2/(4st(s+t))+1/(4(s+t))=(t+s)/(4s(s+t))=t/(4st)$, which is the coefficient on the left. The change of variables $u=y-tx/(s+t)$ leaves a standard Gaussian integral. With $a=(s+t)/(4st)$, Chapter~\ref{ch:products} and the substitution $v=\sqrt a\,u$ give $\int_\R e^{-au^2}\,du=\sqrt{\pi/a}=\sqrt{4\pi st/(s+t)}$. Therefore
\[(G_s*G_t)(x)=\frac1{4\pi\sqrt{st}}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi st}{s+t}}\,e^{-x^2/(4(s+t))}=G_{s+t}(x).\]
Diffusing for time $s$ and then for time $t$ is the same as diffusing for time $s+t$. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:products}, the sum of independent centred Gaussians of variances $2s$ and $2t$ is the centred Gaussian of variance $2(s+t)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:logconvex}
\idea The exponent $p_\theta$ is the weighted average of $p_0$ and $p_1$ on the scale $1/p$. Splitting $|f|^{p_\theta}$ into two powers lets Hölder measure one power in $L^{p_0}$ and the other in $L^{p_1}$.
\writeup Suppose first that $p_1<\infty$. Set $a=p_0/((1-\theta)p_\theta)$ and $b=p_1/(\theta p_\theta)$. Then
\[\frac1a+\frac1b=p_\theta\Bigl(\frac{1-\theta}{p_0}+\frac\theta{p_1}\Bigr)=1.\]
Hölder applied to $|f|^{(1-\theta)p_\theta}$ and $|f|^{\theta p_\theta}$ gives
\[\int|f|^{p_\theta}\,d\mu\le\Bigl(\int|f|^{p_0}\,d\mu\Bigr)^{1/a}\Bigl(\int|f|^{p_1}\,d\mu\Bigr)^{1/b}=\|f\|_{p_0}^{(1-\theta)p_\theta}\|f\|_{p_1}^{\theta p_\theta}.\]
The power $1/p_\theta$ is the claim. If $p_1=\infty$, the relation among the exponents forces $p_\theta=p_0/(1-\theta)$, and
\[\int|f|^{p_\theta}\,d\mu\le\|f\|_\infty^{p_\theta-p_0}\int|f|^{p_0}\,d\mu,\]
which is the same bound because $p_\theta-p_0=\theta p_\theta$. Every $p$ strictly between $p_0$ and $p_1$ arises as some $p_\theta$, so $L^{p_0}\cap L^{p_1}\subset L^p$. Finally let $s=1/p$, $s_0=1/p_0$ and $s_1=1/p_1$. The inequality says that $\ln\|f\|_{1/s}$ lies under the chord from $s_0$ to $s_1$. That is convexity in the variable $s$. Volume~8 proves an operator form of the same convexity, the Riesz--Thorin theorem. The one-function bound proved here does not use it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:pinfty}
\idea Raising a number in $[0,1]$ to a high power pushes it toward $0$, and raising a number larger than $1$ pushes it toward infinity. The $L^p$ norm is an average of those powers, so the largest values take over as $p$ grows.
\writeup Let $M_\infty=\|f\|_\infty$. For $p\ge p_0$ the bound $|f|\le M_\infty$ gives $|f|^p\le M_\infty^{p-p_0}|f|^{p_0}$ almost everywhere, hence
\[\|f\|_p\le M_\infty^{1-p_0/p}\|f\|_{p_0}^{p_0/p}.\]
The right side tends to $M_\infty$, so $\limsup_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p\le M_\infty$. If $M_\infty=0$ the limit is $0$ and we stop. If $0<M<M_\infty$, the set $A=\{|f|>M\}$ has positive measure by the definition of the essential supremum. Markov's inequality, in the form $\mu(A)\le M^{-p_0}\|f\|_{p_0}^{p_0}$, shows that the measure is finite. Then $\|f\|_p\ge M\,\mu(A)^{1/p}$. The factor $\mu(A)^{1/p}$ tends to $1$, so $\liminf_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p\ge M$. Every $M<M_\infty$ is allowed, and the liminf is at least $M_\infty$. Combined with the limsup, the limit exists and equals $M_\infty$.

The constant function $1$ on $\R$ satisfies $\|1\|_\infty=1$, while $\int_\R 1^p\,dx=\infty$ for every finite $p$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:hardy}
\idea After the change of scale $s=xu$, the average $F(x)$ becomes $\int_0^1 f(xu)\,du$. Each slice $f(x\,\cdot)$ has $L^p$ norm $u^{-1/p}\|f\|_p$. A weight $u^{\pm\alpha}$ trades the blow-up at $u=0$ against an integral that Hölder can compute.
\writeup The substitution $s=xu$ gives $F(x)=\int_0^1 f(xu)\,du$. Fix $\alpha>0$ with $\alpha q<1$, where $q$ is conjugate to $p$. Insert $u^{\alpha}u^{-\alpha}$ and apply Hölder in $u$:
\[F(x)^p\le\int_0^1 f(xu)^p u^{\alpha p}\,du\cdot\Bigl(\int_0^1 u^{-\alpha q}\,du\Bigr)^{p/q}.\]
The second integral equals $1/(1-\alpha q)$. Integrate in $x$ and apply Tonelli. The change of scale $\int_0^\infty f(xu)^p\,dx=u^{-1}\|f\|_p^p$ produces
\[\|F\|_p^p\le\|f\|_p^p\cdot\frac1{\alpha p\,(1-\alpha q)^{p/q}}.\]
Choose $\alpha=1/(pq)$. Then $\alpha p=1/q$ and $1-\alpha q=1-1/p=1/q$, so the constant is $q\cdot q^{p/q}=q^{1+p/q}$. The relation $1/p+1/q=1$ forces $p+q=pq$, hence $1+p/q=p$, and the constant is $q^p$. Therefore $\|F\|_p\le q=p/(p-1)$.

For the test function $f=x^{-1/p}\mathbf 1_{[1,N]}$ one has $\|f\|_p^p=\int_1^N x^{-1}\,dx=\ln N$. On the interval $1\le x\le N$,
\[F(x)=\frac1x\int_1^x s^{-1/p}\,ds=\frac qx\bigl(x^{1/q}-1\bigr)=q\bigl(x^{-1/p}-x^{-1}\bigr),\]
where $1-1/p=1/q$ was used. Hence
\[\|F\|_p^p\ge q^p\int_1^N\bigl(x^{-1/p}-x^{-1}\bigr)^p dx=q^p\int_1^N x^{-1}\bigl(1-x^{-1/q}\bigr)^p dx.\]
The elementary inequality $1-(1-v)^p\le pv$ for $v\in[0,1]$ and $p\ge1$, which is the tangent bound for $(1-v)^p$ at $v=0$, gives
\[\int_1^N x^{-1}\Bigl(1-\bigl(1-x^{-1/q}\bigr)^p\Bigr)dx\le\int_1^\infty p\,x^{-1-1/q}\,dx=pq.\]
Therefore the integral in the lower bound is at least $\ln N-pq$. For $N>e^{pq}$,
\[\frac{\|F\|_p^p}{\|f\|_p^p}\ge q^p\Bigl(1-\frac{pq}{\ln N}\Bigr).\]
The ratio of the $p$th powers approaches $q^p$, so no smaller constant works for every $f$ and every $N$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:jensen}
\idea Each claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen} for one convex function and one probability: the exponential, the square, and $-\ln$.
\writeup (a) Let $\mu$ be the uniform probability on $\{1,\dots,n\}$, let $f(i)=\ln x_i$, and let $\varphi=\exp$, which is convex on $\R$. The theorem gives $\exp\bigl(n^{-1}\sum\ln x_i\bigr)\le n^{-1}\sum x_i$, and the left side is $(x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n}$. (b) The map $t\mapsto t^2$ is convex on $\R$. If $\mathbb E[X^2]=\infty$ the inequality is immediate. If not, Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen} applies to $X$. (c) The map $-\ln$ is convex on $]0,\infty[$. Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen} says that the negative part of $-\ln f$ is integrable, so $\int(-\ln f)$ is well defined in $]-\infty,+\infty]$, and $-\ln\int f\le\int(-\ln f)$. Rearrangement is the claim. The integral of $f$ is positive because $f>0$ everywhere on a probability space, by the first step of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:jensen}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:linfinity}
\idea An uncountable set of points at mutual distance $1$ cannot sit in a separable metric space: the open balls of radius $\frac12$ around them are disjoint, and each must contain a different point of a countable dense set. For $p<\infty$ the step functions of Theorem~\ref{thm:ccdense} reduce the question to a countable family.
\writeup (a) If $s<t$, then $\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}-\mathbf 1_{[0,s]}=\mathbf 1_{]s,t]}$, whose essential supremum is $1$. Suppose $\{g_n\}$ were dense in $L^\infty([0,1])$. Each ball of radius $\frac12$ about $\mathbf 1_{[0,t]}$ would contain some $g_n$. Those balls are pairwise disjoint, so the map sending $t$ to one such index $n$ would inject $[0,1]$ into $\N$. That is impossible. (b) Theorem~\ref{thm:ccdense} says that step functions are dense in $L^p([0,1])$. A step function on $[0,1]$ can be approximated in $L^p$ by one whose endpoints are rational and whose real and imaginary parts, if the scalar field is $\C$, are rational. The set of such step functions is countable, and a countable union of finite $\varepsilon$-nets is still a countable dense set. (c) A step function vanishes outside a bounded interval, and so does every function in $C_c(\R)$. Beyond that interval, $\sin$ is larger than $1-\varepsilon$ on a set of positive measure, for every $\varepsilon>0$. The essential supremum of the difference is therefore at least $1$. The zero function belongs to both classes and $\|\sin\|_\infty=1$, so the distance is exactly $1$. Uniform approximation in $L^\infty$ has to match the oscillation at infinity, and compact support cannot do it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:tent}
\idea Each norm here is the integral of a power on $[0,1]$, and the convolution is the length of an overlap of two intervals. The numbers show when Hölder and Young are equalities and when they leave a gap.
\writeup (a) Compute $\int_0^1 x^p\,dx=1/(p+1)$, so $\|f\|_p=(p+1)^{-1/p}$. At $p=1$ this is $\frac12$. At $p=2$ it is $1/\sqrt3$, about $0.577$. At $p=100$ it is $101^{-1/100}$. Since $\ln101\approx4.615$, one has $\ln\|f\|_{100}=-(\ln101)/100\approx-0.0462$, and $\|f\|_{100}\approx0.955$. Also $\ln\|f\|_p=-(\ln(p+1))/p\to0$, so $\|f\|_p\to1=\|f\|_\infty$. On a probability space the norms increase with $p$, and the computation agrees: $\frac12<1/\sqrt3<0.955<1$.

(b) The product integral is $\int_0^1 x^3\,dx=\frac14$. The second norm of $g$ is $\bigl(\int_0^1 x^4\,dx\bigr)^{1/2}=1/\sqrt5$, so $\|f\|_2\|g\|_2=1/\sqrt{15}\approx0.258$. Hölder holds, and the gap past $\frac14=0.25$ is about $0.008$. The functions $x$ and $x^2$ are not proportional, so equality was not expected.

(c) For each $x$, $(h*h)(x)$ is the length of $[0,1]\cap[x-1,x]$. That length equals $x$ for $0\le x\le1$, equals $2-x$ for $1\le x\le2$, and equals $0$ otherwise. The graph is a tent of height $1$ over $[0,2]$. The area of the tent is $1$, so $\|h*h\|_1=1$. The height is $1$, so $\|h*h\|_\infty=1$. The square integral is $2\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=\frac23$, so $\|h*h\|_2=\sqrt{2/3}\approx0.816$. The input norms are $\|h\|_1=\|h\|_2=\|h\|_\infty=1$. Nonnegative functions give equality in $\|h*h\|_1=\|h\|_1\|h\|_1$, by Tonelli. At $x=1$ the two factors $h(1-y)$ and $h(y)$ are the same function, so $|h*h(1)|=\|h\|_2\|h\|_2$ and the bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:convolution}(b) is an equality. The bound $\|h*h\|_2\le\|h\|_1\|h\|_2$ reads $0.816\le1$ and is strict. The tent is continuous, as part (b) of that proposition requires when the exponents are $2$ and $2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c10a}
\idea Counting measure on two points turns every integral into a sum of two terms. The unit ball of that norm is exactly one curve in Figure~\ref{fig:lpballs}.
\writeup (a) The three formulas are the sum of absolute values, the Euclidean length, and the larger absolute value: $\|(3,4)\|_1=7$, $\|(3,4)\|_2=5$ and $\|(3,4)\|_\infty=4$. They decrease as the exponent grows, in agreement with $\ell^p\subset\ell^q$ for $p<q$.

(b) Let $v=\bigl(\frac35,\frac45\bigr)$. Then $\|v\|_1=\frac75>1$, $\|v\|_2=\bigl(\frac{9}{25}+\frac{16}{25}\bigr)^{1/2}=1$, and $\|v\|_\infty=\frac45<1$. The point lies outside the diamond $p=1$, on the circle $p=2$, and inside the square $p=\infty$.

(c) The pairing of $(3,4)$ with $w=(1,2)$ is $3\cdot1+4\cdot2=11$. At the exponents $2$ and $2$, the product of the norms is $5\sqrt5$. Comparing squares, $11^2=121$ and $(5\sqrt5)^2=125$, so $11<5\sqrt5$ and Hölder holds with a small gap. At the exponents $1$ and $\infty$, $\|w\|_\infty=2$ and $7\cdot2=14$, so $11\le14$. The exchanged endpoint bound is $\| (3,4) \|_\infty\|w\|_1=4\cdot3=12$, and $11\le12$ as well.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c10b}
\idea The spike is scaled so that its $L^p$ norm stays equal to $1$. Every other exponent sees a different power of $n$, because the height and the width scale differently.
\writeup (a) The integral of the $p$th power is $\int_0^{1/n} n\,dx=1$, so $\|f_n\|_p=1$. The $L^1$ norm is the height times the width, $n^{1/p}\cdot n^{-1}=n^{1/p-1}$.

(b) If $x\le0$, then $f_n(x)=0$ for every $n$. If $x>0$, choose an integer $N>1/x$. For all $n\ge N$ one has $1/n<x$, so $x$ lies outside $]0,1/n[$ and $f_n(x)=0$. The sequence converges to $0$ at every point. It does not converge to $0$ in $L^p$, because the norm equals $1$ for every $n$.

(c) For $q<\infty$, $\|f_n\|_q^q=\int_0^{1/n}n^{q/p}\,dx=n^{q/p-1}$, hence $\|f_n\|_q=n^{1/p-1/q}$. For $q=\infty$ the norm is the height $n^{1/p}$. If $q<p$, then $1/q>1/p$ and the exponent $1/p-1/q$ is negative, so $\|f_n\|_q\to0$. If $q=p$, the norm equals $1$ for every $n$. If $q>p$, including $q=\infty$, the exponent is positive and $\|f_n\|_q\to\infty$. On a space of infinite measure, membership in one $L^p$ says nothing about the others, and this sequence is a quantitative form of that fact.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c10c}
\idea After normalisation both norms equal $1$, and Hölder's inequality is the integral of Young's inequality. Equality holds in the integral of a nonnegative function precisely when the integrand vanishes almost everywhere.
\writeup Set $F=|f|/\|f\|_p$ and $G=|g|/\|g\|_q$, so that $\|F\|_p=\|G\|_q=1$. The products $fg$ and $FG$ differ by the constant factor $\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$, and equality $\|fg\|_1=\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ is equivalent to $\int FG\,d\mu=1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:young} gives $FG\le F^p/p+G^q/q$, and the integral of the right side equals $1/p+1/q=1$. The difference $F^p/p+G^q/q-FG$ is nonnegative and integrates to $0$, so it vanishes almost everywhere. At every such point Lemma~\ref{lem:young} forces $F^p=G^q$. Substituting the normalisations,
\[\frac{|f|^p}{\|f\|_p^p}=\frac{|g|^q}{\|g\|_q^q}\qquad\text{almost everywhere.}\]
The constant $c=\|f\|_p^p/\|g\|_q^q$ is positive and finite. Conversely, if $|f|^p=c|g|^q$ almost everywhere, integrate to obtain $\|f\|_p^p=c\|g\|_q^q$, so $c$ equals the ratio just written. Then $F^p=G^q$ almost everywhere, Young's inequality is an equality at almost every point, and integrating gives $\int FG=1$.

On $[0,1]$, the choice $f=g=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}$ has both norms equal to $1$ and $\int fg=1$, so equality holds, and $|f|^p=|g|^q$ with constant $c=1$. For $f(x)=x$, $g=1$ and $p=2$, one has $\int fg=\frac12$ and $\|f\|_2\|g\|_2=1/\sqrt3$. Since $\frac14<\frac13$, the inequality is strict. The functions $x^2$ and $1$ are not proportional, in agreement with the criterion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c10d}
\idea Shifting an indicator moves a block of mass of width $|h|$ off the original interval and leaves a hole of the same width. Averaging against the uniform kernel of width $2t$ replaces each jump by a straight ramp of width $t$, and the $L^1$ cost of those two ramps is exactly $t$.
\writeup (a) For $0<h\le1$ the translate $\tau_hf$ is the indicator of $[h,1+h]$. The two functions differ by $1$ on $[0,h)$ and on $(1,1+h]$, and they agree elsewhere. The set of disagreement has measure $2h$, so $\|\tau_hf-f\|_p^p=2h$ and $\|\tau_hf-f\|_p=(2h)^{1/p}$. The case $-1\le h<0$ is the same computation with $|h|$ in place of $h$.

(b) Divide by $h^{1/p}$. The quotient equals $2^{1/p}$ for every $h\in(0,1]$, so it does not tend to $0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:translation} says that the norm tends to $0$, and this example shows that the approach can be no faster than a constant times $|h|^{1/p}$. A statement of the form $\|\tau_hf-f\|_p=o(|h|^{1/p})$ for every $f\in L^p$ would contradict the computation.

(c) The function $\varphi$ is nonnegative and $\int\varphi=\frac12\cdot2=1$. By construction $\varphi_t(x)=t^{-1}\varphi(x/t)$, so $(\varphi_t)$ is an approximate identity in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid}. For $0<t\le\frac12$ and $f=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}$,
\[(f*\varphi_t)(x)=\frac1{2t}\,\lambda\bigl([0,1]\cap[x-t,x+t]\bigr).\]
The intersection is empty for $x\le -t$ and for $x\ge 1+t$. On $[-t,t]$ it equals $[0,x+t]$, of length $x+t$, because $x+t\le 2t\le1$. On $[t,1-t]$ it equals $[x-t,x+t]$, of length $2t$. On $[1-t,1+t]$ it equals $[x-t,1]$, of length $1-x+t$, because $x-t\ge1-2t\ge0$. Dividing by $2t$,
\[
f*\varphi_t(x)=\begin{cases}
(x+t)/(2t) & -t\le x\le t,\\
1 & t\le x\le 1-t,\\
(1-x+t)/(2t) & 1-t\le x\le 1+t,\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
The difference $f*\varphi_t-f$ vanishes on $[t,1-t]$. On $[-t,0]$ the function $f$ is $0$ and the convolution equals $(x+t)/(2t)$, whose integral is $\int_0^t u/(2t)\,du=t/4$. On $[0,t]$ the difference is $(t-x)/(2t)$, whose integral is again $t/4$. The two intervals to the right of $1-t$ repeat these integrals by the substitution $x\mapsto 1-x$. The four contributions add to $t$, so $\|f*\varphi_t-f\|_1=t$. Theorem~\ref{thm:approxid}(a) predicted that the norm tends to $0$. For this kernel and this $f$, the norm equals the width $t$ itself.
\end{solution}

\item E.~H.~Lieb and M.~Loss, \emph{Analysis}, 2nd ed., AMS, 2001, Chapters~2 and~4, discuss convolution, the sharp constant in Young's inequality, and approximation by smooth functions.

Jensen's post at the Copenhagen Telephone Company, and the fact that he held no university appointment, are recorded in his biography at the MacTutor History of Mathematics archive (St Andrews).
