% Polygonal Jordan theorem.
\section{Jordan's theorem for polygons}\label{sec:jordanpoly}

A polygon you can draw with a ruler is the right place to see the theorem before the curve is allowed to bend. Take $n\ge3$ points $v_1,\dots,v_n$ in the plane, and join them in order by straight segments, including the segment from $v_n$ back to $v_1$. Write $e_i=[v_i,v_{i+1}]$, with indices read modulo $n$, so $v_{n+1}$ means $v_1$. These segments are the edges. The polygon $P$ is the closed path $v_1v_2\cdots v_nv_1$. We also use the letter $P$ for the set of points that lie on some edge. That set is compact.

The polygon is \emph{simple} when two edges meet only in the case the definition forces: consecutive edges meet at their shared vertex, and no other pair of edges meets at all. Consecutive edges are also required to be non-collinear, so each corner really turns. A triangle and a rectangle with a rectangular bite taken out of one side are simple. A figure eight is a closed polygon, and it is not simple: the crossing is a point where non-consecutive edges meet.

\begin{theorem}[Jordan's theorem for polygons]\label{thm:jordanpoly}
Let $P$ be a simple polygon. Then $\R^2\setminus P$ has exactly two connected components. One component is bounded and the other is unbounded, and each has boundary equal to $P$.
\end{theorem}

The argument has two halves, and Figure~\ref{fig:polygon} is the picture for the first half. A ray from a test point hits the edges a certain number of times. The parity of that number is constant on each piece of the complement, and it takes both values, so there are at least two pieces. The second half walks along both banks of $P$ and shows that two pieces are all there are. In this section, connected means path-connected. The components of an open set in the plane are themselves open.

\subsection*{Parity gives at least two components}

Fix a unit vector $d$ that is parallel to no edge, and parallel to no line through two vertices. Only finitely many directions are forbidden, so such a $d$ exists. For each vertex $v$, call the line $v+\R d$ a \emph{bad line}. There are $n$ bad lines, and each contains exactly one vertex: a second vertex on that line would mean that $d$ points along the line through those two vertices.

Now take $x\notin P$ off every bad line. The ray $x+\R_{\ge0}d$ meets no vertex. It is not parallel to any edge, so it meets each edge in at most one point, and at that point it passes from one side of the edge to the other. Let $c(x)$ be the number of edges the ray meets, and set $\pi(x)=c(x)\bmod 2$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:parity}
Every point $x\in\R^2\setminus P$ has an open disc $D$ about it, missing $P$, such that $\pi$ takes a single value on the points of $D$ that lie off the bad lines.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $x\notin P$, and let $\ell=x+\R d$. This line contains at most one vertex, so the ray from $x$ meets at most one vertex. A nearby point $x'$ has its ray equal to the ray from $x$, shifted by the vector $x'-x$. We look at one edge at a time and then choose a radius $\rho>0$ small enough for every edge at once. The disc $D$ is the open disc of that radius about $x$.

\emph{An edge the ray from $x$ misses.} The ray is closed and the edge is compact, so the distance between them is positive. If $\rho$ is smaller than that distance, the ray from $x'$ misses the edge as well.

\emph{An edge the ray from $x$ meets at a non-vertex $y$.} The line $\ell$ is not parallel to the edge, so the line $x'+\R d$ meets the line through the edge in exactly one point. That intersection depends continuously on $x'$, and it equals $y$ when $x'=x$. The point $y$ lies in the relative interior of the edge. Also $y=x+td$ with $t>0$, because $x$ itself is not on $P$. For $\rho$ small enough, the intersection stays in the relative interior and stays on the forward ray from $x'$. So the ray from $x'$ meets this edge once.

If the ray from $x$ meets no vertex, every edge is one of these two kinds. The ray from $x'$ then meets exactly the same edges, and $c(x')=c(x)$ throughout $D$.

\emph{The two edges that meet at a vertex $v$ on the ray.} Suppose the ray from $x$ passes through a vertex $v$, where edges $e$ and $f$ meet. Then $\ell$ is the bad line of $v$. Every other edge is one of the two kinds already treated, so those edges contribute the same count for $x'$ as for $x$, once $\rho$ is small. Neither $e$ nor $f$ is parallel to $d$. Each of them leaves $v$ into one of the two open half-planes bounded by $\ell$, and the other end of each is a vertex at a positive distance from $\ell$. Take $\rho$ smaller than both of those distances. Let $x'\in D$ lie off $\ell$. Its ray runs parallel to $\ell$, on the same side of $\ell$ as $x'$, at distance less than $\rho$.

For $\rho$ small, that ray meets $e$ exactly when $e$ lies on the side of $x'$, and the same holds for $f$. Indeed, if $e$ lies on the other side, the line $x'+\R d$ misses $e$ entirely. If $e$ lies on the side of $x'$, the line meets $e$ once: along $e$, the distance to $\ell$ grows linearly from $0$ at $v$ to a value larger than $\rho$. The meeting point tends to $v$ as $x'\to x$. Since $v=x+td$ with $t>0$, a small $\rho$ puts the meeting point on the forward ray. That is the claim.

Now count $e$ and $f$ only. If they lie on the same side of $\ell$, the ray from $x'$ meets both when $x'$ is on that side, and it meets neither when $x'$ is on the other side. Those two edges contribute $2$ or $0$. If they lie on opposite sides, the ray meets exactly one of them, whichever side $x'$ is on, and the contribution is $1$. In both geometries the parity coming from $e$ and $f$ is the same on the two sides of $\ell$. The other edges do not change the comparison. Therefore, on $D$, every point that lies off the bad lines has the same value of $\pi$.
\end{proof}

By the lemma, $\pi$ extends to every point of $\R^2\setminus P$. If $x$ lies on a bad line, set $\pi(x)$ equal to $\pi(x')$ for any $x'$ near $x$ and off the bad lines. The lemma says the nearby value does not depend on the choice. The extended $\pi$ is constant on a neighbourhood of every point of the complement, except that on the bad lines we only used the off-line points; in any case it is locally constant on $\R^2\setminus P$. A locally constant function is constant along a path: the set of times at which it equals its value at the start is open and closed in $[0,1]$, and $[0,1]$ is connected. So $\pi$ is constant on each component of the complement.

It remains only to see that both parities occur. Let $m$ be an interior point of some edge $e$, off the bad lines. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ small enough that the segment $[m-\varepsilon d,\,m+\varepsilon d]$ meets $P$ only at $m$, and small enough that both endpoints lie off the bad lines. The ray from $m-\varepsilon d$ is that short segment, stopped before $m+\varepsilon d$, followed by the ray from $m+\varepsilon d$. It therefore meets $P$ in the hits of the second ray, plus the extra point $m$. Hence $c(m-\varepsilon d)$ and $c(m+\varepsilon d)$ differ by $1$, and $\pi$ differs at the two endpoints. They lie in different components. The complement has at least two components.

\subsection*{The two sides give at most two}

The second half builds a thin neighbourhood of $P$ and watches how its pieces glue. Let $r>0$ be smaller than half the length of every edge, and smaller than half the distance from each vertex to every edge that does not contain it. Simplicity makes each of those distances positive: a vertex cannot land on a non-incident edge. Write $B_v$ for the open disc of radius $r$ centred at the vertex $v$.

In polar coordinates centred at $v$, the set $B_v\cap P$ is two radii, one along each edge that ends at $v$. The two radii are distinct because consecutive edges are not collinear. They cut $B_v\setminus P$ into two open sectors. Each sector is path-connected: it is an open interval of angles, crossed with the radii $(0,r)$.

Now fix an edge $e=[v,w]$. Let $e'$ be the set of points of $e$ at distance at least $r/2$ from both $v$ and $w$. Then $e'$ is a compact segment, and it is disjoint from every other edge, so its distance $\delta_e$ to the rest of $P$ is positive. Let $R_e$ be the open set of points whose orthogonal projection onto the line through $e$ lands in the relative interior of $e'$, and whose distance to that line is less than $h$. Choose $h>0$ smaller than both $\delta_e$ and $r/4$, and small enough that the two ends of $R_e$, the ones near $v$ and near $w$, sit inside $B_v$ and $B_w$ and stay clear of the other edge at those vertices. Then $R_e\cap P$ is the relative interior of $e'$. The endpoints of $e'$ project to the ends of that segment, so they are not in the open set $R_e$. Removing $P$ cuts $R_e$ into two open rectangles, $R_e^+$ and $R_e^-$, one on each side of $e$. Each is convex, hence path-connected.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sides}
Each of $R_e^+$ and $R_e^-$ meets exactly one sector at each of its two ends. At a vertex, the two sides of one edge meet different sectors.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The end of $R_e^+$ near $v$ is a connected subset of $B_v\setminus P$, so it lies in a single sector. Use polar coordinates at $v$. Points of $R_e^+$ near $v$ have angles just to one side of the direction of $e$, and points of $R_e^-$ have angles just to the other side. The two edge-directions at $v$ cut the circle of angles into two open arcs, and the direction of $e$ is an endpoint of both arcs. Angles just above that direction fall in one arc, and angles just below it fall in the other. The rectangles $R_e^+$ and $R_e^-$ therefore meet different sectors.
\end{proof}

Orient the boundary, and travel around $P$ in that direction. For each edge $e$, write $R_e^L$ for the open rectangle on your left and $R_e^R$ for the one on your right. These are $R_e^+$ and $R_e^-$ in some order.

The pieces we glue are the $2n$ side rectangles and the $2n$ sectors. Lemma~\ref{lem:sides} says each rectangle meets one sector at its initial end and one sector at its final end. At the vertex where edges $e$ and $f$ meet, each sector receives exactly one side of $e$ and exactly one side of $f$. The next paragraph names which sector that is.

Stand at the head of $e_i$, where $e_{i+1}$ begins. Choose coordinates so that this vertex is the origin, the incoming edge lies on the negative real axis, and the orientation traverses that edge toward the origin. A step to your left is then a step upward: the leftward unit normal is the direction of travel turned through a right angle counterclockwise, which adds $\pi/2$ to the argument. The outgoing edge is not collinear with the incoming one, so it does not leave along the positive real axis and it does not leave along the negative real axis. Its argument $\varphi$, taken in $(0,2\pi)$, is therefore different from $\pi$.

A test point just above the incoming edge has argument $\pi-\eta$ for a small $\eta>0$. A test point just to the left of the outgoing edge has argument $\varphi+\eta$. Check the easy corner before the general one. If the next edge leaves straight up, then $\varphi=\pi/2$, and both arguments lie strictly between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$. That open quadrant is one sector.

The same pairing holds for every legal $\varphi$. On the circle, run counterclockwise from $\varphi$ until you reach $\pi$. For every sufficiently small $\eta$, both $\varphi+\eta$ and $\pi-\eta$ lie on that open arc: the first just after the start, the second just before the end. The arc is one of the two sectors cut by the edge rays, which sit at angles $\varphi$ and $\pi$. Both left-hand ends therefore meet that one sector. Lemma~\ref{lem:sides} puts both right-hand ends in the other sector.

Walk the left-hand pieces in the order of the orientation. The left rectangle of $e_i$ meets the left sector at the head of $e_i$, and that same sector meets the left rectangle of $e_{i+1}$. Continuing around $P$, the $n$ left rectangles and the $n$ left sectors form a single closed chain, each meeting the next. The right rectangles and the right sectors form a second chain. Every one of the $4n$ pieces appears in exactly one chain.

Each rectangle and each sector is path-connected, and consecutive pieces of a chain meet. The union of the pieces in one chain is therefore path-connected: to go from a point in an early piece to a point in a later one, walk inside the current piece to the overlap, then inside the next piece, and so on. Call the two unions $S_1$ and $S_2$. Both sit in $\R^2\setminus P$.

The discs $B_v$ and the rectangles $R_e$ are open and cover $P$. Remove $P$ from any one of them and what remains is a union of sectors or of side rectangles, hence a subset of $S_1\cup S_2$. Let $U$ be a component of $\R^2\setminus P$. It is open, and it is not the whole plane. The plane is connected, so $U$ is not closed: otherwise it would be a nonempty proper set that is both open and closed. A point of the closure of $U$ that does not lie in $U$ is a boundary point $p$. It cannot lie in a different component of the complement, because each such component is open and therefore contains a disc that misses $U$. So $p\in P$. Some set of the cover contains $p$, and that open set meets $U$. Removing $P$ from it still meets $U$, and the remainder lies in $S_1\cup S_2$. Thus $U$ meets $S_1$ or $S_2$. Whichever of those two it meets, it contains, because that set is path-connected and lies in the complement. There are at most two components.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:jordanpoly}]
The complement has at least two components and at most two, so it has exactly two. Each contains one of $S_1$ and $S_2$. In particular $S_1\ne S_2$: a single path-connected set in the complement would have left room for only one component. We are in the case of two chains. Each component therefore contains one side of every edge, and one sector at every vertex.

Every point of an edge is a limit of points from the left rectangle and of points from the right rectangle. Every vertex is a limit of points from both of its sectors. So every point of $P$ is a limit of points from each component. The boundary of a component is contained in $P$, by the same reason as in the paragraph above: a boundary point cannot lie in the complement. The boundary of each component is therefore all of $P$.

Finally, $P$ is compact, so it lies in some closed disc $B$. The complement of $B$ is path-connected. From the centre of $B$, the outward ray through a point outside $B$ stays outside $B$. Given two such points, walk out along those rays to a common large circle, travel along the circle, and walk back in. The complement of $B$ misses $P$, so it lies in one component of $\R^2\setminus P$. That component is unbounded. The other component cannot meet the complement of $B$, so it is contained in $B$, and it is bounded.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[polygons and Jordan's own proof]\label{rem:jordangeneral}
Jordan's argument of 1887 replaced a general curve by polygons and treated the polygonal case as settled. For a long time that step was held against him. The polygonal case is already a real argument. The ray that passes through a vertex is Lemma~\ref{lem:parity}, and the two sectors at a corner are Lemma~\ref{lem:sides}. Hales's reading of Jordan's text is listed in the sources. It separates the steps Jordan wrote from the steps he left out. The proof of the general theorem, later in this chapter, does not call on polygons at all.
\end{remark}

Where did the argument use that $P$ is simple? Run the same steps on a figure eight. Which step fails, and how many components does the complement have?
\end{think}

A polygon is a curve you can build from segments. A general simple closed curve is only a continuous injective image of a circle. It may contain no straight piece, and it may have no tangent at any point. A ray can then meet the curve in a Cantor set, and a small disc about a point of the curve need not split into two tidy sectors. Parity of crossings will not survive in that form. What survives a continuous motion is an integer. That integer is the winding number.
