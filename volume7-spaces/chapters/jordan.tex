\chapter{The Jordan Curve Theorem}\label{ch:jordan}

\begin{center}\itshape
A closed curve that never crosses itself cuts the plane into an inside and an outside.\\
The picture is old. The proof uses continuity, and nothing smoother.
\end{center}

Draw a loop. Keep the pen on the page, and do not cross the line you have already drawn. Stop when the pen is back at the start.

The sheet now has two pieces. One piece is trapped inside the loop. The other runs off the edge of the page. A path from a trapped point to a point near the edge has to step across the ink.

You already believe this. A proof asks for something else. It asks what the inside is, as a set. If the loop is a maze, staring at a marked point does not tell you which piece the point belongs to.

No coin is flipped in the argument below. The split is a piece of order. Continuity, on its own, cuts the plane in two.

Camille Jordan wrote the statement in his \emph{Cours d'analyse} of 1887. A closed curve with no self-crossing divides the plane into two regions, one bounded and one not. The proof he gave is long. For about a century it was often described as wrong. In January 2005 Thomas Hales finished a proof of the theorem that a computer could check line by line. He then read Jordan again, and judged the old argument essentially right. The thin part was the case of a polygon, which Jordan had treated as obvious. In 1905 Oswald Veblen published a proof that was accepted as complete.

\portrait{jordan}{Camille Jordan (1838--1922). Photograph, before 1923, author unknown. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

A curve that the theorem has to cover can be wild. In 1903 William Osgood built a continuous injective image of a circle that carries positive area, once area has been given a meaning. In 1904 Helge von Koch described the snowflake. Its boundary is a closed curve, and it has no tangent at any point. A proof that includes both examples has only continuity to spend.

Three jobs follow, in order. First come polygons, where a ray and a parity still work. Next comes the winding number of a continuous closed curve, the replacement for that parity when the curve has no straight piece. From the winding number we read two facts about a disc. You cannot squash the closed disc onto its boundary circle while leaving every boundary point fixed. Every continuous map of the closed disc to itself has a fixed point, and the same is true of a closed square. Last comes the theorem itself. The proof is the one Ryuji Maehara gave in 1984. It turns the fixed-point fact into a forced meeting of two paths across a rectangle, and it builds those paths out of the curve.

Volume~8, \emph{Operators, Spectra and Transforms}, uses this inside and this outside to take Cauchy's theorem past star-shaped domains. That sentence is a preview. No proof in this chapter quotes Volume~8.

\section{Winding numbers of continuous curves}

Volume~5 defined the winding number
\[
n(\gamma,a)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{\dd z}{z-a}
\]
for a piecewise $C^1$ closed path. The Jordan theorem needs the same integer for a curve that is only continuous, so there is no derivative and no integral to write down. Here a \emph{curve} is a continuous map $\gamma:[\alpha,\beta]\to\C$. It is closed when $\gamma(\alpha)=\gamma(\beta)$. Its image is written $\gamma^*$.

\begin{lemma}[continuous argument]\label{lem:lift}
Let $\gamma:[\alpha,\beta]\to\C^*$ be continuous. Then there is a continuous $\theta:[\alpha,\beta]\to\R$ with $\gamma(t)=|\gamma(t)|\,e^{i\theta(t)}$ for every $t$. Any two such functions differ by a constant in $2\pi\Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The image of $\gamma$ is compact and misses $0$, so $m=\min|\gamma|$ is positive. Uniform continuity gives $\delta>0$ such that $|s-t|<\delta$ implies $|\gamma(s)-\gamma(t)|<m$. For such a pair,
\[
\Bigl|\frac{\gamma(s)}{\gamma(t)}-1\Bigr|=\frac{|\gamma(s)-\gamma(t)|}{|\gamma(t)|}<1.
\]
The quotient lies in the open disc $D(1,1)$, and that disc lies in the open right half-plane.

On $\{\operatorname{Re} w>0\}$ there is a unique argument in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:exp}(e) puts the unit complex number $w/|w|$ on the circle as $e^{it}$ for a unique $t\in[0,2\pi)$. Positive real part forces that representative, after subtracting $2\pi$ if it sits in $(3\pi/2,2\pi)$, to land in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Call this choice $\operatorname{Arg} w$, and set $\operatorname{Log} w=\ln|w|+i\operatorname{Arg} w$.

This $\operatorname{Arg}$ is continuous on the open right half-plane. A point there has argument $\theta_0$ strictly inside $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Choose a closed subinterval $I\subset(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ that contains $\theta_0$ in its interior. The map $\theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$ is a continuous bijection from the compact interval $I$ onto a closed arc. The arc is Hausdorff, so the inverse is continuous by Proposition~\ref{prop:compactbasic}(d). A small neighbourhood of our point has its unit vectors on that arc, and on that neighbourhood $\operatorname{Arg}$ agrees with the inverse. So $\operatorname{Arg}$, and therefore $\operatorname{Log}$, is continuous on $\{\operatorname{Re} w>0\}$.

Take a subdivision $\alpha=t_0<\dots<t_N=\beta$ of mesh less than $\delta$. Choose $\theta(t_0)$ with $\gamma(t_0)=|\gamma(t_0)|\,e^{i\theta(t_0)}$. On each piece $[t_j,t_{j+1}]$ the quotient $\gamma(t)/\gamma(t_j)$ stays in the right half-plane, and we set
\[
\theta(t)=\theta(t_j)+\operatorname{Arg}\frac{\gamma(t)}{\gamma(t_j)}.
\]
The pieces agree at the joints, so $\theta$ is continuous on the whole interval. It does the job. On one piece,
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\gamma(t)|\,e^{i\theta(t)}
&=|\gamma(t)|\,e^{i\theta(t_j)}\cdot\frac{\gamma(t)/\gamma(t_j)}{|\gamma(t)/\gamma(t_j)|}\\
&=|\gamma(t_j)|\,e^{i\theta(t_j)}\cdot\frac{\gamma(t)}{\gamma(t_j)}
=\gamma(t),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the middle step uses $|\gamma(t)|/|\gamma(t)/\gamma(t_j)|=|\gamma(t_j)|$.

If $\theta$ and $\theta_1$ both work, then $e^{i(\theta_1(t)-\theta(t))}=1$ for every $t$, so the difference takes values in $2\pi\Z$. The difference is continuous. Its image is an interval, by Theorem~\ref{thm:intervals}, and the only intervals inside $2\pi\Z$ are single points. The difference is constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $\gamma$ be a closed curve and let $a\notin\gamma^*$. If $\theta$ is a continuous argument of the curve $\gamma-a$, the \emph{winding number} of $\gamma$ about $a$ is the integer
\[
n(\gamma,a)=\frac{\theta(\beta)-\theta(\alpha)}{2\pi}\in\Z.
\]
\end{definition}

The value is an integer because $\gamma(\alpha)=\gamma(\beta)$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:lift} says the two arguments of the same nonzero point differ by an element of $2\pi\Z$. A second choice of $\theta$ changes both ends by the same constant, and the difference $\theta(\beta)-\theta(\alpha)$ does not move.

The unit circle is the model. Let $\gamma(t)=e^{it}$ for $0\le t\le 2\pi$. Then $\theta(t)=t$ is a continuous argument of $\gamma-0$, and it increases by $2\pi$, so $n(\gamma,0)=1$. The curve turns once about the origin.

Figure~\ref{fig:windingarg} is that count on a bent loop. One turn about the interior point, and no turn about the exterior point. The rest of the section says why the integer is well defined, why a small push does not change it, and why it is constant on each piece of the complement.

For a piecewise $C^1$ closed path, this integer is the winding number of Volume~5. On each piece of a fine subdivision, $L(t)=\log|\gamma(t)-a|+i\theta(t)$ equals $\operatorname{Log}\bigl((\gamma(t)-a)/(\gamma(t_j)-a)\bigr)$ plus a constant, so $L'=\gamma'/(\gamma-a)$. The curve is closed, so the real logarithm returns to the same value at the two ends, and
\[
\int_\gamma\frac{\dd z}{z-a}=L(\beta)-L(\alpha)=i\bigl(\theta(\beta)-\theta(\alpha)\bigr).
\]
Divide by $2\pi i$ and the integral formula appears. Later proofs in this chapter use the argument, not the integral.

Arguments add when you multiply. If $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ are closed curves in $\C^*$, the pointwise product $t\mapsto\gamma_1(t)\gamma_2(t)$ is a closed curve in $\C^*$, and a continuous argument of the product is the sum of continuous arguments. The changes from $\alpha$ to $\beta$ add, so
\[
n(\gamma_1\gamma_2,0)=n(\gamma_1,0)+n(\gamma_2,0).
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:windingprops}
Let $\gamma$, $\gamma_0$ and $\gamma_1$ be closed curves on the same interval $[\alpha,\beta]$, and let $a\in\C$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] (\emph{Dog on a leash.}) If $|\gamma_1(t)-\gamma_0(t)|<|\gamma_0(t)-a|$ at every $t$, then $n(\gamma_1,a)=n(\gamma_0,a)$.
\item[(b)] (\emph{Homotopy.}) Suppose $H:[0,1]\times[\alpha,\beta]\to\C\setminus\{a\}$ is continuous, and suppose that for each fixed $s$ the curve $t\mapsto H(s,t)$ is closed. Then $n(H(s,\cdot),a)$ does not depend on $s$.
\item[(c)] The integer $n(\gamma,a)$ is constant as $a$ moves inside one component of $\C\setminus\gamma^*$. On the unbounded component the constant is $0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The hand holding the leash is $\gamma_0(t)$, the dog is $\gamma_1(t)$, and the tree is the point $a$. The hypothesis says the leash is shorter than the distance from the hand to the tree, so the dog cannot reach the tree. In formulas, the quotient
\[
q=\frac{\gamma_1-a}{\gamma_0-a}=1+\frac{\gamma_1-\gamma_0}{\gamma_0-a}
\]
takes values in the disc $D(1,1)$. Thus $\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Log} q$ is a continuous argument of $q$. The curve $q$ is closed, because both $\gamma_0$ and $\gamma_1$ are, so this argument has the same value at $\alpha$ and at $\beta$. Hence $n(q,0)=0$. The product rule of the previous paragraph gives $n(\gamma_1,a)=n(q,0)+n(\gamma_0,a)$.

(b) The domain of $H$ is compact and $|H-a|$ is positive and continuous, so $m=\min|H-a|$ is positive. Uniform continuity gives $\delta>0$ such that $|s-s'|<\delta$ implies $|H(s,t)-H(s',t)|<m$ for every $t$. Part~(a), with the two curves $H(s,\cdot)$ and $H(s',\cdot)$, says their winding numbers about $a$ agree. The map $s\mapsto n(H(s,\cdot),a)$ is therefore locally constant on the interval $[0,1]$, and a locally constant function on that interval is constant.

(c) Suppose $|a'-a|<d(a,\gamma^*)$. Then $|(\gamma-a')-(\gamma-a)|=|a'-a|<|\gamma-a|$ at every time, and part~(a) yields $n(\gamma,a')=n(\gamma,a)$. The function $a\mapsto n(\gamma,a)$ is locally constant on the complement of the image. A locally constant function is constant on each connected component.

It remains to see the value $0$ far away. Let $|a|>\max|\gamma|$. The dot product of $\gamma(t)-a$ with $a$ is strictly negative:
\[
\operatorname{Re}\bigl((\gamma(t)-a)\overline a\bigr)\le|\gamma(t)|\,|a|-|a|^2<0.
\]
Multiply $\gamma-a$ by the fixed unimodular constant $-\overline a/|a|$. The new curve
\[
\sigma(t)=-\frac{\overline a}{|a|}\bigl(\gamma(t)-a\bigr)
\]
stays in the open right half-plane, and it is closed. On that half-plane, $\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Log}$ is a continuous argument, and a closed curve makes it take the same value at both ends. So $n(\sigma,0)=0$. Multiplication by a fixed nonzero constant adds a constant to the argument and does not change the winding number about $0$. Therefore $n(\gamma,a)=0$. Every point with $|a|$ larger than $\max|\gamma|$ lies in the unbounded component, so the constant on that component is $0$.
\end{proof}

The leash is what later exercises pull on. Exercise~\ref{ex:ftawind} uses it on a large circle to prove the fundamental theorem of algebra.

\begin{theorem}[no retraction; Brouwer]\label{thm:brouwer}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Write $\overline D=\{|z|\le1\}$ and $\partial D=\{|z|=1\}$. There is no continuous map $r:\overline D\to\partial D$ with $r(z)=z$ for every $z\in\partial D$.
\item[(b)] Every continuous map of a closed disc into itself has a fixed point. Every continuous map of a closed square into itself has a fixed point.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) Suppose such an $r$ exists. Define $H(s,t)=r(se^{it})$ for $0\le s\le1$ and $0\le t\le 2\pi$. The values lie on $\partial D$, so they miss $0$. Each curve $t\mapsto H(s,t)$ is closed. At $s=0$ the curve is the constant $r(0)$, whose winding number about $0$ is $0$. At $s=1$ the curve is $e^{it}$, whose winding number is $1$. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(b) says these winding numbers agree. They do not.

(b) First let $f:\overline D\to\overline D$ be continuous, and suppose $f(z)\ne z$ for every $z$ in the closed unit disc. Set $u(z)=z-f(z)$, which never vanishes. From $f(z)$, look along the direction $u$ through $z$, and continue until the ray leaves the unit circle. The exit point is $r(z)=z+\tau(z)\,u(z)$, where $\tau(z)\ge0$ is the larger root of $|z+\tau u|^2=1$. Expanding the square gives the quadratic $|u|^2\tau^2+2\tau\operatorname{Re}(\overline z u)+|z|^2-1=0$, so
\[
\tau(z)=\frac{-\operatorname{Re}(\overline zu)+\sqrt{(\operatorname{Re}\overline zu)^2+|u|^2(1-|z|^2)}}{|u|^2}.
\]
The quantity under the square root is at least $(\operatorname{Re}\overline zu)^2$, so the numerator is nonnegative and $\tau(z)\ge0$. The formula is continuous on the closed disc because $u$ never vanishes. By construction $|r(z)|=1$. If $|z|=1$, then $\operatorname{Re}(\overline z u)=1-\operatorname{Re}(\overline z f(z))\ge0$, since $|\overline z f(z)|\le1$. The product of the two roots of the quadratic is $(|z|^2-1)/|u|^2=0$, and the formula's choice of the larger root is $0$. Thus $\tau(z)=0$ and $r(z)=z$ on the boundary. This $r$ is the retraction forbidden by part~(a).

A closed disc of any centre and radius is the image of $\overline D$ under a similarity $z\mapsto z_0+Rz$. Conjugating by that similarity carries a fixed-point-free self-map of the smaller or larger disc to a fixed-point-free self-map of $\overline D$. The unit-disc case already forbids that.

Now let $S=\{z:\|z\|_\infty\le1\}$ be the filled square with sides parallel to the axes. The rule $\varphi(0)=0$ and $\varphi(z)=(\|z\|_\infty/|z|)z$ for $z\ne0$ is a homeomorphism of $S$ onto $\overline D$. Its inverse sends $w\ne0$ to $(|w|/\|w\|_\infty)w$ and sends $0$ to $0$. Continuity at the origin holds because $|\varphi(z)|=\|z\|_\infty$. If $f:S\to S$ had no fixed point, then $\varphi\circ f\circ\varphi^{-1}$ would be a continuous self-map of $\overline D$ with no fixed point. A general closed square is the image of $S$ under a similarity, and the same conjugation reduces it to $S$.
\end{proof}

The identity map fixes every point of the disc. A constant map fixes its constant value. A rotation about the origin fixes the origin. The theorem promises at least one fixed point, and these maps show three ways the promise is kept. Exercise~\ref{ex:brouwerfails} asks which hypothesis you can drop before the promise fails.

The next lemma, due to Ryuji Maehara (1984), is the step from Brouwer's theorem to Jordan's. A path that crosses a rectangle from left to right, and a path that crosses it from bottom to top, have to meet. The same meeting is why a game of Hex cannot finish in a draw: a left-to-right chain and a bottom-to-top chain would be two such paths.

\begin{lemma}[crossing paths]\label{lem:crossing}
Let $R=[x_0,x_1]\times[y_0,y_1]$. Let $h,v:[-1,1]\to R$ be continuous, and write subscripts for the two coordinates. Suppose $h_1(-1)=x_0$, $h_1(1)=x_1$, $v_2(-1)=y_0$ and $v_2(1)=y_1$. Then $h(s)=v(t)$ for some $s,t\in[-1,1]$. In words: a path in $R$ from the left side to the right side meets every path in $R$ from the bottom side to the top side.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A horizontal segment from the left side to the right side meets a vertical segment from the bottom to the top. The lemma says the segments may bend, as long as they stay in $R$ and keep those endpoints.

Suppose they never meet. Then $N(s,t)=\|h(s)-v(t)\|_\infty$ is positive at every point of the square $[-1,1]^2$. The formula
\[
F(s,t)=\Bigl(\frac{v_1(t)-h_1(s)}{N(s,t)},\frac{h_2(s)-v_2(t)}{N(s,t)}\Bigr)
\]
is continuous. The two coordinates of $h(s)-v(t)$ are $h_1-v_1$ and $h_2-v_2$, so the infinity norm of $(v_1-h_1,\,h_2-v_2)$ equals $N(s,t)$. Hence $\|F(s,t)\|_\infty=1$. The image of $F$ lies on the boundary of the square, and in particular $F$ sends the square into itself. Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}(b) gives a fixed point $(s,t)$, and that point lies on the boundary, so $|s|=1$ or $|t|=1$.

If $s=-1$, then $h_1(s)=x_0$. The first coordinate of $v(t)$ lies between $x_0$ and $x_1$, so $v_1(t)-x_0\ge0$ and $F_1(s,t)\ge0$. A fixed point would need $F_1=s=-1$. If $s=1$, then $h_1(s)=x_1$, so $v_1(t)-x_1\le0$ and $F_1\le0$, which cannot equal $1$. If $t=-1$, then $v_2(t)=y_0$ and $h_2(s)-y_0\ge0$, so $F_2\ge0$, which cannot equal $-1$. If $t=1$, then $v_2(t)=y_1$ and $h_2(s)-y_1\le0$, so $F_2\le0$, which cannot equal $1$. Every boundary case fails. The paths meet.
\end{proof}

\section{The Jordan curve theorem}

A \emph{Jordan curve} is the image $J$ of a closed curve $\gamma:[\alpha,\beta]\to\C$ that is injective on $[\alpha,\beta)$. Equivalently, $J$ is a subset of the plane homeomorphic to a circle. An \emph{arc} is a set that a homeomorphism carries onto the interval $[0,1]$. The curve $J$ is compact, so $\C\setminus J$ is open. Its connected components are open, and Proposition~\ref{prop:openconnected} says each of them is path-connected. Exactly one component is unbounded: the complement of a large closed disc is connected, and it sits inside the complement of $J$. The boundary of each component is contained in $J$. The proof of that last sentence is the first paragraph of the next lemma, applied to a general compact set; we spell it out there for an arc.

\begin{lemma}[arcs do not separate]\label{lem:arc}
If $A\subset\C$ is an arc, then $\C\setminus A$ is connected.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $A$ be an arc, and let $\phi:A\to[0,1]$ be a homeomorphism. An arc is compact, being the continuous image of the compact interval $[0,1]$, so $A$ is closed in the plane. Suppose $\C\setminus A$ is disconnected. One component is unbounded, because the complement of a large disc is connected and misses $A$. Let $W$ be some other component. Then $W$ is bounded.

The boundary of $W$ lies in $A$. If a boundary point $z$ lay outside $A$, it would belong to some component $W'$ of $\C\setminus A$. That component is open and meets $W$, so $W'=W$ and $z\in W$. An open set does not contain its own boundary points. This contradiction forces $\partial W\subset A$.

Tietze's theorem needs a function with values in $[-1,1]$. The map $2\phi-1:A\to[-1,1]$ is continuous, and $A$ is a closed subset of the metric space $\C$. Proposition~\ref{prop:tietze} extends $2\phi-1$ to a continuous $\Psi:\C\to[-1,1]$. Then $\Phi=(\Psi+1)/2$ is a continuous extension of $\phi$ itself, with values in $[0,1]$. The composition $\rho=\phi^{-1}\circ\Phi$ is a continuous map from $\C$ to $A$, and $\rho$ is the identity on $A$.

Choose $z_1\in W$. The set $A\cup\overline W$ is compact, so some closed disc $\Delta=\overline{D}(z_1,R)$ has $A\cup\overline W$ in its interior. On that disc, paste the retraction to the identity:
\[
g(z)=\rho(z)\ \text{ for }z\in\overline W,\qquad g(z)=z\ \text{ for }z\in\Delta\setminus W.
\]
The two closed sets $\overline W$ and $\Delta\setminus W$ cover $\Delta$. They meet in $\partial W$, which is contained in $A$, and on $A$ one has $\rho(z)=z$. The two formulas agree on the overlap, so $g$ is well defined.

The map $g$ is continuous. Let $z_n\to z$ in $\Delta$. If $z$ has a neighbourhood that lies in just one of the two closed pieces, then $z_n$ eventually lies in that piece, where $g$ agrees with $\rho$ or with the identity. If $z$ lies on the overlap $\partial W$, then $g(z)=z$. Every term of the sequence lies in $\overline W$ or in $\Delta\setminus W$. Terms in $\overline W$ satisfy $\rho(z_n)\to\rho(z)=z$, and terms in $\Delta\setminus W$ satisfy $z_n\to z$. Either way the image tends to $z$, so $g(z_n)\to g(z)$.

The point $z_1$ is not a value of $g$. On $\overline W$ the values lie in $A$, and $A$ misses $W$. On $\Delta\setminus W$ the map $g$ is the identity, and $z_1\in W$. Let
\[
\pi(z)=z_1+R\frac{z-z_1}{|z-z_1|}
\]
be the radial projection from $z_1$ onto the circle $\partial\Delta$. Then $\pi\circ g$ is defined on all of $\Delta$. It is continuous, it sends $\Delta$ onto $\partial\Delta$, and it equals the identity on $\partial\Delta$, because $\partial\Delta$ lies in $\Delta\setminus W$ and $|z-z_1|=R$ there. The similarity $z\mapsto(z-z_1)/R$ carries this retraction onto a retraction of the closed unit disc. That contradicts Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}(a).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Jordan curve theorem]\label{thm:jordan}
Suppose $J$ is a Jordan curve. Then $\C\setminus J$ has exactly two connected components, one bounded and one unbounded, and $J$ is the boundary of each.
\end{theorem}

For the unit circle, the bounded component is the open unit disc and the unbounded component is the outside of the closed disc. The circle is the boundary of both. The theorem says an arbitrary Jordan curve cuts the plane in the same way, even when it has no tangent. The bounded component is called the \emph{inside} of $J$, and the unbounded component the \emph{outside}.

The proof follows Maehara. Out of $J$ and three pieces of one vertical segment, one builds a path that crosses a rectangle from top to bottom. Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing} is then applied three times. A path from the left side to the right side that tried to avoid the top-to-bottom path cannot exist. Figure~\ref{fig:jordan} is the picture to keep in view while reading the four steps.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Setup.} The curve $J$ is compact, so some pair of its points realises the maximum of $|u-v|$ for $u,v\in J$. Call the pair $a$ and $b$. A similarity of the plane multiplies all distances by one positive constant, and it is a homeomorphism, so it preserves Jordan curves, components, boundedness, and boundaries. After a similarity we may assume $a=-1$ and $b=1$. Every other point $z\in J$ then satisfies $|z-a|\le2$ and $|z-b|\le2$.

Write $z=x+iy$. The two disc inequalities expand to $(x+1)^2+y^2\le4$ and $(x-1)^2+y^2\le4$. Adding them yields $x^2+y^2\le3$, so $|y|\le\sqrt3<2$. Thus $J$ lies in the open horizontal strip $|\operatorname{Im} z|<2$, and therefore in the closed rectangle $R=[-1,1]\times[-2,2]$. The top and bottom sides of $R$ miss $J$. On the left side, $\operatorname{Re} z=-1$. If such a point also lay in both discs, then $|z-b|^2=4+y^2\le4$, so $y=0$ and $z=a$. The right side meets the lens only at $b$. Hence $J$ meets $\partial R$ only at $a$ and at $b$.

The points $a$ and $b$ split $J$ into two arcs from $a$ to $b$, and those arcs meet only at $a$ and $b$. Write $n=2i$ for the midpoint of the top side of $R$, and $s=-2i$ for the midpoint of the bottom side. Each of the two arcs runs from the left side of $R$ to the right side, and the segment $[n,s]$ runs from the top to the bottom. Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing} says each arc meets $[n,s]$. In the rest of the proof, higher means a larger imaginary part.

\emph{Step 1: the point $z_0$.} Let $l$ be the highest point of the compact set $J\cap[n,s]$. Exactly one of the two arcs contains $l$, because $l$ is neither $a$ nor $b$. Call that arc $J_n$, and call the other arc $J_s$. Among the points of $J_n$ that lie on $[n,s]$, let $m$ be the lowest.

The segment $[m,s]$ meets $J_s$. If it did not, build a path as follows. Go down $[n,l]$, follow $J_n$ from $l$ to $m$, and go down $[m,s]$. The segment $[n,l]$ meets $J$ only at $l$, and $l$ is not on $J_s$. The subarc of $J_n$ from $l$ to $m$ contains neither $a$ nor $b$, so it misses $J_s$. The whole path lies in $R$, starts on the top side, and ends on the bottom side. It would miss $J_s$, contradicting Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing}. So $J_s\cap[m,s]$ is nonempty.

Let $p$ be the highest point of that intersection, and let $q$ be the lowest point of $J_s\cap[n,s]$. The point $p$ is strictly below $m$: the two arcs meet only at $a$ and $b$, so $m$ itself is not on $J_s$. The open segment $]m,p[$ misses $J_n$, because $m$ was the lowest point of $J_n$ on the vertical segment. It misses $J_s$, because $p$ was the highest point of $J_s$ on $[m,s]$. Let $z_0$ be the midpoint of $]m,p[$, and let $U$ be the component of $\C\setminus J$ that contains $z_0$.

\emph{Step 2: $U$ is bounded.} Suppose $U$ were unbounded. Then some path in $U$ would run from $z_0$, an interior point of $R$, to a point outside $R$. Let $w$ be the first point of $\partial R$ on that path, and let $\delta$ be the part of the path from $z_0$ to $w$. The image of $\delta$ lies in $R$, it misses $J$, and $w$ is neither $a$ nor $b$.

Deleting $a$ and $b$ from the boundary of $R$ leaves two open arcs of that boundary. The upper arc $\partial^+$ is the one that contains $n$. The lower arc $\partial^-$ is the one that contains $s$. Both arcs miss $J$, because $J$ meets $\partial R$ only at $a$ and at $b$.

If $w\in\partial^-$, build a top-to-bottom path in $R$ by going down $[n,l]$, along $J_n$ to $m$, along $[m,z_0]$, along $\delta$ to $w$, and along $\partial^-$ to $s$. The pieces $[n,l]$, the subarc of $J_n$, and $[m,z_0]$ miss $J_s$ for the reasons in Step~1, and $\delta$ and $\partial^-$ miss $J$ entirely. The path misses $J_s$ and contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing}.

If $w\in\partial^+$, go along $\partial^+$ from $n$ to $w$, back along $\delta$ to $z_0$, along $[z_0,p]$, along the subarc of $J_s$ from $p$ to $q$, and down $[q,s]$. This path misses $J_n$. The pieces $\partial^+$ and $\delta$ miss $J$. The segment $[z_0,p]$ meets $J$ at most at $p$, which lies on $J_s$. The subarc from $p$ to $q$ contains neither $a$ nor $b$. The segment $[q,s]$ lies strictly below $m$, because $q$ is at most as high as $p$ and $p$ is strictly below $m$, while $J_n$ meets the vertical segment only at height at least $\operatorname{Im} m$. Again Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing} is contradicted.

Both cases fail, so $U$ is bounded. The complement $\C\setminus J$ has at least two components.

\emph{Step 3: $J$ is the boundary of every component.} Let $W$ be any component. The first paragraph of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:arc} shows $\partial W\subset J$; the same sentences apply with $J$ in place of the arc. Suppose $\partial W\ne J$, and pick $x\in J\setminus\partial W$. The set $\partial W$ is closed, so some small open subarc of $J$ about $x$ misses $\partial W$. Delete that open subarc. What remains is homeomorphic to a closed interval: on the circle, the complement of an open arc is a closed arc. Call the remainder $A$. Then $\partial W\subset A$, and $A$ is an arc.

Lemma~\ref{lem:arc} says $\C\setminus A$ is connected. It is open, because $A$ is closed. Proposition~\ref{prop:openconnected} upgrades connectedness to path-connectedness. Take $w\in W$ and take $w'$ in some other component, which Step~2 supplies, and take a path $c:[0,1]\to\C\setminus A$ from $w$ to $w'$. Let
\[
t^*=\sup\{t:c([0,t])\subset W\}.
\]
The set inside the supremum contains $0$, because $c(0)=w\in W$, and it does not contain points arbitrarily close to $1$, because $w'\notin W$. So $0\le t^*<1$. The point $c(t^*)$ lies in the closure of $W$. It does not lie in $W$: if it did, openness of $W$ and continuity of $c$ would keep the path inside $W$ on a slightly larger interval. Therefore $c(t^*)\in\partial W\subset A$. The path was built to miss $A$. This contradiction forces $\partial W=J$.

\emph{Step 4: $U$ is the only bounded component.} Let $\Gamma$ be the path that goes down $[n,l]$, along $J_n$ from $l$ to $m$, along $[m,p]$, along $J_s$ from $p$ to $q$, and down $[q,s]$. Off the set $J$, the points of $\Gamma$ lie on $[n,l[$, on $]m,p[$, and on $]q,s]$. The middle piece lies in $U$.

The half-open segment $[n,l[$ is connected, misses $J$, and contains $n$. The upward vertical ray from $n$ misses $J$, because $|\operatorname{Im} z|\le\sqrt3$ on $J$ while the ray has imaginary part at least $2$, and $n$ itself is not on $J$. That ray shows $n$ lies in the unbounded component. A connected subset of the complement that meets the unbounded component is contained in it, so $[n,l[$ lies in the unbounded component. The same reason puts $]q,s]$ there.

Suppose $W$ were a bounded component different from $U$. Then $\Gamma$ misses $W$: its points off $J$ lie in $U$ or in the unbounded component, and its other points lie on $J$. Neither $a$ nor $b$ lies on $\Gamma$. The vertical pieces of $\Gamma$ have real part $0$, while $a$ and $b$ have real parts $\pm1$, and the subarcs of $\Gamma$ that run along $J_n$ and $J_s$ were chosen between $l$ and $m$ and between $p$ and $q$, so they contain neither endpoint. Some discs $D(a,\varepsilon)$ and $D(b,\varepsilon)$ therefore miss $\Gamma$. Step~3 puts $a$ and $b$ on $\partial W$, so the discs meet $W$. Pick $a'$ in the first meeting and $b'$ in the second.

The complement $\C\setminus R$ is path-connected. Two points outside $R$ can each walk radially out to a large circle, travel along that circle, and the path stays off $R$. The complement is unbounded, and it misses $J$ because $J\subset R$. It therefore lies in the unbounded component. A bounded component such as $W$ is contained in $R$.

Form a path from $a$ to $b$ by travelling along $[a,a']$, then along a path in $W$ from $a'$ to $b'$, then along $[b',b]$. The rectangle $R$ is convex, so the two end segments lie in $R$. They also lie in the discs that miss $\Gamma$. The middle lies in $W$ and misses $\Gamma$. The whole path lies in $R$, runs from the left side to the right side, and misses $\Gamma$. But $\Gamma$ runs from the top of $R$ to the bottom. Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing} says the two paths meet. They do not. So no such $W$ exists.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
Where did the argument use that $J$ does not cross itself? Draw a figure eight and follow the four steps. Which step stops working? The winding number equals $1$ on the inside of a circle and $0$ on the outside. Why is that sentence not already a proof for a wild Jordan curve?
\end{think}

\begin{remark}
The inside of a Jordan curve can still be a strange set. Osgood's curve of 1903 is a Jordan curve that carries positive area, in the sense the next chapter will define, so area can sit on the boundary of the inside. In 1910 Brouwer constructed three disjoint open sets in the plane with one and the same boundary. That picture is now called the Lakes of Wada. Jordan's theorem spends the hypothesis that the separating set is a curve. In 1906 Schoenflies stated a sharper theorem: some homeomorphism of the whole plane carries $J$ onto the unit circle, and the inside is therefore homeomorphic to an open disc. The account we point to in the sources is Thomassen's, which gives a complete argument. We do not prove the sharper statement here.

The conformal form of the same sharpening is Carath\'eodory's theorem. It needs the Riemann mapping theorem, which Volume~9 proves, together with an analysis of the boundary that is not in this volume. The proofs above do not use it. The sentence is a preview.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
The statement is one a drawing makes obvious, and the proof is not obvious at all. For a polygon, the work sits at the corners: one parity check when the ray hits a vertex, and one picture of the two sectors there. For a general curve the corners are gone. What replaces them is an integer attached to each point off the curve. A continuous motion cannot change an integer without a jump, so the integer is constant on each piece of the complement. The argument never measures a length, never asks for a tangent, and never uses an area. An inside and an outside are what continuity can force by itself.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A simple closed polygon cuts the plane into exactly two pieces, and the parity of the hits of a ray tells the pieces apart (Theorem~\ref{thm:jordanpoly}). A continuous closed curve has a winding number about each point off its image. That integer is constant on each component of the complement, it vanishes on the unbounded component, and a continuous deformation that never hits the point leaves it unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}). From the winding number we obtained Brouwer's fixed-point theorem for the disc and the square (Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}), and then the forced meeting of a left-to-right path with a bottom-to-top path in a rectangle (Lemma~\ref{lem:crossing}). The meeting, applied to paths cut out of the curve, is Jordan's theorem: the complement of a Jordan curve has two components, and the curve is the boundary of both (Theorem~\ref{thm:jordan}).

This chapter ends the first part of the volume. The objects so far have been points and the open sets around them: limits, continuity, compactness, connectedness, and Baire's typical point. Size has not been defined. The Cantor set has been described as having length zero, and Osgood's curve as having positive area, and both phrases were used before either word had a definition. Which sets have a length? Chapter~\ref{ch:measures} starts from that question. The first fact there is that the class of sets which have a length is not the class of all sets.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:ftawind}
\emph{The fundamental theorem of algebra, by winding.} Let $p(z)=z^n+c_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\dots+c_0$ with $n\ge1$, and let $\gamma_R(t)=Re^{it}$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. (a) Show that $n(p\circ\gamma_R,0)=n$ whenever $R>1+\sum_j|c_j|$.
(b) If $p$ had no root, what would the homotopy $H(s,t)=p(sRe^{it})$ say about that integer? Conclude that $p$ has a root.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:windcompute}
\emph{Three winding numbers.} On the interval $0\le t\le2\pi$, set $\gamma_1(t)=2e^{2it}+e^{it}$ and $\gamma_2(t)=e^{it}+2e^{-3it}$. Find $n(\gamma_1,0)$, $n(\gamma_2,0)$ and $n(\gamma_1,5)$.
Sketch $\gamma_1$. As a check on the first value, count the crossings of the positive real axis, with a sign for the direction of each crossing.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:brouwerfails}
\emph{Brouwer needs the whole closed disc.} Give a continuous map with no fixed point (a) from the open unit disc into itself, (b) from the closed annulus
$\{1\le|z|\le2\}$ into itself, (c) from $\R$ into itself. For each map, name the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}(b) that has been dropped.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:miranda}
\emph{The Poincar\'e--Miranda theorem in the square.} Let $Q=[-1,1]^2$, and let $f,g:Q\to\R$ be continuous. Suppose $f\le0$ on the left side $x=-1$ and $f\ge0$ on the right side $x=1$. Suppose $g\le0$ on the bottom side $y=-1$ and $g\ge0$ on the top side $y=1$. Show that some point of $Q$ is a common root of $f$ and $g$. For a single continuous function on an interval, the same idea is the intermediate value theorem.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v7c06a}
\emph{The unit circle, inside and outside.} Let $\gamma(t)=e^{it}$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. Using a continuous argument, compute $n(\gamma,0)$. Then compute $n(\gamma,3)$, and name the part of Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops} that gives the second value with no further work.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v7c06b}
\emph{A triangle, counted by an upward ray.} Let $P$ be the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$ and $(1,3)$. The bottom edge is horizontal, so a horizontal ray is one of the directions the parity section refuses. Use the upward direction $d=(0,1)$ instead. For the points $(2,1)$ and $(2,-1)$, count the hits of the upward ray with the edges. Which count is odd, and which point sits inside the triangle?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c06c}
\emph{A leash that holds, and a leash that does not.} Let $\gamma_0(t)=e^{it}$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. (a) Let $\gamma_1(t)=e^{it}+\frac12 e^{2it}$. Check the leash inequality of Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(a) about the point $0$, and compute $n(\gamma_1,0)$. (b) Let $\gamma_1$ be the constant curve $2$. Show that the leash inequality fails for at least one $t$, and that the two winding numbers about $0$ are different.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c06d}
\emph{Even outside, odd inside.} Let $P$ be a simple polygon, and let $\pi$ be the parity built in the first half of the polygonal argument, extended to every point of $\R^2\setminus P$. Show that $\pi=0$ on the unbounded component and $\pi=1$ on the bounded component.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:ftawind}
\idea On a large circle the polynomial is a small push away from $z^n$, so it winds the same number of times. If it never hits $0$, that circle can be shrunk to a point, and the winding number of a point is $0$.
\writeup (a) Let $|z|=R$ with $R>1+\sum_j|c_j|$. Then $R>1$ and $R>\sum_j|c_j|$. Hence
\[
|p(z)-z^n|\le\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|c_j|R^j\le R^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|c_j|<R^{n-1}\cdot R=R^n=|z^n|.
\]
The inequality $R^j\le R^{n-1}$ for $j\le n-1$ uses $R>1$. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(a) gives $n(p\circ\gamma_R,0)=n(\gamma_R^{\,n},0)$. The curve $\gamma_R(t)^n=R^ne^{int}$ has continuous argument $nt$, which increases by $2\pi n$. The winding number is $n$.

(b) Suppose $p$ has no root. Then $p(0)\ne0$, and $H(s,t)=p(sRe^{it})$ never vanishes. The map $H$ is a continuous family of closed curves in $\C\setminus\{0\}$, running from the constant curve $p(0)$ to $p\circ\gamma_R$. A constant curve has winding number $0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(b) forces $0=n$. That is false for $n\ge1$. So $p$ has a root.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:windcompute}
\idea Compare each curve with its longer summand. The winding number of that summand is read off from the argument in the exponent. Do not try to invent a continuous argument for the whole sum by hand.
\writeup For $\gamma_1$, take $\gamma_0(t)=2e^{2it}$. Then $|\gamma_1(t)-\gamma_0(t)|=1<2=|\gamma_0(t)|$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(a) gives $n(\gamma_1,0)=n(\gamma_0,0)$. The argument $2t$ increases by $4\pi$, and the winding number is $2$.

For $\gamma_2$, take $\gamma_0(t)=2e^{-3it}$. Again the difference has length $1$, which is less than $2$. The argument $-3t$ decreases by $6\pi$, so $n(\gamma_2,0)=-3$.

For the point $5$, note that $|\gamma_1(t)|\le3$ for every $t$. The image of $\gamma_1$ lies in the closed disc of radius $3$, and $5$ lies outside that disc, in the unbounded component of the complement. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(c) gives $n(\gamma_1,5)=0$.

The check on the positive axis uses $\operatorname{Im}\gamma_1(t)=2\sin 2t+\sin t=\sin t\,(4\cos t+1)$. This vanishes at $t=0$, at $t=\pi$, and where $\cos t=-\frac14$. At $t=0$ the curve is at $3$. At $t=\pi$ it is at $2-1=1$. Where $\cos t=-\frac14$,
\[
\operatorname{Re}\gamma_1(t)=2\cos 2t+\cos t=2\Bigl(2\cdot\frac1{16}-1\Bigr)-\frac14=-2,
\]
so those hits lie on the negative axis and do not count. The derivative of the imaginary part is $4\cos 2t+\cos t$. At $t=0$ it equals $4+1=5>0$. At $t=\pi$ it equals $4\cos 2\pi+\cos\pi=4-1=3>0$. Both crossings of the positive axis go upward. Two upward crossings contribute $+1$ each, and the total is $2$.

The same curve is a shifted lima\c con with an inner loop. Winding number $2$ at the origin means the origin sits in that inner loop.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:brouwerfails}
\idea A fixed point can slip out through a missing boundary point, through a hole, or out to infinity. Each example uses one exit.
\writeup (a) The map $f(z)=\frac{z+1}2$ sends the open unit disc into itself, because $|f(z)|\le\frac{|z|+1}2<1$. A fixed point would solve $z=\frac{z+1}2$, hence $z=1$, and $1$ is not in the open disc. The set is not closed. The fixed point of the same formula on the closed disc sits on the boundary that was removed.

(b) The map $f(z)=-z$ preserves modulus, so it sends the annulus onto itself. The equation $-z=z$ holds only at $z=0$, which lies in the hole. The annulus is closed and bounded, and it is not a disc. Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer} says nothing about it.

(c) The map $f(x)=x+1$ has no fixed point on $\R$. The domain is closed in itself, and it is not bounded.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:miranda}
\idea Copy the pattern of Brouwer's theorem. If $(f,g)$ has no common root, normalise the pair so that it lands on the boundary of the square, and reverse the direction. The fixed point that Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}(b) supplies would have to lie on the boundary, and each side forbids it.
\writeup Suppose $(f(p),g(p))\ne(0,0)$ for every $p\in Q$. Then $m(p)=\max(|f(p)|,|g(p)|)$ is continuous and positive, and
\[
r(p)=-\frac{(f(p),g(p))}{m(p)}
\]
is a continuous map from $Q$ into $Q$. One coordinate of $r(p)$ always has absolute value $1$, because $m(p)$ is the larger of $|f(p)|$ and $|g(p)|$. So $r(p)$ lies on the boundary of the square. Theorem~\ref{thm:brouwer}(b) supplies a point $p=(x,y)$ with $r(p)=p$, and this $p$ lies on the boundary. Check the four sides.

If $x=1$, then $f(p)\ge0$, so the first coordinate of $r(p)$ equals $-f(p)/m(p)\le0$, and it cannot equal $1$. If $x=-1$, then $f(p)\le0$, so the first coordinate of $r(p)$ is at least $0$, and it cannot equal $-1$. If $y=1$, then $g(p)\ge0$, and the second coordinate of $r(p)$ is at most $0$, so it cannot equal $1$. If $y=-1$, then $g(p)\le0$, and the second coordinate of $r(p)$ is at least $0$, so it cannot equal $-1$. Every side is impossible. The pair $(f,g)$ has a common root in $Q$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c06a}
\idea The argument of a point on the unit circle is the angle you used to write the point. Far from the origin, Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(c) already knows the winding number.
\writeup The function $\theta(t)=t$ is continuous, and $e^{it}=|e^{it}|\,e^{i\theta(t)}$. It increases by $2\pi$ on $[0,2\pi]$, so $n(\gamma,0)=1$.

The image of $\gamma$ is the unit circle, so $|\gamma(t)|=1<3$. The point $3$ lies outside the closed disc of radius $1$, in the unbounded component of $\C\setminus\gamma^*$. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(c) gives $n(\gamma,3)=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c06b}
\idea Draw the vertical line $x=2$. It is legal because no edge of this triangle is vertical, and it does not pass through two vertices. Count only the hits with $y$ at least as large as the starting height.
\writeup The edge directions are $(1,0)$, $(-3,3)$ and $(-1,-3)$. None is vertical, so $d=(0,1)$ is not parallel to an edge or to a line through two vertices.

The upward ray from $(2,1)$ is the set of points $(2,y)$ with $y\ge1$. The bottom edge has height $0$, so the ray misses it. The edge from $(1,3)$ to $(0,0)$ is parametrised by $(1-s,3-3s)$ for $s\in[0,1]$, and $1-s=2$ forces $s=-1$, which is off the segment. The edge from $(4,0)$ to $(1,3)$ is parametrised by $(4-3t,3t)$ for $t\in[0,1]$. Setting the real part equal to $2$ gives $t=\frac23$, and the height is $2$, which is at least $1$. That hit is the single intersection, at $(2,2)$. The count is $1$, which is odd.

The upward ray from $(2,-1)$ is $(2,y)$ with $y\ge-1$. It meets the bottom edge at $(2,0)$, an interior point of that edge. It meets the slanted edge again at $(2,2)$. The third edge still contributes nothing. The count is $2$, which is even.

The weights $\frac14$, $\frac{5}{12}$ and $\frac13$ are positive and sum to $1$, and
\[
\frac14(0,0)+\frac{5}{12}(4,0)+\frac13(1,3)=(2,1).
\]
So $(2,1)$ lies in the triangle. The triangle lies in the half-plane $y\ge0$, and $(2,-1)$ does not. The odd count is the inside point. The even count is the outside point.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c06c}
\idea Part~(a) is the unit circle with a push of length $\frac12$. The leash is shorter than the distance from the circle to the origin, so the winding number stays $1$. Part~(b) is a dog that stands still at $2$ while the hand walks around the unit circle. At $t=0$ the leash is exactly as long as the distance to the origin, and the strict inequality fails.
\writeup (a) Here $|\gamma_1(t)-\gamma_0(t)|=\frac12<1=|\gamma_0(t)-0|$ for every $t$. Proposition~\ref{prop:windingprops}(a) gives $n(\gamma_1,0)=n(\gamma_0,0)$. The argument of $e^{it}$ increases by $2\pi$, so both winding numbers equal $1$.

(b) Now $\gamma_1(t)=2$. Then $|\gamma_1(t)-\gamma_0(t)|=|2-e^{it}|$, which equals $1$ at $t=0$. The leash asks for a strict inequality against $|\gamma_0(t)|=1$ at every time, and $1<1$ is false. The constant curve has constant argument, so $n(\gamma_1,0)=0$. The unit circle still has $n(\gamma_0,0)=1$. The two winding numbers differ.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c06d}
\idea The parity is constant on each component, the two sides of one edge realise both parities, and a point far downstream of $d$ has count $0$. Two components, and the unbounded one is the side with count $0$, so the bounded one has count $1$.
\writeup The direction $d$ was chosen to be a unit vector. Let $M$ be larger than $u\cdot d$ for every point $u$ of $P$. Any point $x$ with $x\cdot d>M$ has forward ray missing $P$: for $t\ge0$,
\[
(x+td)\cdot d=x\cdot d+t\,|d|^2=x\cdot d+t>M,
\]
while every point of $P$ has dot product with $d$ strictly less than $M$. The bad lines are $n$ lines, so some such $x$ lies off all of them. For that $x$ one has $c(x)=0$, hence $\pi(x)=0$. Walking farther in the direction $d$ stays in the complement and goes to infinity, so $x$ lies in the unbounded component. The function $\pi$ is constant on components, and the constant on the unbounded component is $0$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:jordanpoly} says there are exactly two components. In the first half of that proof, the two sides of one edge are points of different parity, so they lie in different components. The side that lies in the unbounded component has $\pi=0$. The other side lies in the remaining component, the bounded one, and its parity is $1$. Constancy of $\pi$ on that component finishes the proof.
\end{solution}
