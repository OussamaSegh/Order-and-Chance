\chapter{Measures on Metric Spaces}\label{ch:metricmeasures}

\begin{center}\itshape
How long is the coast of Britain? Set a divider to a kilometre and walk the shore. Set it to a metre, then to a centimetre.
Each time the measured length grows. A coastline can sit strictly between a curve and a surface.
\end{center}

Lewis Fry Richardson spent his working life predicting the weather and wars by mathematics. He died in 1953. In 1961 a table he had
left was published: measured lengths of borders and coastlines, read off maps of finer and finer scale. The length $L$ grew like a
power of the inverse ruler, $L\approx C\varepsilon^{1-D}$. For the west coast of Britain the exponent was about $1.25$. Little notice was
taken until Beno\^it Mandelbrot's paper ``How long is the coast of Britain?'' appeared in \emph{Science} in 1967. He read $D$ as a
dimension, strictly between $1$ and $2$. Felix Hausdorff had built the matching measure in 1918. It makes sense in any metric space, and
the exponent $s$ may be any real number at least $0$.

Distances give a Borel measure extra structure, and this chapter uses that structure four times. Ulam's theorem puts almost all the mass
of a finite measure on a compact set. Riesz's theorem of 1909 says that a positive linear average of continuous functions is integration
against a measure. Weak convergence is the convergence of laws behind the central limit theorem, with a portmanteau of equivalent tests and
with Prokhorov's compactness theorem of 1956. Kolmogorov's extension theorem of 1933 builds one random sequence out of consistent
finite-dimensional pieces.

\section{Regularity and tightness}

Throughout, $X$ is a metric space, and the Borel sets are those of its metric topology. Lemma~\ref{lem:outerreg} already gives regularity
for a finite Borel measure $\mu$: for every Borel set $A$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there are a closed set $C$ and an open set $U$ with
$C\subseteq A\subseteq U$ and $\mu(U\setminus C)<\varepsilon$. Compact sets are the ones we can hold. A metric space is \emph{Polish} when it is
complete and separable. The spaces $\R^d$ and $C([0,1])$ are Polish, and so is $\R^\N$ with a product metric, and so is every closed
subset of these.

\begin{theorem}[Ulam]\label{thm:ulam}
Let $\mu$ be a finite Borel measure on a Polish space $X$. Then $\mu$ is \emph{tight}: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a compact set $K$ with
$\mu(X\setminus K)<\varepsilon$. Consequently, for every Borel set $A$,
\[\mu(A)=\sup\{\mu(K):K\subseteq A\text{ compact}\}.\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The job is to trap all but $\varepsilon$ of the mass in a closed totally bounded set. Let $(x_i)$ be a dense sequence. For each $k\ge 1$ the closed
balls $\overline B(x_i,\frac1k)$ cover $X$. Continuity from below gives an index $n_k$ such that
\[\mu\Bigl(X\setminus\bigcup_{i\le n_k}\overline B\bigl(x_i,\tfrac1k\bigr)\Bigr)<\varepsilon 2^{-k}.\]
Set $K=\bigcap_k\bigcup_{i\le n_k}\overline B(x_i,\frac1k)$. The set $K$ is closed in a complete space, so $K$ is complete. It is totally bounded,
because the $n_k$ balls of radius $\frac1k$ cover it. Theorem~\ref{thm:compactmetric} says that a complete totally bounded metric space is
compact, so $K$ is compact. The complement $X\setminus K$ is covered by the sets we discarded, and those have total mass less than
$\varepsilon\sum_k 2^{-k}=\varepsilon$. Thus $\mu(X\setminus K)<\varepsilon$.

For the second claim, start from regularity. Choose a closed set $C\subseteq A$ with $\mu(A\setminus C)<\varepsilon$. The intersection $C\cap K$ is a
closed subset of the compact set $K$, hence compact, and it sits inside $A$. Then
\[\mu\bigl(A\setminus(C\cap K)\bigr)\le\mu(A\setminus C)+\mu(X\setminus K)<2\varepsilon.\]
The supremum of $\mu$ over compact subsets of $A$ is therefore within $2\varepsilon$ of $\mu(A)$. Let $\varepsilon$ tend to $0$.
\end{proof}

On $[0,1]$, Lebesgue measure is already carried by the compact set $[0,1]$. On $\R$, a law with light tails is carried by a long closed
interval, and every closed bounded interval is compact. The same sentence applies to a law on $C([0,1])$: up to $\varepsilon$, the mass sits on a
compact set of functions, and Theorem~\ref{thm:ascoli} says that such a set is closed, pointwise bounded, and equicontinuous. Volume~12 uses
this when it builds Brownian motion. The construction is not used here.

\section{The Riesz representation theorem}

Let $K$ be a compact metric space and let $\mu$ be a finite Borel measure on $K$. The formula $\Lambda f=\int f\,d\mu$ defines a linear form
on $C(K)$. It is \emph{positive}: $f\ge 0$ forces $\Lambda f\ge 0$. Riesz's theorem says that every positive linear form arises in this way.
On the two-point space $\{0,1\}$, a positive form is just a pair of nonnegative weights, and the measure is the matching combination of
point masses. Exercise~\ref{ex:v7c12a} writes that case out. The general proof needs a partition of unity.

The \emph{support} of a continuous $f$ is the closure of $\{f\ne 0\}$. The shorthand $f\prec U$ means that $f\in C(K)$, that $0\le f\le 1$, and
that the support of $f$ lies in the open set $U$.

\begin{lemma}[partitions of unity]\label{lem:pou}
Let $C\subseteq K$ be closed, and let $C\subseteq V_1\cup\cdots\cup V_n$ with each $V_i$ open. Then there exist functions $h_i\prec V_i$ such that
$h_1+\cdots+h_n=1$ on $C$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Each point $x\in C$ has some index $i(x)$ and some radius $r_x>0$ with $\overline B(x,2r_x)\subseteq V_{i(x)}$. The open balls $B(x,r_x)$ cover the
compact set $C$, so finitely many of them suffice. Let $W_i$ be the union of those chosen balls that carry the index $i$. Then
$\overline{W_i}$ is compact and contained in $V_i$. If $V_i\ne K$, the distance $\delta_i=d(\overline{W_i},K\setminus V_i)$ is positive. The open
neighbourhood $U_i=\{y:d(y,\overline{W_i})<\delta_i/2\}$ then satisfies $\overline{U_i}\subseteq V_i$. If $V_i=K$, take $U_i=K$ and, below, take the
function $g_i$ to be the constant $1$.

On the remaining sets, Proposition~\ref{prop:urysohn} supplies the function
\[g_i(y)=\frac{d(y,K\setminus U_i)}{d(y,K\setminus U_i)+d(y,\overline{W_i})}.\]
The denominator is positive. On $\overline{W_i}$ the second distance vanishes and the first does not, so $g_i=1$ there. Also $0\le g_i\le 1$,
and $g_i$ vanishes off $U_i$, so $g_i\prec V_i$.

Set $h_1=g_1$ and $h_j=(1-g_1)\cdots(1-g_{j-1})g_j$ for $j\ge 2$. Each factor $g_j$ forces $h_j\prec V_j$. The partial sums telescope:
\[h_1+\cdots+h_n=1-(1-g_1)\cdots(1-g_n).\]
On $C\subseteq\bigcup_i\overline{W_i}$ some $g_i$ equals $1$, so the product on the right vanishes and the sum equals $1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Riesz representation]\label{thm:riesz}
Let $K$ be a compact metric space, and let $\Lambda$ be a positive linear form on $C(K,\R)$. There is a unique finite Borel measure $\mu$ on
$K$ such that $\Lambda f=\int f\,d\mu$ for every $f\in C(K)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Build an outer measure from $\Lambda$.} For $U$ open, set $\mu(U)=\sup\{\Lambda f:f\prec U\}$. For an arbitrary $A\subseteq K$, set
$\mu^*(A)=\inf\{\mu(U):U\supseteq A\text{ open}\}$. Positivity of $\Lambda$ makes $\mu$ monotone on open sets, so $\mu^*$ agrees with $\mu$ on
open sets.

\emph{Check that $\mu^*$ is an outer measure.} Monotonicity is immediate from the definition, and $\mu^*(\varnothing)=0$. The countable
subadditivity reduces to open sets, after which the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:extension} applies. So let $U_i$ be open and let
$f\prec U=\bigcup_i U_i$. The support of $f$ is compact, hence it lies in a finite union $U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_n$. Lemma~\ref{lem:pou} gives
$h_i\prec U_i$ with $\sum_{i\le n}h_i=1$ on that support. Then $f=\sum_i h_if$ and $h_if\prec U_i$, so
\[\Lambda f=\sum_i\Lambda(h_if)\le\sum_i\mu(U_i).\]
Taking the supremum over $f\prec U$ yields $\mu(U)\le\sum_i\mu(U_i)$.

\emph{Show that open sets are measurable.} Carath\'eodory's test asks for $\mu^*(A)\ge\mu^*(A\cap U)+\mu^*(A\setminus U)$. By the definition of
$\mu^*$ it is enough to test this when $A=V$ is open. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $f\prec V\cap U$ with $\Lambda f>\mu(V\cap U)-\varepsilon$. The set
$V\setminus\operatorname{supp}f$ is open and contains $V\setminus U$. Choose $g\prec V\setminus\operatorname{supp}f$ with
\[\Lambda g>\mu(V\setminus\operatorname{supp}f)-\varepsilon\ge\mu^*(V\setminus U)-\varepsilon.\]
The sum $f+g$ satisfies $f+g\prec V$, so
\[\mu(V)\ge\Lambda f+\Lambda g\ge\mu(V\cap U)+\mu^*(V\setminus U)-2\varepsilon.\]
Thus open sets are $\mu^*$-measurable. Theorem~\ref{thm:caratheodory} says that $\mu^*$ restricts to a Borel measure $\mu$. Also
$\mu(K)=\Lambda 1$, because $f\prec K$ means only that $0\le f\le 1$, and the constant $1$ is admissible.

\emph{Match $\Lambda$ with integration.} It is enough to prove $\Lambda f\le\int f\,d\mu$ for every real $f$, and then to apply the same bound
to $-f$. Fix $f$, and choose $a<b$ with $f(K)\subseteq\,]a,b[$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and cut $]a,b[$ by points $y_0<a<y_1<\cdots<y_n=b$ with
$y_i-y_{i-1}<\varepsilon$. The Borel sets $E_i=\{y_{i-1}<f\le y_i\}$ are disjoint and cover $K$. Choose open sets $V_i\supseteq E_i$ with
$\mu(V_i)<\mu(E_i)+\varepsilon/n$ and with $f<y_i+\varepsilon$ throughout $V_i$; intersecting with $\{f<y_i+\varepsilon\}$ arranges the second demand.
Lemma~\ref{lem:pou} gives $h_i\prec V_i$ with $\sum_i h_i=1$ on $K$. Then $\Lambda h_i\le\mu(V_i)$ and $\sum_i\Lambda h_i=\Lambda 1=\mu(K)$. Also
$h_if\le(y_i+\varepsilon)h_i$.

Write $c=|a|$. The shift by $c$ makes every coefficient $c+y_i+\varepsilon$ nonnegative: each $y_i$ is greater than $a$, and $a\ge -c$. A
nonnegative coefficient lets us replace $\Lambda h_i$ by the larger number $\mu(E_i)+\varepsilon/n$. Expanding, the terms $c\sum_i\mu(E_i)$ cancel,
and what remains is $\sum_i(y_i+\varepsilon)\mu(E_i)$ plus a remainder at most $\varepsilon(c+b+\varepsilon)$, because $y_i\le b$ and there are $n$ summands.
Split $y_i+\varepsilon=(y_i-\varepsilon)+2\varepsilon$. The piece $2\varepsilon\sum_i\mu(E_i)$ equals $2\varepsilon\mu(K)$. On $E_i$ one has $f>y_{i-1}>y_i-\varepsilon$, so
$\sum_i(y_i-\varepsilon)\mu(E_i)\le\int f\,d\mu$. Collecting these comparisons gives
\[\begin{aligned}\Lambda f=\sum_i\Lambda(h_if)&\le\sum_i(c+y_i+\varepsilon)\Lambda h_i-c\,\mu(K)\eqbr\le\sum_i(c+y_i+\varepsilon)\Bigl(\mu(E_i)+\frac\varepsilon n\Bigr)-c\sum_i\mu(E_i)\\&\le\sum_i(y_i-\varepsilon)\mu(E_i)+2\varepsilon\mu(K)+\varepsilon(c+b+\varepsilon)\eqbr\le\int f\,d\mu+\varepsilon\bigl(2\mu(K)+c+b+\varepsilon\bigr),\end{aligned}\]
using $c+y_i+\varepsilon\ge 0$ and $y_i-\varepsilon\le f$ on $E_i$. Let $\varepsilon\to 0$.

\emph{Uniqueness.} Suppose $\int f\,d\mu=\int f\,d\nu$ for every $f\in C(K)$. For $C$ closed, the functions
$f_n(x)=\max(0,1-n\,d(x,C))$ decrease pointwise to $\mathbf 1_C$, and $0\le f_n\le 1$. Dominated convergence (Theorem~\ref{thm:dct6}) gives
$\mu(C)=\lim\int f_n\,d\mu=\lim\int f_n\,d\nu=\nu(C)$. Closed sets form a $\pi$-system, they generate the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, and
$\mu(K)=\nu(K)$ is finite, so Theorem~\ref{thm:uniq6} yields $\mu=\nu$.
\end{proof}

In 1909 Riesz proved the case $K=[0,1]$. There, every positive linear form on $C([0,1])$ is a Stieltjes integral $\int f\,dF$ with $F$
non-decreasing. One may therefore start from the integral, as a linear form on continuous functions, and recover the measure afterwards.
Volume~8 extends the statement to signed forms, and identifies the dual of $C(K)$ with the finite signed measures. No argument in this
volume uses that extension.

\section{Weak convergence}

Let $\mu_n$ and $\mu$ be probabilities on a metric space $X$. The sequence \emph{converges weakly} to $\mu$, written $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$, when
$\int f\,d\mu_n\to\int f\,d\mu$ for every bounded continuous $f$. For random variables, $X_n\Rightarrow X$ means that the laws converge weakly.
Volume~4 calls this convergence in distribution, in the central limit theorem.

\begin{think}
Suppose $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$. Must $\mu_n(A)\to\mu(A)$ for every Borel set $A$? Test $\mu_n=\delta_{1/n}$ on $A=\{0\}$, and again on $A=\,]0,1]$.
\end{think}

Take $f$ bounded and continuous. Then $\int f\,d\delta_{1/n}=f(1/n)\to f(0)=\int f\,d\delta_0$, so $\delta_{1/n}\Rightarrow\delta_0$. For a concrete
function, $e^{-1/n}\to 1$. The sets do not follow. One has $\delta_{1/n}(\{0\})=0\not\to 1=\delta_0(\{0\})$, and
$\delta_{1/n}(]0,1])=1\not\to 0=\delta_0(]0,1])$. The mass arrives on the boundary at the last moment. The \emph{boundary} of $A$ is
$\partial A=\overline A\setminus\mathring A$. The set $A$ is a \emph{$\mu$-continuity set} when $\mu(\partial A)=0$.

The equivalences below rest on one identity, the layer-cake formula. You met it as Exercise~\ref{ex:layercake}; the short proof is repeated
here because the next theorem quotes it. Let $f\ge 0$ be measurable. Then $\int f\,d\mu=\int_0^\infty\mu(f>t)\,dt$.

Check it on a simple function before the general case. Let $s=\sum_{i\le m}a_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}$ with $0=a_0<a_1<\cdots<a_m$ and with the sets
$A_i$ disjoint. If $a_{k-1}\le t<a_k$, then $\mu(s>t)=\sum_{i\ge k}\mu(A_i)$, so
\[\int_0^\infty\mu(s>t)\,dt=\sum_{k\le m}(a_k-a_{k-1})\sum_{i\ge k}\mu(A_i)=\sum_ia_i\mu(A_i)=\int s\,d\mu.\]
For a general $f\ge 0$, Proposition~\ref{prop:measurablefns}(c) gives simple functions $s_n\uparrow f$. For each fixed $t$, the sets
$\{s_n>t\}$ increase to $\{f>t\}$, so $\mu(s_n>t)\uparrow\mu(f>t)$. Apply monotone convergence once in the space variable and once in $t$.
Each map $t\mapsto\mu(s_n>t)$ is non-increasing, hence Borel, so the integrals in $t$ are ordinary.

On a probability space the formula is easy to see for a constant. If $f=2$ on a set of measure $1$, then $\int f=2$ and
$\int_0^\infty\mu(f>t)\,dt=\int_0^2 1\,dt=2$.

\begin{theorem}[portmanteau]\label{thm:portmanteau}
Let $\mu_n$ and $\mu$ be probabilities on a metric space $X$. The following five statements are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$;
\item $\int f\,d\mu_n\to\int f\,d\mu$ whenever $f$ is bounded and Lipschitz;
\item $\limsup_n\mu_n(F)\le\mu(F)$ for every closed set $F$;
\item $\liminf_n\mu_n(U)\ge\mu(U)$ for every open set $U$;
\item $\mu_n(A)\to\mu(A)$ for every Borel $\mu$-continuity set $A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a)$\Rightarrow$(b) is the definition, restricted to Lipschitz integrands.

(b)$\Rightarrow$(c). The function $f_k(x)=\max(0,1-k\,d(x,F))$ is $k$-Lipschitz, because distance to $F$ is $1$-Lipschitz and truncation to $[0,1]$
does not increase Lipschitz constants. Also $\mathbf 1_F\le f_k$, and $f_k$ decreases pointwise to $\mathbf 1_F$. Therefore
\[\limsup_n\mu_n(F)\le\lim_n\int f_k\,d\mu_n=\int f_k\,d\mu,\]
and the right-hand side decreases to $\mu(F)$ as $k\to\infty$, by dominated convergence.

(c)$\Leftrightarrow$(d). If $U$ is open then $X\setminus U$ is closed, and
\[\liminf_n\mu_n(U)=1-\limsup_n\mu_n(X\setminus U).\]
The closed-set bound turns the right-hand side into something at least $1-\mu(X\setminus U)=\mu(U)$. The converse is the same sentence with
complements reversed.

(c) and (d)$\Rightarrow$(e). For any Borel $A$,
\[\mu(\mathring A)\le\liminf_n\mu_n(\mathring A)\le\liminf_n\mu_n(A)\le\limsup_n\mu_n(A)\le\limsup_n\mu_n(\overline A)\le\mu(\overline A).\]
If $\mu(\partial A)=0$, then $\mu(\mathring A)=\mu(A)=\mu(\overline A)$, and the two ends agree. The squeezed sequence $\mu_n(A)$ converges to $\mu(A)$.

(e)$\Rightarrow$(a). Let $f$ be bounded and continuous. An affine change of the values reduces us to the case $0<f<1$; a constant function is
already settled. The level sets $\{f=t\}$ are pairwise disjoint, so $\mu(\{f=t\})=0$ for all but countably many $t$, hence for Lebesgue-almost
every $t\in[0,1]$. Continuity of $f$ forces $\partial\{f>t\}\subseteq\{f=t\}$: if $f(x)>t$ then a neighbourhood stays above $t$, and if $f(x)<t$
then a neighbourhood stays below $t$. Thus $\{f>t\}$ is a continuity set for almost every $t$, and (e) gives $\mu_n(f>t)\to\mu(f>t)$ for
almost every $t$. The layer-cake formula and dominated convergence on $[0,1]$, where the integrands are bounded by $1$, give
\[\int f\,d\mu_n=\int_0^1\mu_n(f>t)\,dt\to\int_0^1\mu(f>t)\,dt=\int f\,d\mu.\]
\end{proof}

Run the five tests on $\delta_{1/n}\Rightarrow\delta_0$. The closed set $\{0\}$ satisfies $\limsup\delta_{1/n}(\{0\})=0\le 1$. The open interval
$]0,1[$ satisfies $\liminf\delta_{1/n}(]0,1[)=1\ge 0$. The half-open interval $]0,1]$ has boundary $\{0,1\}$, and $\delta_0$ charges that
boundary, so (e) does not apply to it. The integrals of bounded continuous functions converge, as $f(1/n)\to f(0)$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:cdfweak}
Let $\mu_n$ and $\mu$ be probabilities on $\R$, with distribution functions $F_n$ and $F$. Then $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$ if and only if
$F_n(t)\to F(t)$ at every $t$ where $F$ is continuous.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The boundary of $]-\infty,t]$ is $\{t\}$. If $F$ is continuous at $t$, then $\mu(\{t\})=0$, so (e) of Theorem~\ref{thm:portmanteau} yields
$F_n(t)\to F(t)$.

Conversely, assume $F_n\to F$ on the set $D$ of continuity points of $F$. That set is dense, because a monotone function has at most
countably many jumps. Every open subset of $\R$ is a countable disjoint union of open intervals, bounded or unbounded. The goal is (d).

Take a bounded component $]a,b[$. It contains a half-open piece $]a',b']$ with $a',b'\in D$ and with $\mu(]a,b[\setminus\,]a',b'])$ as small
as we like. Then
\[\liminf_n\mu_n(]a,b[)\ge\lim_n\bigl(F_n(b')-F_n(a')\bigr)=F(b')-F(a'),\]
and the right-hand side is as close as we like to $\mu(]a,b[)$.

Take a component $]a,\infty[$. Choose $a',M\in D$ with $a<a'$ and with $F(M)$ close to $1$. Then
\[\mu\bigl(]a,\infty[\setminus\,]a',M]\bigr)=(1-F(M))+(F(a')-F(a))\]
is small, and
\[\liminf_n\mu_n(]a,\infty[)\ge\lim_n\bigl(F_n(M)-F_n(a')\bigr)=F(M)-F(a'),\]
which is close to $\mu(]a,\infty[)$. A component $]-\infty,b[$ is the same estimate, read from a continuity point $m$ at which $F(m)$ is close
to $0$. In all three shapes the lower bound can be pushed as close as we like to the measure of the component, so
$\liminf_n\mu_n(I)\ge\mu(I)$ for every connected component $I$.

For a countable disjoint union $U=\bigsqcup_j I_j$, fix $J$ and use $\mu_n(U)\ge\sum_{j\le J}\mu_n(I_j)$. The $\liminf$ in $n$ is at least
$\sum_{j\le J}\mu(I_j)$, because a finite sum passes inside a $\liminf$. Let $J\to\infty$. Then
$\liminf_n\mu_n(U)\ge\sum_j\mu(I_j)=\mu(U)$. So (d) holds, and Theorem~\ref{thm:portmanteau} gives weak convergence.
\end{proof}

\section{Prokhorov's theorem}

A single law on a Polish space is tight, by Theorem~\ref{thm:ulam}. A family may still lose mass at infinity. A family $\mathcal M$ of
probabilities is \emph{tight} when, for every $\varepsilon>0$, one compact set $K$ satisfies $\mu(K)\ge 1-\varepsilon$ for every $\mu\in\mathcal M$.
The laws $\delta_n$ on $\R$ are not tight: any compact set is bounded, and $\delta_n$ eventually lives outside it. They also have no weakly
convergent subsequence. Along any indices $n_j\to\infty$, build a bounded continuous function with value $(-1)^j$ at $n_j$ and linear between
those integers. Its integrals against $\delta_{n_j}$ are $(-1)^j$, which do not converge. Exercise~\ref{ex:v7c12b} shows a broader fact on the
line: any sequence that does converge weakly is a tight family.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:separable}
If $K$ is a compact metric space, then $C(K)$ is separable.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Chapter~\ref{ch:compact} gives a countable dense set $(a_k)$ in $K$. Let $\mathcal A$ be the set of polynomials, with rational coefficients, in
finitely many of the functions $x\mapsto d(x,a_k)$, together with the constant $1$. Then $\mathcal A$ is countable. Its uniform closure is a
closed subalgebra of $C(K)$: a uniform limit of rational multiples is a real multiple. The closure contains the constants. It separates
points. If $x\ne y$, choose $a_k$ with $d(x,a_k)<\frac13 d(x,y)$. Then
\[d(y,a_k)\ge d(x,y)-d(x,a_k)>\tfrac23 d(x,y)>d(x,a_k),\]
so $d(\cdot,a_k)$ takes different values at $x$ and at $y$. Stone--Weierstrass (Theorem~\ref{thm:sw}) says that the closure is all of $C(K)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Prokhorov]\label{thm:prokhorov}
Let $\mu_n$ be a tight sequence of probabilities. If the underlying space is a compact metric space, or if the underlying space is $\R^d$,
then some subsequence converges weakly.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Compact $K$.} Here $K$ itself is a compact set of mass $1$, so every sequence of probabilities is tight. Let $(f_j)$ be dense in
$C(K)$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:separable}. For each $j$ the sequence $\int f_j\,d\mu_n$ is bounded by $\|f_j\|_\infty$. A diagonal extraction
produces a subsequence, still written $\mu_n$, along which $\int f_j\,d\mu_n$ converges for every $j$.

Now take an arbitrary $f\in C(K)$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $j$ with $\|f-f_j\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. For every probability $\nu$,
\[\Bigl|\int f\,d\nu-\int f_j\,d\nu\Bigr|<\varepsilon.\]
Hence
\[\limsup_{n,m}\Bigl|\int f\,d\mu_n-\int f\,d\mu_m\Bigr|\le 2\varepsilon.\]
The number $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, so $\int f\,d\mu_n$ is Cauchy and the limit $\Lambda f=\lim_n\int f\,d\mu_n$ exists. The form $\Lambda$ is
linear and positive, and $\Lambda 1=1$. Theorem~\ref{thm:riesz} supplies a probability $\mu$ with $\Lambda f=\int f\,d\mu$. By construction,
$\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$ along the subsequence.

\emph{$\R^d$.} The coordinatewise map $\varphi(x)=\bigl(x_i/(1+|x_i|)\bigr)_i$ is a homeomorphism of $\R^d$ onto the open cube
$Q=\,]-1,1[^d$. For a single coordinate, $\varphi(0)=0$, $\varphi(3)=3/4$ and $\varphi(100)=100/101$. The closure $\overline Q$ is compact.
Let $\nu_n=\varphi_*\mu_n$, a probability on $\overline Q$. The compact case gives a subsequence along which $\nu_n$ converges weakly to some
probability $\nu$ on $\overline Q$.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose a compact $K\subset\R^d$ with $\mu_n(K)\ge 1-\varepsilon$ for every $n$. Then $\varphi(K)$ is compact in $\overline Q$.
Portmanteau (c) of Theorem~\ref{thm:portmanteau}, applied to the closed set $\varphi(K)$, yields
\[\nu(\varphi(K))\ge\limsup_n\nu_n(\varphi(K))\ge 1-\varepsilon.\]
The set $\varphi(K)$ lies in $Q$. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $\nu(Q)=1$. The pushforward $\mu=(\varphi^{-1})_*\nu$ is therefore a probability
on $\R^d$.

Let $g\in C_c(\R^d)$. The composite $g\circ\varphi^{-1}$ vanishes near the boundary of $Q$, because $g$ vanishes outside a compact subset of
$\R^d$ and $\varphi^{-1}$ sends points near $\partial Q$ to infinity. Extending it by $0$ on $\partial Q$ produces a continuous function on
$\overline Q$. Weak convergence of $\nu_n$ thus gives $\int g\,d\mu_n\to\int g\,d\mu$ along the subsequence.

To pass from $C_c$ to a bounded continuous $f$, choose $\chi\in C_c(\R^d)$ with $0\le\chi\le 1$ and $\int\chi\,d\mu>1-\varepsilon$. Along the
subsequence, $\int\chi\,d\mu_n\to\int\chi\,d\mu$, so $\int(1-\chi)\,d\mu_n<\varepsilon$ for all large indices of that subsequence. Also
$\int(1-\chi)\,d\mu<\varepsilon$. Then
\[\Bigl|\int f\,d\mu_n-\int f\,d\mu\Bigr|\le\Bigl|\int f\chi\,d\mu_n-\int f\chi\,d\mu\Bigr|+\|f\|_\infty\Bigl(\int(1-\chi)\,d\mu_n+\int(1-\chi)\,d\mu\Bigr).\]
The product $f\chi$ lies in $C_c(\R^d)$, so the first term tends to $0$. Eventually the whole right-hand side is less than
$(2\|f\|_\infty+1)\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, $\int f\,d\mu_n\to\int f\,d\mu$ along the subsequence.
\end{proof}

Prokhorov's paper of 1956 proves more. On a Polish space, a family of probabilities is tight if and only if every sequence drawn from it has
a weakly convergent subsequence. That general form is quoted from the paper listed in the sources. It is not proved here, and nothing in this
volume uses it. It is the compactness behind Donsker's theorem, the central limit theorem for random walks read as random functions, which
belongs to Volume~12. Exercise~\ref{ex:helly} is Helly's one-dimensional selection theorem, the ancestor of the argument above.

\section{Kolmogorov's extension theorem}

A random sequence $(X_1,X_2,\ldots)$ may be read as one point, chosen at random, in the big space $\R^\N$. Write $\mu_n$ for the law of $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$. These laws are
\emph{consistent}: $\mu_{n+1}(B\times\R)=\mu_n(B)$ for every Borel $B\subseteq\R^n$. Kolmogorov's theorem says that consistency is the only
obstruction. Let $\pi_n(x)=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$. A \emph{cylinder} is a set $\pi_n^{-1}(B)$ with $B$ Borel in $\R^n$. The product $\sigma$-algebra
on $\R^\N$ is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the cylinders.

\begin{theorem}[Kolmogorov, 1933]\label{thm:kolmogorov}
Let $\mu_n$ be a consistent sequence of probabilities on $\R^n$, for $n\ge 1$. There is a unique probability $\mu$ on the product
$\sigma$-algebra of $\R^\N$ such that $\mu(\pi_n^{-1}(B))=\mu_n(B)$ for every $n$ and every Borel $B\subseteq\R^n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The cylinders form an algebra $\mathcal C$. Two cylinders may be rewritten at a common level: if $n\le m$ then
$\pi_n^{-1}(B)=\pi_m^{-1}(B\times\R^{m-n})$, and consistency makes the description unambiguous. The intersection of two cylinders at the same
level is the cylinder of the intersection, and the complement of $\pi_n^{-1}(B)$ is $\pi_n^{-1}(\R^n\setminus B)$.

Define $\mu_0(\pi_n^{-1}(B))=\mu_n(B)$. This is well defined. If $\pi_n^{-1}(B)=\pi_m^{-1}(B')$ with $n\le m$, consistency gives
$\mu_m(B\times\R^{m-n})=\mu_n(B)$. Finite additivity is the same fact one level up. A finite family of cylinders may be written at one common
level $N$ as $\pi_N^{-1}(B_j)$. If the cylinders are disjoint, the sets $B_j$ are disjoint. If their union is a cylinder $\pi_N^{-1}(B)$, then
$B=\bigsqcup_j B_j$, so $\mu_0$ of the union equals $\mu_N(B)=\sum_j\mu_N(B_j)$.

An algebra is a semiring in the sense of Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}: it contains $\varnothing$, it is closed under intersections, and the
difference of two members is again a member, hence a disjoint union of one member. Carath\'eodory's extension theorem
(Theorem~\ref{thm:extension}) will produce the measure once $\mu_0$ is countably additive on $\mathcal C$. Uniqueness is then
Theorem~\ref{thm:uniq6}, because $\mathcal C$ is a $\pi$-system and $\mu_0(\R^\N)=1$.

Finite additivity reduces countable additivity to a continuity statement. If disjoint cylinders $C_i$ have union $C$ still in $\mathcal C$,
the remainders $R_N=C\setminus\bigcup_{i\le N}C_i$ lie in $\mathcal C$, decrease to $\varnothing$, and satisfy
$\mu_0(C)=\sum_{i\le N}\mu_0(C_i)+\mu_0(R_N)$. So it is enough to prove: if cylinders $C_k$ decrease to $\varnothing$, then $\mu_0(C_k)\to 0$.

Suppose, on the contrary, that $\mu_0(C_k)\ge\delta>0$ for every $k$. Write $C_k=\pi_{n_k}^{-1}(B_k)$ with $n_1<n_2<\cdots$. Each $\mu_{n_k}$
is a finite Borel measure on the Polish space $\R^{n_k}$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:ulam} gives a compact set $K_k\subseteq B_k$ with
$\mu_{n_k}(B_k\setminus K_k)<\delta 2^{-k-1}$. Define $D_k=\bigcap_{j\le k}\pi_{n_j}^{-1}(K_j)$. Then $D_k\subseteq C_k$. Because the cylinders
$C_k$ decrease, a point of $C_k$ that misses $\pi_{n_j}^{-1}(K_j)$ for some $j\le k$ lies in $C_j\setminus\pi_{n_j}^{-1}(K_j)$. Therefore
\[\mu_0(C_k\setminus D_k)\le\sum_{j\le k}\mu_0\bigl(C_j\setminus\pi_{n_j}^{-1}(K_j)\bigr)<\delta/2,\]
so $\mu_0(D_k)>\delta/2$ and $D_k$ is nonempty. Pick $x^{(k)}\in D_k$.

For each fixed $j$, the points $\pi_{n_j}(x^{(k)})$ with $k\ge j$ lie in the compact set $K_j$. Extract a subsequence along which
$\pi_{n_1}(x^{(k)})$ converges in $K_1$, then a further subsequence along which $\pi_{n_2}(x^{(k)})$ converges in $K_2$, and so on. The
diagonal subsequence makes $\pi_{n_j}(x^{(k)})$ converge for every $j$. Since $n_j\to\infty$, every coordinate of $x^{(k)}$ converges. Call
the limit point $x\in\R^\N$. Each $K_j$ is closed, so $\pi_{n_j}(x)\in K_j$. Hence
\[x\in\bigcap_j\pi_{n_j}^{-1}(K_j)\subseteq\bigcap_j C_j=\varnothing,\]
which is absurd.
\end{proof}

If $\mu_n=\nu_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\nu_n$, the theorem produces an independent sequence with those laws. That is a second proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:independent}. The same theorem builds a Markov chain from a transition kernel, a Gaussian sequence from a covariance, and
any process indexed by $\N$ once the finite-dimensional laws are known and consistent. Volume~12 extends the indexing set from $\N$ to
$[0,\infty[$, and that extension is the first step toward Brownian motion. Nothing above relies on it.

One metric that makes $\R^\N$ Polish is
\[d(x,y)=\sum_{n\ge 1}2^{-n}\min\bigl(1,|x_n-y_n|\bigr).\]
A $d$-Cauchy sequence is Cauchy in each coordinate, the coordinatewise limit is a $d$-limit, and the sequences of rationals with finitely many
nonzero terms are dense. The product $\sigma$-algebra is the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of this topology: both are generated by the cylinders with
rational corners, by the same counting used for finite products in Proposition~\ref{prop:borelproduct}.

\section{Hausdorff measures and dimension}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch11-carpet}
\caption{The Sierpi\'nski carpet, attractor of eight similarities of ratio $\frac13$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:metric}). Stage $k$ keeps $8^k$ of the
$9^k$ subsquares. The Hausdorff dimension is $\frac{\log 8}{\log 3}\approx 1.893$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:carpet}).}
\label{fig:carpet}
\end{figure}

Lengths scale as $r^1$, areas as $r^2$, volumes as $r^3$, when the set is dilated by $r$. Cover the unit interval by $n$ intervals of length
$1/n$. The sum of the diameters to the power $1$ equals $1$. The sum to the power $2$ equals $1/n$ and tends to $0$. The sum to the power $0$
equals $n$ and tends to infinity. The exponent $1$ is the one that stays positive and finite. Hausdorff copies that comparison in every
metric space, with an arbitrary real exponent $s\ge 0$.

For $A\subseteq X$ and $\delta>0$, define
\[\mathcal H^s_\delta(A)=\inf\Bigl\{\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^s:A\subseteq\bigcup_iU_i,\ \operatorname{diam}U_i\le\delta\Bigr\},\]
the infimum running over countable covers, with the convention $(\operatorname{diam}\varnothing)^s=0$. As $\delta$ decreases, fewer covers are allowed, so
$\mathcal H^s_\delta(A)$ increases. The limit $\mathcal H^s(A)\in[0,\infty]$ is the \emph{$s$-dimensional Hausdorff measure} of $A$. The set
functions $\mathcal H^s_\delta$ and $\mathcal H^s$ are outer measures. The proof that $\lambda^*$ is an outer measure, in
Theorem~\ref{thm:extension}, applies verbatim: a cover of a union is a union of covers, and the sums add.

\begin{remark}[quoted, not proved here]\label{rem:hausdorffquoted}
Two further facts about $\mathcal H^s$ are quoted from K.~Falconer, \emph{Fractal Geometry}, 3rd ed., Chapter~3 (Sections~3.1 and~3.2). They
are not proved in this volume. First, the restriction of $\mathcal H^s$ to the Borel sets is a measure. The reason is that $\mathcal H^s$ is a
\emph{metric} outer measure: $\mathcal H^s(A\cup B)=\mathcal H^s(A)+\mathcal H^s(B)$ whenever $d(A,B)>0$. Second, on $\R^d$ the measure
$\mathcal H^d$ is a constant multiple of Lebesgue measure $\lambda_d$. No statement below, and no proof below, uses either fact. Every
argument from here to the end of the chapter uses $\mathcal H^s$ only as an outer measure, which the preceding paragraph already checked.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:jump}
If $\mathcal H^s(A)<\infty$ and $t>s$, then $\mathcal H^t(A)=0$. Hence there is a unique value $\dim_H A\in[0,\infty]$, the \emph{Hausdorff
dimension} of $A$, such that $\mathcal H^s(A)=\infty$ for every $s<\dim_H A$ and $\mathcal H^s(A)=0$ for every $s>\dim_H A$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take a cover by sets of diameter at most $\delta$. Each diameter is at most $\delta$, so
\[\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^t\le\delta^{t-s}\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^s.\]
Therefore $\mathcal H^t_\delta(A)\le\delta^{t-s}\mathcal H^s_\delta(A)\le\delta^{t-s}\mathcal H^s(A)$. Let $\delta\to 0$. The right-hand side
tends to $0$, so $\mathcal H^t(A)=0$. The jump from $\infty$ down to $0$ can happen at only one exponent. That exponent is $\dim_H A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
A line has dimension $1$ and a plane has dimension $2$. What should the dimension be for a set that is thicker than a curve and thinner than
a filled region? Can a subset of the plane have Lebesgue measure $0$ and Hausdorff dimension $2$?
\end{think}

An explicit cover gives an upper bound on dimension, because $\mathcal H^s$ is an infimum. A lower bound has to face every cover. The device
is to spread a measure on the set so that no small piece carries too much mass.

\begin{lemma}[mass distribution principle]\label{lem:massdist}
Let $\mu$ be a finite Borel measure with $\mu(A)>0$. Suppose there exist $c<\infty$ and $\delta>0$ such that $\mu(U)\le c\,(\operatorname{diam}U)^s$ for
every closed set $U$ of diameter at most $\delta$. Then $\mathcal H^s(A)\ge\mu(A)/c>0$, and therefore $\dim_H A\ge s$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $A\subseteq\bigcup_i U_i$ with $\operatorname{diam}U_i\le\delta$. Replacing $U_i$ by its closure does not change the diameter. Then
\[\mu(A)\le\sum_i\mu(\overline{U_i})\le c\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^s.\]
The infimum over all such covers is at least $\mu(A)/c$. Letting the mesh $\delta$ be the one in the hypothesis gives the bound on
$\mathcal H^s(A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch11-cantorcover}
\caption{Three covers of the middle-thirds Cantor set: $2$ intervals of length $\frac13$, then $4$ of length $\frac19$, then $8$ of length
$\frac1{27}$. At the exponent $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$, each cover has $\sum(\operatorname{diam})^s=1$. The number of pieces and the size of each piece
balance.}
\label{fig:cantorcover}
\end{figure}

For the middle-thirds Cantor set the natural guess is the exponent that balances two copies at scale $\frac13$: solve $2\cdot 3^{-s}=1$, that
is $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$. The next theorem proves that this guess is the dimension, and it pins the measure between $\frac12$ and $1$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cantordim}
The middle-thirds Cantor set $C$ has Hausdorff dimension $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\approx 0.631$, and $\frac12\le\mathcal H^s(C)\le 1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Upper bound.} Let $C_n$ be the union of the $2^n$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$ that appear at stage $n$ of the construction.
These intervals cover $C$. Since $3^s=2$, one has $2^n(3^{-n})^s=1$. Therefore $\mathcal H^s_{3^{-n}}(C)\le 1$ for every $n$, and
$\mathcal H^s(C)\le 1$. You can check the first stage by hand: two intervals of length $\frac13$, and $2\cdot 3^{-s}=1$.

\emph{Lower bound.} Let $\mu_c$ be the Cantor law of Chapter~\ref{ch:measures}. It is supported on $C$, and it gives mass $2^{-k}$ to each of
the $2^k$ intervals of $C_k$. Any two of those intervals are separated by a gap of length at least $3^{-k}$. Let $U$ be closed, with
$0<\operatorname{diam}U<1$, and choose $k$ so that $3^{-k-1}\le\operatorname{diam}U<3^{-k}$. Then $U$ meets at most one interval of $C_k$, because a second interval
would require a jump of at least $3^{-k}$. Hence
\[\mu_c(U)\le 2^{-k}=(3^{-k})^s=3^s(3^{-k-1})^s\le 2(\operatorname{diam}U)^s.\]
A set of diameter $0$ is a single point, and $\mu_c$ has no atoms, so the same bound holds with room to spare. Lemma~\ref{lem:massdist} with
$c=2$ yields $\mathcal H^s(C)\ge\frac12$.
\end{proof}

So $C$ is too thin to carry length: $1>s$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:jump} forces $\mathcal H^1(C)=0$. It is too thick to be counted:
$\mathcal H^0$ is counting measure, and $C$ is uncountable. In dimension $0.631$ the size is positive and finite. In fact $\mathcal H^s(C)=1$.
Hausdorff's paper of 1918 contains that sharper lower bound. The equality is quoted here and is not used.

The same balance, $m$ similar copies of ratio $r$ that do not overlap too much, suggests the dimension $\frac{\log m}{\log(1/r)}$. The general
theorem is due to Moran (1946) and Hutchinson (1981). It is quoted, and it is not used as a premise. The exercises compute the exponent
directly for the numbers in $[0,1]$ with no decimal digit $7$, and for the Sierpi\'nski carpet.

\begin{beauty}
Lebesgue measure sizes sets in dimensions $1$, $2$ and $3$. Hausdorff's sum of $s$th powers of diameters sizes them at every real $s$. For
the Cantor set the balancing exponent is $\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$, about $0.631$, and the $s$-measure lies between $\frac12$ and $1$. On the
side of chance, a positive linear form on $C(K)$ is a finite measure, a tight sequence of laws on $\R^d$ has a weakly convergent subsequence,
and consistent finite-dimensional laws are one law on the space of sequences.
\end{beauty}

\section{What is proved, and the next question}

On a Polish space, every finite Borel measure is carried, up to an arbitrary $\varepsilon$, by a compact set (Theorem~\ref{thm:ulam}). On a compact
metric space, the positive linear forms on the continuous real functions are exactly the integrals against finite Borel measures
(Theorem~\ref{thm:riesz}). Weak convergence of probabilities has the five equivalent forms in Theorem~\ref{thm:portmanteau}, and a tight
sequence of laws on $\R^d$ has a weakly convergent subsequence (Theorem~\ref{thm:prokhorov}). Consistent finite-dimensional laws are the
marginals of one law on the space of sequences (Theorem~\ref{thm:kolmogorov}). Later volumes cite the Prokhorov and Kolmogorov theorems in
exactly these forms. The middle-thirds Cantor set has dimension $\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:cantordim}).

Three statements were quoted and left unproved, each marked where it stands. Remark~\ref{rem:hausdorffquoted} quotes that $\mathcal H^s$ is a
Borel measure and that, when $s=d$, it is a constant multiple of Lebesgue measure on $\R^d$. The paragraph after Theorem~\ref{thm:prokhorov}
quotes the general Prokhorov theorem on Polish spaces. The paragraph after Theorem~\ref{thm:cantordim} quotes the theorem of Moran and
Hutchinson on self-similar sets. No proof in this volume uses them.

The next chapter takes up a question from Volume~2. How well can a typical real number be approximated by fractions? Volume~2 could compare
one number with its fractions. It had no word yet for a typical number. There are now two exact meanings: almost every number, and a comeagre
set of numbers. The next chapter shows that the two meanings disagree.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:nodigit7}
Let $E$ be the set of $x\in[0,1]$ that admit a decimal expansion with no digit equal to $7$. Show that $E$ is closed, that $\lambda(E)=0$, and
that $\dim_H E=\frac{\log 9}{\log 10}$. Kept intervals of the construction may touch at an endpoint, so count how many a set of small diameter
can meet.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:carpet}
Show that the Sierpi\'nski triangle has dimension $\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}$ and that the Sierpi\'nski carpet has dimension
$\frac{\log 8}{\log 3}$. Give a subset of the plane with Hausdorff dimension $2$ and Lebesgue measure $0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:lipschitzdim}
Suppose $f:A\to Y$ is $L$-Lipschitz. Show that $\mathcal H^s(f(A))\le L^s\mathcal H^s(A)$ and that $\dim_H f(A)\le\dim_H A$. Deduce that the
Cantor set and $[0,1]$ are not bi-Lipschitz equivalent, even though a continuous surjection carries one onto the other
(Exercise~\ref{ex:lebesguecurve}). Show that a countable set has Hausdorff dimension $0$, and that
$\dim_H\bigl(\bigcup_k A_k\bigr)=\sup_k\dim_H A_k$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:helly}
\emph{Helly's selection theorem.} Start with distribution functions $F_n$ on $\R$. (a) Produce a subsequence that converges at every rational, to
a limit $G$, and show that $F(t)=\inf_{q>t}G(q)$ is non-decreasing and right-continuous, with $F_{n_k}(t)\to F(t)$ at every continuity point
of $F$. (b) Show that $F$ itself is a distribution function, with limits $0$ and $1$ at $\pm\infty$, whenever the laws are tight. Give an
example where the limit is not a distribution function.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:glivenko}
\emph{Empirical laws.} Let $U_1,U_2,\ldots$ be independent and uniform on $[0,1]$, and set
$F_n(t)=\frac1n\#\{i\le n:U_i\le t\}$. Using the strong law at the grid points $\frac jm$, together with monotonicity, show that
$\sup_t|F_n(t)-t|\to 0$ almost surely (the Glivenko--Cantelli theorem). From that uniform convergence, deduce the weak convergence
$\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}\delta_{U_i}\Rightarrow\lambda|_{[0,1]}$ almost surely.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:weakexamples}
(a) Show that $\mathcal N(0,\frac1n)\Rightarrow\delta_0$, and that the uniform laws on $\{\frac1n,\frac2n,\ldots,1\}$ converge weakly to
$\lambda|_{[0,1]}$. (b) Find probabilities $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$ on $\R$ with $\int x\,d\mu_n\not\to\int x\,d\mu$. (c) Show that if
$\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$ and $\sup_n\int|x|^2\,d\mu_n<\infty$, then $\int x\,d\mu_n\to\int x\,d\mu$. Compare the truncation with Vitali's theorem
in Chapter~\ref{ch:integral}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:coversums}
\emph{The exponent that balances.} For the cover of the Cantor set by the $2^n$ intervals of $C_n$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cantorcover}), compute
$\sum(\operatorname{diam})^s$ at $s=\frac12$, at $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$ and at $s=\frac34$, first for $n=3$ and then as $n\to\infty$. What do the three
limits say about $\mathcal H^{1/2}(C)$ and about $\mathcal H^{3/4}(C)$? Which of the two conclusions needs a tool beyond these particular
covers?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v7c12a}
Let $K=\{0,1\}$ with $d(0,1)=1$. Every real function on $K$ is continuous. Define $\Lambda f=\frac14 f(0)+\frac34 f(1)$.
(a) Check that $\Lambda$ is linear and positive, and that $\Lambda 1=1$.
(b) Let $\mu=\frac14\delta_0+\frac34\delta_1$. Check that $\Lambda f=\int f\,d\mu$ for every $f$.
(c) If $\nu$ is any finite Borel measure on $K$ with $\int f\,d\nu=\Lambda f$ for every $f$, show that $\nu(\{0\})=\frac14$ and
$\nu(\{1\})=\frac34$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c12b}
Let $\mu_n$ and $\mu$ be probabilities on $\R$ with $\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu$. Show that the family $\{\mu_n:n\ge 1\}\cup\{\mu\}$ is tight. Use
Theorem~\ref{thm:ulam} on each of the first finitely many laws, and use the portmanteau theorem on the closed tails
$(-\infty,-M]\cup[M,\infty)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c12c}
(a) Show that $\mathcal H^s(\R)=0$ for every $s>1$, and deduce that every subset of $\R$ has Hausdorff dimension at most $1$.
(b) Show that $\lambda^*(A)\le\mathcal H^1(A)$ for every $A\subseteq\R$. Deduce that $\lambda^*(A)>0$ forces $\dim_H A=1$. Do this by comparing
covers; do not use the quoted identification of $\mathcal H^1$ with a multiple of Lebesgue measure.
(c) Fix $s\in(0,1)$ and set $r=2^{-1/s}$. Let $E_s$ be the set obtained by starting from $[0,1]$ and, at every stage, keeping the two closed
end subintervals of relative length $r$. Show that $\dim_H E_s=s$ and $\lambda(E_s)=0$. Deduce that
$A=\bigcup_{n\ge 2}E_{1-1/n}$ satisfies $\dim_H A=1$ and $\lambda(A)=0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v7c12d}
Let $\nu$ be a probability on $\R$, and let $\mu_n$ be the law of $(X,\ldots,X)\in\R^n$ when $X$ has law $\nu$.
(a) Check that the family $(\mu_n)$ is consistent, and let $\mu$ be its Kolmogorov extension on $\R^\N$.
(b) Show that $\mu$ is supported on the set of constant sequences.
(c) Let $\rho$ be the law of an independent sequence with every coordinate distributed as $\nu$. Show that if $\nu$ is not a Dirac mass, then
$\mu\ne\rho$, even though $\mu$ and $\rho$ have the same one-dimensional marginals.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:nodigit7}
\idea The set is a Cantor set with $9$ branches of ratio $\frac1{10}$, in place of $2$ branches of ratio $\frac13$. Kept intervals may share an
endpoint, so a small set may meet two of them. That costs a factor $2$ in the mass bound, and it does not change the dimension.
\writeup Let $E_k$ be the union of the $9^k$ closed intervals $[\frac{m}{10^k},\frac{m+1}{10^k}]$ whose $k$ decimal digits all avoid $7$. Then
$E=\bigcap_k E_k$. A number with an expansion that avoids $7$ lies in every $E_k$. Conversely, a point of every $E_k$ has, at each level, a
digit other than $7$, and the intervals are nested, so the digits form an expansion that avoids $7$. Thus $E$ is an intersection of closed
sets, hence closed. Also $\lambda(E)\le\lambda(E_k)=(\frac9{10})^k\to 0$.

For the upper bound, take $s=\frac{\log 9}{\log 10}$, so that $10^s=9$. The stage-$k$ intervals cover $E$ and have diameter $10^{-k}$, and
$9^k(10^{-k})^s=1$. Therefore $\mathcal H^s_{10^{-k}}(E)\le 1$ for every $k$, and $\mathcal H^s(E)\le 1$.

For the lower bound, let the digits $D_j$ be independent and uniform on $\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,8,9\}$, and let $\mu$ be the law of
$\sum_j D_j 10^{-j}$. Then $\mu$ is supported on $E$, and $\mu$ gives mass $9^{-k}$ to each interval of $E_k$. The ambient decimal grid at
scale $10^{-k}$ consists of intervals of length $10^{-k}$ with disjoint interiors. A set of diameter less than $10^{-k}$ has a projection, on
the line, of diameter less than $10^{-k}$, so it meets at most two intervals of that grid. The kept intervals are a subcollection, so the same
bound applies to them. If $U$ is closed and $10^{-k-1}\le\operatorname{diam}U<10^{-k}$, then
\[\mu(U)\le 2\cdot 9^{-k}=2(10^{-k})^s\le 2\cdot 10^s(\operatorname{diam}U)^s=18(\operatorname{diam}U)^s,\]
where the last step uses $10^{-k}=10\cdot 10^{-k-1}\le 10\operatorname{diam}U$ and $10^s=9$. Lemma~\ref{lem:massdist} gives
$\mathcal H^s(E)\ge\frac1{18}$. Combined with the upper bound and Proposition~\ref{prop:jump}, one gets
$\dim_H E=\frac{\log 9}{\log 10}\approx 0.954$. Almost every number in $[0,1]$ has a digit $7$. The numbers that avoid $7$ still form a set of
dimension close to $1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:carpet}
\idea Count the pieces at stage $k$ for the upper bound. For the lower bound, spread the uniform mass of the construction, and bound the
number of pieces a set of the right diameter can touch.
\writeup \emph{Carpet.} Let $S$ be the carpet. Stage $k$ is a union of $8^k$ closed squares of side $3^{-k}$, hence of diameter
$\sqrt 2\cdot 3^{-k}$, and these squares cover $S$. With $s=\frac{\log 8}{\log 3}$ one has $3^s=8$, so
\[\mathcal H^s_{\sqrt 2\cdot 3^{-k}}(S)\le 8^k(\sqrt 2\cdot 3^{-k})^s=2^{s/2}.\]
Thus $\mathcal H^s(S)<\infty$, and $\dim_H S\le s$.

Let $\mu$ be the law of the random point whose successive pairs of base-$3$ digits are drawn independently and uniformly from the $8$ pairs
that the construction keeps. Then $\mu$ is supported on $S$ and gives mass $8^{-k}$ to each stage-$k$ square. Let $U$ be closed, with
$3^{-k-1}\le\operatorname{diam}U<3^{-k}$. The projection of $U$ on each coordinate axis has diameter less than $3^{-k}$, so each projection meets at most
two intervals of the grid of length $3^{-k}$. Therefore $U$ meets at most $4$ closed squares of that grid, and at most $4$ squares of stage
$k$. Hence
\[\mu(U)\le 4\cdot 8^{-k}=4(3^{-k})^s\le 4\cdot 3^s(\operatorname{diam}U)^s,\]
because $3^{-k}\le 3\operatorname{diam}U$ and $3^s=8$. Lemma~\ref{lem:massdist} yields $\mathcal H^s(S)>0$, so $\dim_H S=s=\frac{\log 8}{\log 3}$.

\emph{Triangle.} Let $T$ be the Sierpi\'nski triangle, built by keeping, at each stage, the three corner subtriangles and removing the middle
one. Stage $k$ consists of $3^k$ closed equilateral triangles of side $2^{-k}$. With $s=\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}$ one has $2^s=3$, and the same
covering estimate used for the carpet gives $\mathcal H^s(T)<\infty$. The lower bound needs a count of how many stage-$k$ triangles a small
set can meet. All triangles of the construction have the same orientation.

At most two stage-$k$ triangles contain any given vertex. A corner of the original triangle belongs to exactly one triangle at every stage,
the corner child. Every later vertex is born as a midpoint when a triangle is subdivided, and exactly two of the three children meet there,
the middle child having been removed. At the next stage, each of those two triangles contributes exactly one child at that same point. No
third triangle is added. Thus each vertex of a stage-$k$ triangle is shared with at most one other stage-$k$ triangle, and the triangle has
at most three neighbours.

Two of those neighbours are its siblings, the other two children of the same parent, and those two siblings share a vertex with each other.
A third neighbour, if it exists, meets the parent only at the outer vertex, so it shares no vertex with either sibling. Consider a family of
stage-$k$ triangles in which every pair shares a vertex. Such a family has at most three members: start from one triangle, and the third
neighbour cannot be added to a family that already contains a sibling. Therefore any four stage-$k$ triangles include two that share no
vertex.

Those two are at least $\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-k}$ apart. The proof is by induction on the stage. At stage $1$ there are three triangles, and
every pair shares a vertex, so there is no pair to separate. Children of one parent always share a vertex with each other, as just noted. So
at a later stage, two triangles that share no vertex lie in different parents of stage $k-1$.

If those parents themselves share no vertex, the inductive hypothesis puts them at least $\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-(k-1)}=\sqrt 3\cdot 2^{-k}$
apart, which is twice the altitude of a stage-$k$ triangle. Subsets are at least as far apart.

If the parents do meet, they meet at a single vertex $V$ and share no edge. All construction triangles have the same orientation, and two
distinct equilateral triangles of the same orientation cannot share an edge: the third vertex would be the same point. A rotation by
$60^\circ$ reverses that orientation, so neighbouring sectors of the triangular grid point opposite ways. Two sectors of our orientation
therefore have at least one empty $60^\circ$ sector between them. Place $V$ at the origin,
and place the parent of the child $P$ that does not contain $V$ in the sector of angles $[0^\circ,60^\circ]$, with side length
$L=2^{1-k}$. The nearest possible location of the other parent is the sector $[120^\circ,180^\circ]$. The child $P$ that omits $V$ and meets
the edge at angle $60^\circ$ is the convex hull of three points: the far vertex $B=L(\cos 60^\circ,\sin 60^\circ)$, the midpoint
$M=(L/2)(\cos 60^\circ,\sin 60^\circ)$, and the midpoint $N$ of the opposite side. Every point of $P$ is at distance at least $L/2=2^{-k}$
from $V$, with equality at $M$.

Let $n=(\cos 30^\circ,\sin 30^\circ)=(\frac{\sqrt 3}{2},\frac12)$. The line through $V$ at angle $120^\circ$ is the set $\{X:X\cdot n=0\}$, and
the sector $[120^\circ,180^\circ]$ lies in $\{X:X\cdot n\le 0\}$. Distance from a point $X$ with $X\cdot n\ge 0$ to that sector is at least
$X\cdot n$. The three vertices give
\[M\cdot n=\frac{L\sqrt 3}{4},\qquad B\cdot n=\frac{L\sqrt 3}{2},\qquad N\cdot n=\frac{L\sqrt 3}{2}.\]
The function $X\mapsto X\cdot n$ is linear, so on the whole convex hull it is at least $\frac{L\sqrt 3}{4}=\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-k}$. A parent
sitting farther from the edge at $120^\circ$ is only farther away. Thus the two stage-$k$ triangles are at least $\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-k}$
apart.

A closed set $U$ with $\operatorname{diam}U<\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-k}$ therefore meets at most three stage-$k$ triangles: four triangles would include a
pair at least that far apart. Given $0<\operatorname{diam}U$, choose $k$ with
\[\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-(k+1)}\le\operatorname{diam}U<\frac{\sqrt 3}{2}2^{-k}.\]
The uniform mass on the construction satisfies $\mu(U)\le 3\cdot 3^{-k}=3(2^{-k})^s$. The lower bound on the diameter rearranges to
$2^{-k}\le\frac{4}{\sqrt 3}\operatorname{diam}U$, so
\[\mu(U)\le 3\Bigl(\frac{4}{\sqrt 3}\Bigr)^s(\operatorname{diam}U)^s.\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:massdist} gives $\mathcal H^s(T)>0$. Hence $\dim_H T=\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}$.

\emph{Dimension $2$, measure $0$.} For each integer $m\ge 2$, let $S_m$ be the carpet obtained by removing, at every stage, one central
square out of an $m\times m$ grid. The same counting as for the ordinary carpet gives
$\dim_H S_m=\frac{\log(m^2-1)}{\log m}$, which tends to $2$ as $m\to\infty$. The area of the stage-$k$ picture is $(1-m^{-2})^k$ times the
area of the unit square, so $\lambda_2(S_m)=0$. The union $\bigcup_m S_m$ is a countable union of null sets, hence null. Exercise~\ref{ex:lipschitzdim}
gives $\dim_H\bigl(\bigcup_m S_m\bigr)=\sup_m\dim_H S_m=2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:lipschitzdim}
\idea An $L$-Lipschitz map multiplies diameters by at most $L$, so it sends covers to covers and multiplies the sums by at most $L^s$.
Dimension cannot rise.
\writeup Suppose $A\subseteq\bigcup_i U_i$ with $\operatorname{diam}U_i\le\delta$. Then $f(A)\subseteq\bigcup_i f(U_i\cap A)$, and
$\operatorname{diam}f(U_i\cap A)\le L\operatorname{diam}U_i\le L\delta$. Therefore
\[\mathcal H^s_{L\delta}(f(A))\le L^s\sum_i(\operatorname{diam}U_i)^s.\]
Take the infimum over covers and let $\delta\to 0$. This is the inequality $\mathcal H^s(f(A))\le L^s\mathcal H^s(A)$. If $s>\dim_H A$, then
$\mathcal H^s(A)=0$, so $\mathcal H^s(f(A))=0$ and $\dim_H f(A)\le s$. Hence $\dim_H f(A)\le\dim_H A$.

A bi-Lipschitz bijection, and its inverse, would preserve dimension. Theorem~\ref{thm:cantordim} gives $\dim_H C=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}\approx 0.631$.
For the interval, cover $[0,1]$ by $n$ subintervals of length $1/n$: the sum of the diameters to the power $1$ equals $1$, so
$\mathcal H^1([0,1])\le 1$. In the other direction, any set of diameter $\ell$ is contained in an interval of length $\ell$, so a Hausdorff
cover gives $\lambda^*([0,1])\le\mathcal H^1_\delta([0,1])$ for every $\delta$. Thus $\mathcal H^1([0,1])\ge 1$. The two dimensions differ, so
no bi-Lipschitz bijection exists. Exercise~\ref{ex:lebesguecurve} supplies a continuous surjection from $C$ onto $[0,1]$, so continuity alone
does not preserve dimension.

A single point has $\mathcal H^s=0$ for every $s>0$: cover it by one set of diameter $\delta$, and let $\delta\to 0$. The outer measure
$\mathcal H^s$ is countably subadditive, so a countable set has $\mathcal H^s=0$ for every $s>0$, and its dimension is $0$.

For a countable union, monotonicity gives $\dim_H\bigl(\bigcup_k A_k\bigr)\ge\sup_k\dim_H A_k$. If $s>\sup_k\dim_H A_k$, then
$\mathcal H^s(A_k)=0$ for every $k$, so $\mathcal H^s\bigl(\bigcup_k A_k\bigr)=0$ by countable subadditivity. Thus the dimension of the union
is at most $s$, and therefore equals the supremum.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:helly}
\idea Distribution functions are monotone and take values in $[0,1]$. A diagonal argument makes the values converge on the countable set of
rationals, and monotonicity fills in the remaining points, except at jumps of the limit. Tightness is what stops the mass from leaving every
bounded interval.
\writeup (a) Enumerate the rationals as $q_1,q_2,\ldots$. The values $F_n(q_1)$ lie in $[0,1]$, so some subsequence converges at $q_1$. From
that subsequence, extract a further one that converges at $q_2$, and continue. The diagonal subsequence, written $F_{n_k}$, converges at every
rational. Let $G(q)$ be the limit. If $q<q'$ then $F_{n_k}(q)\le F_{n_k}(q')$, so $G(q)\le G(q')$.

Define $F(t)=\inf_{q>t}G(q)$. If $t<t'$, the constraint $q>t'$ is stricter than $q>t$, so the infimum rises or stays: $F$ is non-decreasing.
For right-continuity, the infimum of $G$ over $q>t'$ decreases to the infimum over $q>t$ as $t'\downarrow t$, because every rational $q>t$
eventually satisfies $q>t'$.

Now let $F$ be continuous at $t$, and fix $\varepsilon>0$. By definition of the infimum there is a rational $q>t$ with $G(q)<F(t)+\varepsilon$. By
continuity and monotonicity there is $s<t$ with $F(s)>F(t)-\varepsilon$. Choose a rational $r\in\,]s,t[$. Then $r>s$, so
$G(r)\ge\inf_{q'>s}G(q')=F(s)>F(t)-\varepsilon$. Monotonicity of each $F_{n_k}$ gives $F_{n_k}(r)\le F_{n_k}(t)\le F_{n_k}(q)$. Let $k\to\infty$:
\[F(t)-\varepsilon<G(r)\le\liminf_k F_{n_k}(t)\le\limsup_k F_{n_k}(t)\le G(q)<F(t)+\varepsilon.\]
Thus $F_{n_k}(t)\to F(t)$.

(b) Suppose $\mu_n([-M,M])\ge 1-\varepsilon$ for every $n$. Then $F_n(-M-1)\le\varepsilon$, because $]-\infty,-M-1]$ misses $[-M,M]$, and
$F_n(M)\ge 1-\varepsilon$. Passing to the limit at continuity points, $F(t)\le\varepsilon$ for every continuity point $t<-M-1$, and $F(t)\ge 1-\varepsilon$
for every continuity point $t>M$. Continuity points are dense, and $F$ is non-decreasing, so $\lim_{t\to-\infty}F(t)=0$ and
$\lim_{t\to+\infty}F(t)=1$. Thus $F$ is a distribution function.

For the escape, take $F_n=\mathbf 1_{[n,\infty[}$, the distribution function of $\delta_n$. For each fixed rational $q$, one has $F_n(q)=0$ for
all $n>q$, so $G(q)=0$ and $F$ is identically $0$. All the mass has left to $+\infty$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:glivenko}
\idea The strong law handles one height $t$ at a time. A countable grid of heights is still a null set of exceptions. Monotonicity of $F_n$
and of the identity then controls every $t$, up to the mesh $\frac1m$.
\writeup Fix a rational $t\in[0,1]$. The random variables $\mathbf 1_{U_i\le t}$ are independent, take values in $\{0,1\}$, and have mean $t$.
The fourth-moment argument in the solution of Exercise~\ref{ex:salem} applies to them with success probability $p=t$: the variables
$Y_i=\mathbf 1_{U_i\le t}-t$ are bounded by $1$, centred, and independent, the fourth-moment count is at most $3n^2$, and Markov's inequality
plus Borel--Cantelli (Proposition~\ref{prop:measureprops}(e)) give
\[F_n(t)=\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}\mathbf 1_{U_i\le t}\to t\]
almost surely. A countable union of null sets is null, so outside one null set the convergence holds at every rational simultaneously.

Fix $m\ge 1$ and take $t\in[\frac{j-1}m,\frac jm]$. Monotonicity gives $F_n(t)\le F_n(\frac jm)$ and $t\ge\frac{j-1}m$, so
\[F_n(t)-t\le F_n\Bigl(\frac jm\Bigr)-\frac jm+\frac1m.\]
The matching lower bound is
\[F_n(t)-t\ge F_n\Bigl(\frac{j-1}m\Bigr)-\frac{j-1}m-\frac1m.\]
Therefore
\[\sup_t|F_n(t)-t|\le\max_{0\le j\le m}\Bigl|F_n\Bigl(\frac jm\Bigr)-\frac jm\Bigr|+\frac1m.\]
The $\limsup$ of the left-hand side is at most $\frac1m$. Let $m\to\infty$. The supremum tends to $0$ almost surely.

Corollary~\ref{cor:cdfweak} turns uniform convergence of distribution functions into weak convergence, since every point of the limit
$F(t)=t$ on $[0,1]$ is a continuity point. Hence $\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}\delta_{U_i}\Rightarrow\lambda|_{[0,1]}$ almost surely.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:weakexamples}
\idea Weak convergence controls integrals of bounded continuous functions. The integrand $x\mapsto x$ is unbounded, and a small mass far away
can keep the mean from settling. A uniform bound on the second moment stops that escape, in the same way that uniform integrability did in
Chapter~\ref{ch:integral}.
\writeup (a) The distribution function of $\mathcal N(0,\frac1n)$ at a point $t$ equals $\Phi(t\sqrt n)$. As $n\to\infty$ this tends to $0$ for
$t<0$ and to $1$ for $t>0$, which are the values of the distribution function of $\delta_0$ at its continuity points. Corollary~\ref{cor:cdfweak}
gives $\mathcal N(0,\frac1n)\Rightarrow\delta_0$. For the uniform law on $\{\frac kn:1\le k\le n\}$, the distribution function on $[0,1]$ is
$F_n(t)=\frac{\lfloor nt\rfloor}n$, which tends to $t$. The same corollary gives weak convergence to $\lambda|_{[0,1]}$.

(b) Let $\mu_n=(1-\frac1n)\delta_0+\frac1n\delta_n$. If $f$ is bounded and continuous, then
\[\int f\,d\mu_n=\Bigl(1-\frac1n\Bigr)f(0)+\frac1n f(n)\to f(0),\]
because $f(n)$ stays bounded. Thus $\mu_n\Rightarrow\delta_0$. The means are $\int x\,d\mu_n=1$ for every $n$, while $\int x\,d\delta_0=0$.

(c) Let $C=\sup_n\int x^2\,d\mu_n$, and let $\tau_M(x)=\max(-M,\min(x,M))$. The function $\tau_M$ is bounded and continuous. If $|x|>M$ then
$|x|-M\le x^2/M$, because $x^2-M|x|+M^2=(|x|-M/2)^2+3M^2/4\ge 0$ rearranges to that inequality. Therefore
\[\Bigl|\int x\,d\mu_n-\int\tau_M\,d\mu_n\Bigr|\le\frac1M\int x^2\,d\mu_n\le\frac CM.\]
The same comparison will apply to $\mu$ once $\int x^2\,d\mu\le C$ is known. For each fixed $K$, the function $\min(x^2,K)$ is bounded and
continuous, so weak convergence gives $\int\min(x^2,K)\,d\mu=\lim_n\int\min(x^2,K)\,d\mu_n\le C$. Let $K\to\infty$. Monotone convergence
yields $\int x^2\,d\mu\le C$, and the truncation bound holds for $\mu$ as well. Hence
\[\limsup_n\Bigl|\int x\,d\mu_n-\int x\,d\mu\Bigr|\le\frac{2C}M\]
for every $M>0$. Let $M\to\infty$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:coversums}
\idea Each of these covers has $2^n$ pieces of diameter $3^{-n}$. The sum is a geometric term in $n$, with ratio $2\cdot 3^{-s}$. That ratio
equals $1$ precisely when $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$.
\writeup The sum of $s$th powers of the diameters is $2^n(3^{-n})^s=(2\cdot 3^{-s})^n$.

At $s=\frac12$ the ratio is $\frac{2}{\sqrt 3}\approx 1.155$. For $n=3$ the sum is $(\frac{2}{\sqrt 3})^3=\frac{8}{3\sqrt 3}\approx 1.54$, and as
$n\to\infty$ the sum tends to infinity.

At $s=\frac{\log 2}{\log 3}$ the ratio equals $1$, because $3^s=2$. The sum equals $1$ for every $n$.

At $s=\frac34$ the ratio is $2\cdot 3^{-3/4}\approx 0.877$. For $n=3$ the sum is about $0.675$, and as $n\to\infty$ the sum tends to $0$.

For $s=\frac34$, these covers have diameters tending to $0$ and sums tending to $0$. Therefore $\mathcal H^{3/4}_\delta(C)=0$ for every
$\delta>0$, and $\mathcal H^{3/4}(C)=0$. That conclusion is complete: an upper bound on $\mathcal H^s$ only needs one good family of covers.

For $s=\frac12$, the sums of these particular covers tend to infinity. That does not force $\mathcal H^{1/2}(C)=\infty$. The quantity
$\mathcal H^{1/2}$ is an infimum over all covers, and some other cover might do better. The conclusion $\mathcal H^{1/2}(C)=\infty$ comes from
Theorem~\ref{thm:cantordim} together with Proposition~\ref{prop:jump}. The theorem's lower bound used the mass distribution principle, which
speaks about every cover at once.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c12a}
\idea On a finite discrete space every function is continuous, so the representing measure is visible on the indicator of each point. This is
Theorem~\ref{thm:riesz} with two points and no analysis.
\writeup (a) If $f=c_0\mathbf 1_{\{0\}}+c_1\mathbf 1_{\{1\}}$ and $g=d_0\mathbf 1_{\{0\}}+d_1\mathbf 1_{\{1\}}$, then
\[\Lambda(af+bg)=\frac14(ac_0+bd_0)+\frac34(ac_1+bd_1)=a\Lambda f+b\Lambda g.\]
If $f\ge 0$, then $f(0)\ge 0$ and $f(1)\ge 0$, so $\Lambda f\ge 0$. The constant function $1$ gives $\Lambda 1=\frac14+\frac34=1$.

(b) For the same $f$,

(c) The function $\mathbf 1_{\{0\}}$ is continuous. Integrating it gives $\nu(\{0\})=\Lambda(\mathbf 1_{\{0\}})=\frac14$. The same computation

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c12b}
\idea A single probability on $\R$ is tight by Ulam's theorem. A weak limit cannot put more mass on a closed tail than the limsup of the
approximating laws, so the tails of $\mu_n$ are eventually as small as the tails of $\mu$.
\writeup Fix $\varepsilon>0$. The sets $F_M=(-\infty,-M]\cup[M,\infty)$ are closed, and $\mu(F_M)\to 0$ as $M\to\infty$, because $\mu$ is a finite
measure on $\R$ and the sets decrease to the empty set. Choose $M$ with $\mu(F_M)<\varepsilon$. Portmanteau (c) of Theorem~\ref{thm:portmanteau}
gives $\limsup_n\mu_n(F_M)\le\mu(F_M)<\varepsilon$. Hence there exists $N$ such that $\mu_n(F_M)<\varepsilon$ for every $n\ge N$, which means
$\mu_n([-M,M])\ge 1-\varepsilon$.

The finite list $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_{N-1},\mu$ consists of finite Borel probabilities on the Polish space $\R$. Theorem~\ref{thm:ulam} gives, for
each of them, a compact set carrying mass at least $1-\varepsilon$. The union of those finitely many compact sets with the interval $[-M,M]$ is
compact. Call it $K$. Every measure in the family $\{\mu_n:n\ge 1\}\cup\{\mu\}$ then satisfies $\nu(K)\ge 1-\varepsilon$. The family is tight.

This is one direction of the general Prokhorov theorem, proved here only on $\R$, from the portmanteau theorem and from Ulam's theorem. The
equivalence on a general Polish space stays quoted, as in the paragraph after Theorem~\ref{thm:prokhorov}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c12c}
\idea Above exponent $1$, small intervals make the Hausdorff sums tend to $0$ on each bounded piece of the line. At exponent $1$, diameter and
length agree for intervals, so Lebesgue outer measure sits under $\mathcal H^1$. A two-branch Cantor set of ratio $r=2^{-1/s}$ copies the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cantordim} at any dimension $s<1$.
\writeup (a) Fix $s>1$ and $N\ge 1$. Cover $[-N,N]$ by intervals of length $\delta\in(0,1)$. At most $\frac{2N}{\delta}+1$ of them are needed, and
\[\sum(\operatorname{diam})^s\le\Bigl(\frac{2N}{\delta}+1\Bigr)\delta^s\le(2N+1)\delta^{s-1}.\]
Let $\delta\to 0$. Then $\mathcal H^s([-N,N])=0$. A countable union of null sets for an outer measure is null, so $\mathcal H^s(\R)=0$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:jump}, read in the opposite direction from a finite value at every $s>1$, forces $\dim_H\R\le 1$. Monotonicity gives the
same upper bound for every subset of $\R$.

(b) Let $U\subseteq\R$ with $\operatorname{diam}U=\ell<\infty$. Then $U$ is contained in a closed interval of length $\ell$, so $\lambda^*(U)\le\ell$. If
$A\subseteq\bigcup_i U_i$ is any countable cover, then $\lambda^*(A)\le\sum_i\lambda^*(U_i)\le\sum_i\operatorname{diam}U_i$. Taking the infimum over covers
by sets of diameter at most $\delta$ yields $\lambda^*(A)\le\mathcal H^1_\delta(A)$ for every $\delta$, hence $\lambda^*(A)\le\mathcal H^1(A)$.
This comparison does not identify $\mathcal H^1$ with Lebesgue measure. It only bounds one outer measure by the other. If $\lambda^*(A)>0$,
then $\mathcal H^1(A)>0$, so $\dim_H A\ge 1$. Part (a) supplies the matching upper bound, and $\dim_H A=1$.

(c) Since $s<1$, one has $1/s>1$, so $r=2^{-1/s}<2^{-1}=\frac12$ and $2r<1$. The construction keeps, inside each current interval, the two
closed end subintervals of relative length $r$, and it discards the open middle. Stage $k$ is a union of $2^k$ closed intervals of length
$r^k$, and the total length is $(2r)^k\to 0$. Thus $\lambda(E_s)=0$, and $E_s$ is closed, being the intersection of the stages.

The exponent was chosen so that $2r^s=1$. The stage-$k$ intervals cover $E_s$, and $2^k(r^k)^s=1$, so $\mathcal H^s(E_s)\le 1$.

For the lower bound, let $\mu$ give mass $2^{-k}$ to each stage-$k$ interval, as in the Cantor law. The closest pair of stage-$k$ intervals
consists of the two children of one parent: the discarded middle of that parent has length $r^{k-1}(1-2r)>0$. Intervals that split apart at an
earlier stage are separated by a coarser gap, and a coarser gap is longer because $r<1$. So every two stage-$k$ intervals are at least
$r^{k-1}(1-2r)$ apart. A closed set $U$ of smaller diameter meets at most one of them.

The lemma only has to be checked for small sets. Take $0<\operatorname{diam}U<1-2r$, and choose the integer $k\ge 1$ with
\[r^{k}(1-2r)\le\operatorname{diam}U<r^{k-1}(1-2r).\]
The right-hand inequality is the separation just proved, so $\mu(U)\le 2^{-k}$. The relation $2r^s=1$ rewrites the mass as $(r^k)^s$. The
left-hand inequality gives $r^k\le\operatorname{diam}U/(1-2r)$, and therefore
\[\mu(U)\le(1-2r)^{-s}(\operatorname{diam}U)^s.\]
A point has $\mu$-measure $0$, since it sits in an interval of mass $2^{-k}$ for every $k$. Every closed set of diameter at most $(1-2r)/2$
falls under the estimate above. Lemma~\ref{lem:massdist}, with that constant and that $\delta$, gives $\mathcal H^s(E_s)>0$. With the covering
upper bound, $\dim_H E_s=s$.

Each $E_{1-1/n}$ is null, so $A=\bigcup_{n\ge 2}E_{1-1/n}$ is null. Exercise~\ref{ex:lipschitzdim} gives
$\dim_H A=\sup_{n\ge 2}\bigl(1-\frac1n\bigr)=1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v7c12d}
\idea The diagonal map $x\mapsto(x,\ldots,x)$ pushes $\nu$ forward to a law on $\R^n$. Consistency is the observation that forgetting the last
coordinate of a constant vector leaves a constant vector. The extension is then one application of Theorem~\ref{thm:kolmogorov}. The product
measure gives a different value to the cylinder $\{x_1=x_2\}$, unless $\nu$ is a single atom.
\writeup (a) Let $\iota_n:\R\to\R^n$ be $\iota_n(x)=(x,\ldots,x)$, and set $\mu_n=\nu\circ\iota_n^{-1}$. If $B\subseteq\R^n$ is Borel, then
\[\mu_{n+1}(B\times\R)=\nu\bigl(\{x:\iota_{n+1}(x)\in B\times\R\}\bigr)=\nu\bigl(\{x:\iota_n(x)\in B\}\bigr)=\mu_n(B),\]
because $\iota_n(x)$ is the first $n$ coordinates of $\iota_{n+1}(x)$. The family is consistent. Theorem~\ref{thm:kolmogorov} supplies a unique
probability $\mu$ on the product $\sigma$-algebra of $\R^\N$ with these finite-dimensional laws.

(b) For $i<j$, the set $\{x:x_i\ne x_j\}$ equals $\pi_j^{-1}(\{y\in\R^j:y_i\ne y_j\})$, a cylinder. Under $\mu_j$ the coordinates are two copies of
the same draw, so they are equal with probability $1$, and the cylinder has measure $0$. The non-constant sequences are the countable union of
these cylinders over $1\le i<j<\infty$. Thus $\mu$ gives mass $0$ to the non-constant sequences, and mass $1$ to the constant sequences.

(c) Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:kolmogorov} to the product laws $\nu^{\otimes n}$. The text after the theorem records that the extension $\rho$ is
the law of an independent sequence with each coordinate distributed as $\nu$. The one-dimensional marginals of $\mu$ are all equal to $\nu$,
because $\mu(\pi_1^{-1}(B))=\mu_1(B)=\nu(B)$, and likewise for every later coordinate by consistency. The same is true of $\rho$.

It remains to separate $\mu$ from $\rho$ when $\nu$ is not a Dirac mass. One has $\mu(x_1=x_2)=1$ by part (b). For the product,
\[(\nu\otimes\nu)(\{(t,t):t\in\R\})=\int\nu(\{t\})\,d\nu(t)=\sum_a\nu(\{a\})^2,\]
the sum running over the atoms of $\nu$. Write $p_a=\nu(\{a\})$, so $\sum_a p_a\le 1$. If some $p_a>\frac12$, that atom is unique, and it is
strictly less than $1$ because $\nu$ is not a Dirac mass. Then $\sum_a p_a^2\le p_a<1$. If every atom has mass at most $\frac12$, then
$\sum_a p_a^2\le\frac12\sum_a p_a\le\frac12<1$. Either way $\rho(x_1=x_2)<1=\mu(x_1=x_2)$, so $\mu\ne\rho$.
\end{solution}
