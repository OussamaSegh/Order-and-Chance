# Description

What is a real number, a limit, a length? The earlier volumes used the words and left the definitions standing. The answers, found between 1858 and 1933, are built here, and every step is proved.

The book begins with sets. The natural numbers are the smallest inductive set, the integers and the rationals are pairs, and the reals are limits of rational Cauchy sequences. The least upper bound property, an axiom in the first volume, becomes a theorem. Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the rationals can be listed and the reals cannot. A distance is then enough for a limit. Metric spaces, completeness and Banach's fixed point; the \(p\)-adic numbers as a second completion of the rationals; topology, connectedness and compactness in the form later volumes use; Baire's theorem, and the fact that a typical continuous function has a derivative at no point. The first part ends with the Jordan curve theorem: a closed curve that does not cross itself has an inside and an outside. The proof treats polygons first, then winding numbers, then a fixed point on the disc.

The second half measures. Lebesgue's length, Carathéodory's extension, and Vitali's set, which has no length. The integral that slices the values of a function instead of the axis, with the monotone and dominated convergence theorems. Fubini and Tonelli, the change of variables, the volume of the ball in every dimension, and the Beta and Gamma functions. The spaces \(L^p\), their completeness, and convolution. Signed measures, the Radon–Nikodym theorem, Lebesgue's differentiation theorem, and conditional expectation. The Riesz representation theorem for positive functionals on a compact metric space, weak convergence, and Prokhorov's theorem for tight laws on Euclidean space. Kolmogorov's extension theorem for consistent laws on \(\R\). Hausdorff dimension, with the Cantor set of dimension \(\log 2/\log 3\) and the Sierpiński carpet of dimension \(\log 8/\log 3\). The general fact that Hausdorff measure is a Borel measure is quoted, not proved. A last chapter of problems asks what almost every real number looks like, and proves Khinchin's theorem on approximation by rationals.

The summits are these: the construction of the reals, Baire's theorem on nowhere differentiable functions, the existence of a non-measurable set, the dominated convergence theorem, the Jordan curve theorem, the completeness of \(L^p\), the Radon–Nikodym theorem, and the dimension of the Cantor set.

Every theorem is proved in full. A result quoted only for its interest is marked as quoted, and nothing proved here depends on it. Every chapter opens with the question and the people, and closes with exercises, each with a hint and a solution that explains how to find the argument.

Written for the reader who wants to understand, not to pass an examination.
