# Description

What is a real number, a limit, a length? Mathematics used these words for two thousand years before anyone could say what they meant. The answers were found between 1858 and 1933, and they are among the most beautiful constructions in the subject. This book tells that story and proves every step of it.

It begins with nothing but sets, builds the real line from Cauchy sequences of rationals, and shows that infinity comes in sizes: the rationals can be listed, the reals cannot. It then frees the idea of a limit from coordinates. Metric spaces, completeness and the contraction principle; topology, connectedness and compactness in the form that actually gets used; and Baire's theorem, with its startling consequence that the typical continuous function has a derivative at no point at all. The first part ends with the Jordan curve theorem: a closed curve that does not cross itself has an inside and an outside.

The second half measures. Lebesgue's length and the set that has none; the integral that slices the values instead of the axis, and the convergence theorems that make it the right one; Fubini and the change of variables, the volume of the ball in every dimension, the Beta and Gamma functions; the spaces of integrable functions, their completeness and their convolution; densities as derivatives of measures, through Radon-Nikodym and Lebesgue decomposition; weak convergence and tightness, the language in which probability states its limit theorems; and finally fractals, whose dimension is not a whole number, with the Hausdorff measure that detects it. A last chapter of problems asks what almost every real number looks like, and proves Khinchin's theorem on approximation by fractions.

The summits are these: the construction of the reals, Baire's theorem on nowhere differentiable functions, the existence of a non-measurable set, the dominated convergence theorem, the Jordan curve theorem, the completeness of the spaces of integrable functions, the Radon-Nikodym theorem, and the dimension of the Cantor set and the Sierpinski carpet.

Every theorem, including the ones usually waved through, is proved in full. The few results quoted for their interest are marked as quoted. Every chapter opens with the story and the people behind the idea, and closes with exercises chosen for their beauty, each with a hint and a solution that explains how to find the argument rather than only how to check it.

Written for the reader who wants to understand, not to pass an examination.
