\chapter*{Preface}
Compute $1^3+2^3+\dots+10^3$. The sum is $3025$. So is $(1+2+\dots+10)^2$.
The sum $1+\dots+10$ is $55$, and $55^2=3025$. Try further values and the same agreement appears. Does the pattern hold for every positive integer, or only for the values you have checked?

A longer list of checks still only checks those cases. A proof has to say why the pattern cannot fail, and it has to say so in a way you can test line by line.

That is what this volume is for. The series is \emph{Order and Chance: The Hidden Architecture of Mathematics}. Order, here, is a pattern that has to hold in every case, such as the sum of cubes. The picture on the left is still only a list of checks. On the right, $n$ guests collect their hats at random: does anyone receive their own? The picture shows one return in which nobody does. Chance is the proportion of all returns that look like the picture. The count of those returns comes later. In both cases you say what the sentence claims, and you prove that claim.

You need secondary-school algebra. That means arithmetic with fractions, powers, equations and inequalities, and some familiarity with graphs and elementary geometry. You do not need a previous course in proof, calculus, linear algebra or probability. The language chapter starts from what a mathematical sentence means. Later chapters rebuild a tool before they use it. Some three-star problems take sustained thought. That difficulty is part of the work. It is not a sign that a prerequisite was skipped.

The cube sum comes back in the next chapter, as a claim about every positive integer rather than ten checks. Counting follows, and the hats in the picture are one of the things to be counted. Only after that count exists does the proportion become a probability. Completeness of the real numbers is assumed before any existence proof that needs it, and the rules of calculus are proved before a derivative is used to compare two quantities. A few early problems borrow a tool from later in the book. Each one says so. Read the question, and return when the tool is there.

\section*{The foundational contract}
A book of first principles has to say where it starts. Euclid's \emph{Elements} opens Book~I by writing down what the proofs are allowed to use. One common notion, in Heath's translation, is that things equal to the same thing are equal to one another. A later proposition may use that list. It may not quietly add to it. What follows is our list, for the mathematics of this volume.

We use classical logic, ordinary set notation, and the standard arithmetic of the natural numbers $\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, the integers $\Z$, the rationals $\Q$ and the reals $\R$. Their usual inclusions and ordered-field arithmetic are assumed. We assume well ordering of $\N$: every nonempty subset has a least element. Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} states that fact and derives the induction principles from it, together with the elementary divisibility facts that chapter uses.

For the reals we assume completeness: every nonempty set that is bounded above has a least upper bound. From that one assumption the book proves the convergence criteria it needs, the intermediate-value and extreme-value theorems, uniform continuity on a closed bounded interval, Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem. Those conclusions are not assumed separately. The short derivative toolkit is in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}. The full rules of differentiation, with their proofs, are in Chapter~\ref{ch:calculus}.

We admit the elementary functions $\exp$, $\ln$, $\cos$ and $\sin$, on their usual real domains, with their addition laws and derivatives: $\exp'=\exp$, $\ln'(x)=1/x$ for $x>0$, $\sin'=\cos$ and $\cos'=-\sin$. We assume $\exp(0)=1$, that $\exp$ is a bijection from $\R$ onto the positive reals with inverse $\ln$, and the usual unit-circle normalisation and period $2\pi$ of sine and cosine. When a power series is used to compute one of these functions, the text justifies that use. The series is not a substitute for an unstated construction of the function.

We also assume the integral of a continuous real function on a closed bounded interval, with linearity, preservation of order, additivity over adjacent intervals, and the integral of a constant. The fundamental theorem, integration by parts, change of variables and the approximation estimates used here are proved from that assumption. An improper integral is a limit. It is not an extra permission to interchange two infinite operations.

Volume~7, \emph{Spaces: Topology, Measure and Limits}, returns to sets, the number systems and the integral, and builds them from earlier ground. Volume~5, \emph{Calculation: From Formula to Insight}, carries series, integrals and the functions built from them further, as methods. Neither development is used here as an unproved lemma.

Probability starts with finite spaces. Examples with a countably infinite set of outcomes are marked as an extension. They use convergent sums and truncation. The construction of a measure on a space of infinite sequences belongs to later volumes. A challenge note that points beyond this volume says which part is proved here and which part is an outlook, and it carries a reference. Historical sources accompany the chapter openings. The schoolroom story about Gauss, for example, is told with the early account separated from later embellishments.

\section*{How to use the book}
Each chapter begins with a mathematical question, sometimes with the history of that question. Read the from-scratch material with a pencil. In a Pause and think box, make a prediction before you read the argument that follows. A workshop shows how a proof is found: examples, a failed attempt, and a change of language that makes the obstruction visible.

Exercises have three levels. One star builds technique and precision. Two stars ask you to choose a method, or to join several steps. Three stars ask for a longer discovery. Every numbered exercise has a separate hint and a complete solution. \emph{How to find it} explains the search. \emph{Write-up} gives the argument that survives that search. Try the problem before you read either. A challenge box may point to a result beyond this volume. Its notes say which parts are elementary and which are outlooks.

For a first reading, follow the chapter order, attempt the one-star exercises, and work through the workshops. For a second reading, add the two-star exercises and the bridge problems, which join tools that first looked unrelated.

If you are reading this volume in order to start Volume~2, spend time on the language of proof, induction, counting, roots of unity, polynomial arithmetic, series, and estimates, including the short calculus those estimates use. Linear algebra through the spectral theorem, and a little finite probability, come back in bridge problems there. The field guide at the end is for the next problem that looks unfamiliar.

This book is Volume~1, \emph{First Principles}. The subtitle is \emph{Proof, Problem Solving and Mathematical Thinking}. Other volumes follow. You do not need them to read this one.

The next book is Volume~2, \emph{The Integers: Primes, Patterns and Proofs}. A prime is easy to define and hard to command. This volume does not prove the laws of the primes. It gives you the language, the induction and the estimates those proofs use. The language chapter fixes what ``for every'' means. The chapter after it puts ``for every'' to work on the sum of cubes.
