\chapter*{Preface}
Add up the first ten cubes: $1+8+27+\dots+1000=3025$. Now add the first ten numbers and square the answer: $1+2+\dots+10=55$, and $55^2=3025$. The two numbers agree. Try $n=4$ and they agree again: both sides are $100$. Do they agree for every $n$, or only for the ones you happened to try?

Here is a different kind of question. Four guests leave their hats at the door, and the hats come back in random order. How often does nobody get their own hat back? There are $24$ ways to hand back four hats, and in $9$ of them every guest walks off with the wrong one. That is $9/24$, a little over a third. With ten guests, or ten million, the answer barely moves.

The two questions want different kinds of answer. The cubes make a claim about every $n$, and no pile of examples settles that. You can check a million cases and the next one is still unchecked. What settles it is an argument that shows why the agreement cannot stop. The hats make a claim about a proportion. Here nobody asks whether it always happens. The question is how often it happens, and why that fraction settles where it does. The series is called \emph{Order and Chance: The Hidden Architecture of Mathematics}, and those are its two kinds of sentence: patterns that have to hold in every case, and proportions that hold across all the cases together. Each kind needs a proof, and by the end of this book you will have proved both of these.

\section*{What this book is for}
This book teaches you to do three things. You will learn to read a mathematical sentence and say exactly what it claims. You will learn to look for a proof, which usually means trying small cases, making a guess, getting stuck, and finding a better way to say the problem. And you will learn to write the proof down so that someone else can check every line.

You need secondary-school algebra and nothing more: fractions, powers, equations, inequalities, and a little feeling for graphs and elementary geometry. You do not need a previous course in proof, calculus, linear algebra or probability. The derivative and the integral are taught in this book, from a picture and a definition, in the chapter on sequences. You are not expected to have met them before that page. The number $e$ is defined below, before any chapter uses it. Every other tool on the main reading path is built here before it is used. A few clearly marked bridges are meant to be read again after a later chapter; they are not hidden entrance requirements. Some problems will still take you an afternoon. That is the work itself. It does not mean you missed something earlier.

Along the way you will meet induction and binomial coefficients, counting, limits and series, complex numbers and roots of unity, polynomials, linear algebra, calculus, inequalities and convexity, and first probability. Each subject gets one job: to give you a tool you can prove things with. None of them is treated in the depth a specialist course would give it. Later volumes take that up. What you get here is enough of each to use it honestly, and the habit of asking what each step is allowed to assume.

The book is not a survey, and it is not a collection of tricks. A result appears here because you can prove it from what came before, or because the text says plainly that it cannot be proved yet.

\section*{What we take for granted}
Every proof starts somewhere. Euclid's \emph{Elements} opens by listing what its proofs may use. One of the common notions, in Heath's translation, is that things equal to the same thing are equal to one another. Later propositions may use the list. They may not add to it quietly.

Here is the list in ordinary words. You can read past the boxed record on a first pass. Each item is opened again in the chapter that uses it.

We use the logic of ``and'', ``or'', ``not'' and ``if \dots then'', and the numbers you already calculate with: the natural numbers $0,1,2,\ldots$, the integers, the fractions, and the real numbers, with the usual addition, multiplication and order. Every nonempty collection of natural numbers has a least element. The sums chapter turns that sentence into induction.

One extra fact about the real line is assumed. A nonempty collection of real numbers that has some upper bound has a least upper bound. The sequences chapter explains the words and builds limits from that sentence.

You may treat the exponential, the natural logarithm, sine and cosine as known functions, with the addition formulas and the unit circle of radius $1$. Write $e$ for the value of the exponential at $1$. Then $e\approx 2.718$, and $e^x$ means the exponential of $x$. The word \emph{derivative} is defined in the sequences chapter, from the slope of a graph. You do not need it before then. Once it has been defined, we take four formulas as named facts about these four functions: the derivative of the exponential is itself, the derivative of $\ln x$ is $1/x$ for $x>0$, the derivative of sine is cosine, and the derivative of cosine is $-\sin$. The book does not ask you to have proved those four lines in an earlier course.

The integral of a continuous function is the signed area under its graph. The sequences chapter draws that area, with rectangles, before the symbol is used in an argument. Four rules are then taken as known: the area respects sums and constant multiples, it respects inequalities, it adds when you place intervals side by side, and a constant height $c$ on an interval of length $b-a$ gives area $c(b-a)$. A later volume builds the area from the rectangles. We do not use that construction as a hidden lemma.

Probability in this book starts with finitely many outcomes, so every probability is a finite sum. A few marked examples allow an infinite list of outcomes that you can number $1,2,3,\ldots$. An unending sequence of coin tosses needs a later volume. When a challenge points past this book, it says which part is proved here and where to read on.

History comes with sources. When the sums chapter tells the schoolroom story about the young Gauss, it keeps the earliest account apart from what was added later.

\begin{remark}[for the record]
The same list, in the notation the proofs quote. The natural numbers are $\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, the positive integers $\N^*=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, and $\Z\subset\Q\subset\R$ are the integers, the rationals and the reals. Well ordering of $\N$, and completeness of $\R$, are the two sentences in the paragraphs above. Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} derives induction from well ordering. Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences} defines the limit, the derivative and the integral, and proves the convergence tests, the intermediate-value and extreme-value theorems, uniform continuity on a closed bounded interval, Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem.

The standing assumptions on the elementary functions, used only after those words have been defined, are $\exp'=\exp$, $\ln'(x)=1/x$ for $x>0$, $\sin'=\cos$, $\cos'=-\sin$, together with $\exp(0)=1$, the fact that $\exp$ matches each real number with exactly one positive real and has inverse $\ln$, and the usual unit-circle values and period $2\pi$ for sine and cosine. We write $e=\exp(1)$ and $e^x=\exp(x)$. When a power series is used to compute one of these functions, the text proves that the series gives the right value.

The integral of a continuous function on a closed bounded interval is linear, preserves order, adds over adjacent intervals, and sends the constant $c$ on $[a,b]$ to $c(b-a)$. An integral over an unbounded interval, or up to a point where the function blows up, is a limit of ordinary integrals. It does not by itself allow two infinite processes to be swapped.
\end{remark}

\section*{How to read it}
Read with a pencil. When a Pause and think box asks a question, write down your guess before you read on. A wrong guess you made yourself teaches more than a right answer you only read. A workshop shows how a proof is found, not just how it looks at the end: a few examples, an attempt that fails, and the change of viewpoint that shows why it failed.

Exercises come in three levels. One star builds technique and care. Two stars ask you to choose a method or join several steps. Three stars ask for a longer piece of discovery. Every numbered exercise has a hint and a full solution, kept separate. In each solution, \emph{How to find it} shows the search and \emph{Write-up} gives the argument that came out of it. Try the problem before you read either.

\subsection*{Two routes through the book}

The \emph{foundations route} is for a first reading. Read the chapters in order, including the worked examples and the proof workshops. The chapter on orders uses the counting chapter and does not use limits, so you can read it immediately after counting if you prefer, and return to sequences afterwards. Try the one-star exercises before looking at their hints. In the counting chapter, Exercise~\ref{ex:count-paths-first} is a short stepping stone to the Catalan paths. If a two- or three-star problem stops you for an hour, leave it marked and carry on. This first route is complete without specialist research problems or results from later volumes. You do not need to finish every exercise to continue, or to begin Volume~2.

The \emph{exploration route} is for a second reading, or for a reader who already knows some of the subject. Return to the two-star exercises and then choose three-star problems that interest you. The stars describe the work required, not the value of a problem. Some of the later bridge problems borrow a theorem from another chapter or volume. Their statements say where that tool comes from. Read the tool first, or treat the problem as a preview; it is not an unannounced prerequisite for the next chapter.

Here is a way to tackle either route. For a count, list the smallest objects and decide whether the cases overlap. For an induction, write the first case and the exact statement you will carry to the next. For a limit, name the quantity you need to make small. For a difficult exercise, try one of these smaller jobs before reading its hint. The solution then shows not just which calculation worked, but how it was found.

The field guide at the end brings these habits together for a problem that does not come with a chapter name.

This is Volume~1, \emph{First Principles}: \emph{Proof, Problem Solving and Mathematical Thinking}. You can read it without any other volume. It starts before the cubes, with a smaller question: what does a mathematical sentence say? The equation $x^2=1$ asks three different questions depending on its domain and the words around it, and the next chapter reads all three. The chapter after that proves the cube identity for every $n$, four different ways. The hats wait for the counting chapter, which counts them exactly, and the probability chapter, which shows why the proportion settles near $1/e$. Here $e=\exp(1)\approx 2.718$, so $1/e\approx 0.368$. After this book comes Volume~2, \emph{The Integers: Primes, Patterns and Proofs}. A prime is easy to define and hard to control, and this volume stops before those proofs begin.
