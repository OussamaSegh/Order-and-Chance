\chapter*{Preface}
Compute $1^3+2^3+\dots+10^3$. The sum is $3025$. So is $(1+2+\dots+10)^2$.
The sum $1+\dots+10$ is $55$, and $55^2=3025$. Try further values and the same agreement appears. Does the pattern hold for every positive integer, or only for the values you have checked?

A longer list of checks still only checks those cases. A proof has to say why the pattern cannot fail, and it has to say so in a way you can test line by line.

That is what this volume is for. The series is \emph{Order and Chance: The Hidden Architecture of Mathematics}. Order, here, is a pattern that has to hold in every case, such as the sum of cubes. The picture on the left is still only a list of checks. Chance is a count of outcomes, such as the hats: one drawing is one outcome, and the count of all of them is a later question. In both cases you say what the sentence claims, and you prove that claim.

You need secondary-school algebra. That means arithmetic with fractions, powers, equations and inequalities, and some familiarity with graphs and elementary geometry. You do not need a previous course in proof, calculus, linear algebra or probability. The language chapter starts from what a mathematical sentence means. Later chapters rebuild a tool before they use it. Some three-star problems take sustained thought. That difficulty is part of the work. It is not a sign that a prerequisite was skipped.

\section*{The route through the volume}
Each chapter starts from one question, and then builds the tool that answers it. There are eleven chapters, numbered from $0$ to $10$. They sit in four parts.

\paragraph{The Language of Proof.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:language} fixes statements, quantifiers, sets, functions, relations, orders and the shapes of a proof. The question is: what does a mathematical sentence promise?

\paragraph{Counting and Sums.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} treats sums, induction, binomial coefficients and finite differences. The question is why a pattern seen in the first few positive integers holds for all of them.
Chapter~\ref{ch:counting} turns those tools into counting, including lattice paths, a first generating function, and the hats. Its question is how to count once when the restrictions overlap.

\paragraph{Algebra, Limits and Functions.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences} builds real convergence from completeness, then continuity, series and a short derivative and mean-value toolkit. The question is what it means for infinitely many numbers to have a sum.
Chapter~\ref{ch:orders} introduces partial orders and proves Dilworth's theorem, Sperner's theorem, and the monotone-subsequence theorem. A counting proof of that last theorem is an exercise in Chapter~\ref{ch:counting}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:complex} studies complex numbers and roots of unity. The question is how multiplication turns the plane.
Chapter~\ref{ch:polynomials} develops polynomials, interpolation and factorisation. The question is when a polynomial identity follows from finitely many checks.
Chapter~\ref{ch:linear} builds linear algebra from spaces and maps through orthogonality, the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, and approximation. The question is what a matrix knows that its entries hide.
Chapter~\ref{ch:calculus} gives the rules of differentiation and integration, Taylor formulas and certified errors. The question is how large the error is when a function is replaced by a polynomial.

\paragraph{Chance and Inequality.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:inequalities} develops inequalities and convexity as tools for estimates. The question is which comparisons convexity forces.
Chapter~\ref{ch:probability} builds elementary probability on the preceding mathematics and proves a first law of large numbers. The question is what a finite experiment says about the long run. The hats return there, as a probability, after the counting chapter has counted them.

The order of the chapters follows the dependence of the tools. Completeness comes before the extreme-value arguments used for polynomials. Linear maps come before determinants. The calculus comes before convexity proved by derivatives. A finite identity and its limiting consequence stay visibly distinct. A few early problems use a tool proved only later. Each such problem says so. You can read an early integral or matrix question for its meaning, and complete it after the chapter that supplies the tool.

\section*{The foundational contract}
A book of first principles has to say where it starts. Euclid's \emph{Elements} opens Book~I by writing down what the proofs are allowed to use. One common notion, in Heath's translation, is that things equal to the same thing are equal to one another. A later proposition may use that list. It may not quietly add to it. What follows is our list, for the mathematics of this volume.

We use classical logic, ordinary set notation, and the standard arithmetic of the natural numbers $\N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, the integers $\Z$, the rationals $\Q$ and the reals $\R$. Their usual inclusions and ordered-field arithmetic are assumed. We assume well ordering of $\N$: every nonempty subset has a least element. Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} states that fact and derives the induction principles from it, together with the elementary divisibility facts that chapter uses.

For the reals we assume completeness: every nonempty set that is bounded above has a least upper bound. From that one assumption the book proves the convergence criteria it needs, the intermediate-value and extreme-value theorems, uniform continuity on a closed bounded interval, Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem. Those conclusions are not assumed separately. The short derivative toolkit is in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}. The full rules of differentiation, with their proofs, are in Chapter~\ref{ch:calculus}.

We admit the elementary functions $\exp$, $\ln$, $\cos$ and $\sin$, on their usual real domains, with their addition laws and derivatives: $\exp'=\exp$, $\ln'(x)=1/x$ for $x>0$, $\sin'=\cos$ and $\cos'=-\sin$. We assume $\exp(0)=1$, that $\exp$ is a bijection from $\R$ onto the positive reals with inverse $\ln$, and the usual unit-circle normalisation and period $2\pi$ of sine and cosine. When a power series is used to compute one of these functions, the text justifies that use. The series is not a substitute for an unstated construction of the function.

We also assume the integral of a continuous real function on a closed bounded interval, with linearity, preservation of order, additivity over adjacent intervals, and the integral of a constant. The fundamental theorem, integration by parts, change of variables and the approximation estimates used here are proved from that assumption. An improper integral is a limit. It is not an extra permission to interchange two infinite operations.

Volume~7, \emph{Spaces: Topology, Measure and Limits}, returns to sets, the number systems and the integral, and builds them from earlier ground. Volume~5, \emph{Calculation: From Formula to Insight}, carries series, integrals and the functions built from them further, as methods. Neither development is used here as an unproved lemma.

Probability starts with finite spaces. Examples with a countably infinite set of outcomes are marked as an extension. They use convergent sums and truncation. The construction of a measure on a space of infinite sequences belongs to later volumes. A challenge note that points beyond this volume says which part is proved here and which part is an outlook, and it carries a reference. Historical sources accompany the chapter openings. The schoolroom story about Gauss, for example, is told with the early account separated from later embellishments.

\section*{How to use the book}
Each chapter begins with a mathematical question, sometimes with the history of that question. Read the from-scratch material with a pencil. In a Pause and think box, make a prediction before you read the argument that follows. A workshop shows how a proof is found: examples, a failed attempt, and a change of language that makes the obstruction visible.

Exercises have three levels. One star builds technique and precision. Two stars ask you to choose a method, or to join several steps. Three stars ask for a longer discovery. Every numbered exercise has a separate hint and a complete solution. \emph{How to find it} explains the search. \emph{Write-up} gives the argument that survives that search. Try the problem before you read either. A challenge box may point to a result beyond this volume. Its notes say which parts are elementary and which are outlooks.

For a first reading, follow the chapter order, attempt the one-star exercises, and work through the workshops. For a second reading, add the two-star exercises and the bridge problems, which join tools that first looked unrelated.

If you are reading this volume in order to start Volume~2, spend time on the language of proof, induction, counting, roots of unity, polynomial arithmetic, series, and estimates, including the short calculus those estimates use. Linear algebra through the spectral theorem, and a little finite probability, come back in bridge problems there. The field guide at the end is for the next problem that looks unfamiliar.

\section*{From this volume to the series}
This book is Volume~1, \emph{First Principles}. Its subtitle is \emph{Proof, Problem Solving and Mathematical Thinking}. The fifteen volumes form six parts. The list below says where a tool from this volume is used again. You do not need those later books in order to read this one.

The next book is Volume~2, \emph{The Integers: Primes, Patterns and Proofs}. It asks when a pattern in the whole numbers is a law. Divisibility is the first tool, and the primes are the objects on which that standard becomes hard. This volume does not prove those laws. It builds the language, the induction and the estimates those proofs will need.

\begin{description}
\item[Part I. Foundations.]
Volume~2 is the integers, as above.
Volume~3, \emph{Symmetry: Groups, Rings and Fields}, uses functions, equivalence relations, fields of coefficients, linear maps and roots of unity.
Volume~4, \emph{Chance: The Logic of Uncertainty}, uses counting, finite probability, expectation, variance, convexity and convergence, and then goes on to infinite experiments.
\item[Part II. Bridges.]
Volume~5, \emph{Calculation: From Formula to Insight}, uses series, summation by parts, Taylor errors, integration and inequalities as methods.
Volume~6, \emph{Crossroads: Arithmetic Meets Analysis}, uses those estimates at the first meeting of arithmetic and probability.
\item[Part III. Analysis and Geometry.]
Volume~7, \emph{Spaces: Topology, Measure and Limits}, rebuilds sets, functions, completeness and the integral as topology, measure and limits.
Volume~8, \emph{Analysis: Operators, Spectra and Transforms}, extends limits, complex numbers and linear maps to operators, spectra and transforms.
Volume~9, \emph{Shapes: The Geometry of Connection}, uses the linear algebra and the estimates of this volume as local language for geometry.
Volume~10, \emph{Abstraction: Categories, Algebra and Homology}, reads maps and equivalence one level up.
\item[Part IV. Randomness.]
Volume~11, \emph{Randomness: Martingales, Limits and Ergodicity}, uses expectation, variance and convergence in the form needed for martingales, limit theorems and ergodic theory.
Volume~12, \emph{Random Motion: The Geometry of Random Paths}, follows probabilistic estimates along paths, together with the spectral theorem.
\item[Part V. Number.]
Volume~13, \emph{Arithmetic: From Equations to Hidden Worlds}, uses polynomials, roots of unity and factorisation inside number fields.
Volume~14, \emph{The Primes: Their Hidden Rhythm}, aims series, sums and inequalities at the primes.
\item[Part VI. Synthesis.]
Volume~15, \emph{Order and Chance: The Meeting of Structure and Randomness}, uses finite probability and integer counting to study typical integers.
\end{description}

The way on from here is Volume~2. A prime is easy to define and hard to command. The proofs that handle them use what you are about to practise, starting with what the words ``for every'' mean. The language chapter fixes that phrase. The chapter after it puts them to the sum of cubes.
