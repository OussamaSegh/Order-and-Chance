\chapter{Polynomials}\label{ch:polynomials}

\begin{center}\itshape
Among all monic polynomials of degree $n$, which one stays closest to $0$ on $[-1,1]$?\\
And how can the roots of a polynomial compute $1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\cdots$?
\end{center}

A polynomial is a piece of order. A finite list of coefficients decides its values, its roots, and every symmetric expression in those roots. Chance comes in when a list of values is mistaken for a forecast, or when a secret is hidden by choosing the other coefficients at random. Both mistakes, and both successes, use the same bound: a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots.

In 1650 Pietro Mengoli, a priest and professor in Bologna, published \emph{Novae quadraturae arithmeticae}. He could sum $\frac1{1\cdot2}+\frac1{2\cdot3}+\frac1{3\cdot4}+\cdots$. It telescopes to $1$, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:sums}. He could not sum
\[
1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\cdots.
\]
Leibniz could not either. Jacob Bernoulli, in his 1689 treatise on infinite series, proved that the sum is less than $2$, and then wrote, in Dunham's translation, ``If anyone finds and communicates to us that which thus far has eluded our efforts, great will be our gratitude.'' The question took the name of Bernoulli's city. It is the Basel problem.

The person who found the sum had been a student of Jacob's brother Johann, and was working in St Petersburg: Leonhard Euler. The paper \emph{De summis serierum reciprocarum} was read to the Academy there on 5 December 1735 and printed in 1740. The idea is outrageous, and it is the right idea. A polynomial with roots $a_1,\dots,a_n$ and constant term $1$ is $\prod_k\bigl(1-\frac x{a_k}\bigr)$. The sum of the reciprocals $\frac1{a_k}$ sits in the coefficient of $x$. Euler treated $\frac{\sin x}x$, whose roots are $\pm\pi,\pm2\pi,\dots$, as if it were a polynomial of infinite degree,
\[
\frac{\sin x}x=\Bigl(1-\frac{x^2}{\pi^2}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{x^2}{4\pi^2}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac{x^2}{9\pi^2}\Bigr)\cdots,
\]
compared the coefficients of $x^2$, and found $\frac{\pi^2}6$. The infinite product was not yet a theorem. Euler later gave other arguments. The value matched every decimal he could compute, and he could compute many. Figure~\ref{fig:sineprod} shows the partial products closing on $\frac{\sin x}x$. This chapter builds the finite tools behind the idea. Exercise~\ref{ex:basel} carries the sum out with a genuine polynomial, of finite degree, whose roots are $\cot^2\frac{k\pi}N$.

Near $0$, the product with $N=5$ is already close. The infinite product is a later theorem. Exercise~\ref{ex:basel} reaches $\frac{\pi^2}6$ with a polynomial of finite degree.}

The other question looks unrelated. It is the same bound, spent on size instead of on a sum. Pafnuty Chebyshev (1821--1894) met it in 1854, in a paper on the linkages of steam engines, \emph{Th\'eorie des m\'ecanismes connus sous le nom de parall\'elogrammes}. Watt's parallelogram was supposed to turn the rocking of a beam into a straight line. How straight can a linkage of a given complexity be? That becomes: which monic polynomial of degree $n$ stays closest to zero on an interval? In a lecture of 1856 on the drawing of maps, he said that the closer mutual approximation of the points of view of theory and practice brings most beneficial results. The polynomial that answers him does not sit still. It hits its own bound as often as a polynomial of degree $n$ is allowed to.

A field, in this chapter, is a number system in which you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide by anything except zero, with the usual laws. Unless a sentence names a finite field, the coefficients lie in $\Q$, $\R$ or $\C$.

\section{Polynomials from scratch}

The first job is to say what a polynomial is, before it is asked to be a function.

\begin{definitionx}
A \emph{polynomial} over a field $\mathbb K$ is a formal expression $P=a_0+a_1X+\dots+a_nX^n$ with coefficients $a_k\in\mathbb K$. Two polynomials are equal when all their coefficients are. If $a_n\ne0$, then $n$ is the \emph{degree} $\deg P$ and $a_n$ is the \emph{leading coefficient}. $P$ is \emph{monic} if $a_n=1$. The zero polynomial has every coefficient $0$ and, by convention, degree $-\infty$. Polynomials are added coefficient by coefficient. In a product $AB$, the coefficient of $X^k$ is $\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j$. The set of all of them is written $\mathbb K[X]$.
\end{definitionx}

The convention $\deg0=-\infty$ makes two rules true with no exceptions: $\deg(A+B)\le\max(\deg A,\deg B)$, and $\deg(AB)=\deg A+\deg B$. The second holds because the leading coefficient of $AB$ is the product of the two leading coefficients, and a product of two nonzero field elements is nonzero. So if $AB=0$, then $A=0$ or $B=0$. And if $AB=AC$ with $A\ne0$, then $B=C$.

Why ``formal''? Because the expression and the function $x\mapsto P(x)$ are different objects. Over $\R$ or $\C$ the difference is harmless: Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor} will show that a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots, so two polynomials that agree as functions on an infinite set are equal as polynomials. Over a finite field the two notions really differ. Over the field with two elements, $X^2+X$ is not the zero polynomial, yet $x^2+x=0$ at both $x=0$ and $x=1$. Figure~\ref{fig:values} is the whole trap. The function has forgotten the coefficients. The polynomial has not. Later volumes work seriously over finite fields. Here the field is infinite unless a sentence says otherwise, as in the secret-sharing example and in reduction modulo a prime.

\begin{examplex}[Horner's scheme]
To evaluate $P=2X^4-3X^3+X-5$ at $x=3$, do not compute the powers of $3$. Write $P=(((2X-3)X+0)X+1)X-5$ and work from the inside:
\[
2,\qquad 2\cdot3-3=3,\qquad 3\cdot3+0=9,\qquad 9\cdot3+1=28,\qquad 28\cdot3-5=79.
\]
So $P(3)=79$, with four multiplications and no separate powers. Figure~\ref{fig:horner} is that arithmetic. The intermediate numbers $2,3,9,28$ are the coefficients of the quotient of $P$ by $X-3$. The last number is the remainder. Both facts are the division algorithm, one step below.
\end{examplex}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-horner}
\caption{Horner's scheme for $2X^4-3X^3+X-5$ at $3$. Each arrow is one multiplication by $3$ and one addition of the next coefficient. The first four boxes are the quotient $2X^3+3X^2+9X+28$. The last box is $P(3)=79$.}
\label{fig:horner}
\end{figure}

The scheme is Horner's in the textbooks. The earliest surviving account is Qin Jiushao's, in the \emph{Shushu Jiuzhang} of 1247. Paolo Ruffini published the same nested multiplication in 1804, and William Horner in 1819. The name that stuck was the last of the three.

\begin{definitionx}[formal derivative]
The derivative of $P=\sum_ka_kX^k$ is $P'=\sum_kka_kX^{k-1}$. The formula defines it. No limit is involved. It obeys $(A+B)'=A'+B'$, $(AB)'=A'B+AB'$ and $(A^m)'=mA^{m-1}A'$.
\end{definitionx}
\begin{proof}[Why the product rule holds]
Both sides of $(AB)'=A'B+AB'$ are linear in $A$ and in $B$, so it is enough to check the rule when $A=X^i$ and $B=X^j$. Then $(X^{i+j})'=(i+j)X^{i+j-1}$, and $A'B+AB'=iX^{i-1}X^j+jX^iX^{j-1}$. The two sides agree. The power rule follows by induction on $m$.
\end{proof}

A nonconstant polynomial is \emph{irreducible over} $\mathbb K$ if it cannot be written as a product of two polynomials in $\mathbb K[X]$ of positive degree. The field matters. $X^2+1$ is irreducible over $\R$, and equals $(X-i)(X+i)$ over $\C$.

\section{Division, roots and multiplicities}

Throughout, $\mathbb K$ is a field. Division is the tool that turns a root into a factor.

\begin{proposition}[Euclidean division]\label{prop:polydiv}
For $A,B\in\mathbb K[X]$ with $B\ne0$ there is a unique pair $(Q,R)$ with $A=BQ+R$ and $\deg R<\deg B$ (and $\deg0=-\infty$).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Existence}, by strong induction on $\deg A$. If $\deg A<\deg B$, take $Q=0$ and $R=A$. Otherwise let $a$ and $b$ be the leading coefficients and set $d=\deg A-\deg B$. Then $A_1=A-\frac abX^dB$ has degree strictly less than $\deg A$, so the induction hypothesis writes $A_1=BQ_1+R$. Hence $A=B\bigl(Q_1+\frac abX^d\bigr)+R$.

\emph{Uniqueness.} If $BQ+R=BQ'+R'$, then $B(Q-Q')=R'-R$. The right side has degree less than $\deg B$. The degree formula forces $Q-Q'=0$, and then $R=R'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{examplex}[long division]
The proof is the algorithm taught at school. Divide $A=X^4+2X^3-X+1$ by $B=X^2+1$. The leading terms give $X^2$. Subtract $X^2B$ and the remainder so far is $2X^3-X^2-X+1$. The leading terms give $2X$. Subtract $2XB$ and what is left is $-X^2-3X+1$. The leading terms give $-1$. Subtract $-B$ and what is left is $-3X+2$, of degree less than $2$. Stop. So $Q=X^2+2X-1$ and $R=-3X+2$. Multiply back: $(X^2+1)(X^2+2X-1)+(-3X+2)=X^4+2X^3-X+1$. That check takes a moment and catches most mistakes.

When $B=X-a$, the same division is Horner's scheme. Dividing $2X^4-3X^3+X-5$ by $X-3$ gives the quotient $2X^3+3X^2+9X+28$ and the remainder $79$, the numbers of Figure~\ref{fig:horner}. The next proposition says why the remainder had to be $P(3)$.
\end{examplex}

\begin{proposition}[roots]\label{prop:rootsfactor}
Let $P\in\mathbb K[X]$ be nonzero of degree $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $a$ is a root of $P$ if and only if $(X-a)$ divides $P$.
\item If $x_1,\dots,x_r$ are distinct roots of $P$, then $\prod_{i=1}^r(X-x_i)$ divides $P$. In particular $P$ has at most $n$ roots.
\item (Characteristic $0$.) Let the \emph{multiplicity} of $a$ be the largest $m$ such that $(X-a)^m$ divides $P$. In characteristic $0$ every $k!$ is invertible in $\mathbb K$, so the Taylor coefficient $P^{(k)}(a)/k!$ is defined. Then $a$ has multiplicity at least $m$ if and only if $P(a)=P'(a)=\dots=P^{(m-1)}(a)=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Divide: $P=(X-a)Q+c$ with $c$ constant. Evaluate at $a$. The first term vanishes, so $c=P(a)$. Thus $X-a$ divides $P$ exactly when $P(a)=0$.

(b) Induction on $r$. By (a), $P=(X-x_r)Q$. For $i<r$, the identity $0=P(x_i)=(x_i-x_r)Q(x_i)$ and the fact $x_i\ne x_r$ give $Q(x_i)=0$, because a field has no zero divisors. By induction, $\prod_{i<r}(X-x_i)$ divides $Q$. Comparing degrees, $r\le n$.

(c) \emph{Taylor's formula for polynomials}: $P=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{P^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(X-a)^k$. Both sides are linear in $P$, so it is enough to check the formula on the basis $(X-a)^j$ for $0\le j\le n$. For that polynomial, the $k$-th derivative at $a$ is $j!$ if $k=j$ and $0$ otherwise. So $(X-a)^m$ divides $P$ if and only if the first $m$ Taylor coefficients vanish: the remaining sum is visibly divisible by $(X-a)^m$. Conversely, if some $P^{(k)}(a)\ne0$ with $k<m$, take the least such $k$. Then $P=(X-a)^kU$ with $U(a)\ne0$. Part (a) says $X-a$ does not divide $U$. If $(X-a)^m$ divided $P$ with $m>k$, cancellation in $\mathbb K[X]$ would give $U=(X-a)^{m-k}Q$, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

Part (b) is the fact behind every ``two polynomials that agree at enough points are equal'' argument in this book. If $P$ and $Q$ have degree at most $n$ and agree at $n+1$ points, then $P-Q$ has $n+1$ roots and degree at most $n$, so $P-Q=0$. Figure~\ref{fig:threepoints} is the case $n=2$. Three nodes leave a polynomial of degree at most $2$ no freedom. A fourth prescribed value is a new problem, not a prediction.

\subsection*{The Euclidean algorithm and B\'ezout's identity}
An integer gcd measures common divisibility. For polynomials the same algorithm works, with degree in place of size.

\begin{proposition}[polynomial gcd and B\'ezout]\label{prop:polynomial-bezout}
For $A,B\in\mathbb K[X]$ not both zero, there is a unique monic polynomial $D$ which divides both and is divisible by every common divisor. Moreover there are $U,V\in\mathbb K[X]$ with $D=UA+VB$. We write $D=\gcd(A,B)$ and call $A,B$ \emph{coprime} when $D=1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Repeatedly divide the preceding remainder by the current nonzero one. The degrees are nonnegative and strictly decrease, so the algorithm stops. Its last nonzero remainder $R$ divides the preceding remainder, and, walking the division identities backwards, divides $A$ and $B$. Conversely every common divisor of $A$ and $B$ divides each remainder, hence divides $R$. Back-substitution writes $R$ as $UA+VB$. Dividing by the nonzero leading coefficient makes $D$ monic and keeps an expression of the same shape. If one input is zero, the same argument starts from the other. Two monic polynomials with the stated divisibility properties divide one another, so they have the same degree and differ by a constant factor. Monicity makes that factor $1$.
\end{proof}

For a check, $(X^3-1)-X(X^2-1)=X-1$ and $X^2-1=(X+1)(X-1)$. The gcd is $X-1$, and the first identity is already a B\'ezout identity. If $\gcd(A,B)=1$ and $A$ divides $BC$, multiply $1=UA+VB$ by $C$. Both terms on the right are divisible by $A$, so $A$ divides $C$. This is polynomial coprime cancellation, the counterpart of the arithmetic fact in Chapter~\ref{ch:sums}. After the d'Alembert--Gauss theorem, two nonzero complex polynomials are coprime exactly when they share no root.

\begin{examplex}[multiple roots]
For which real $a$ does $X^3-3X+a$ have a multiple root? By Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(c), such a root is a common root of $P$ and of $P'=3X^2-3$, so it is $\pm1$. Then $P(1)=a-2$ and $P(-1)=a+2$. The common root exists exactly for $a=\pm2$. Indeed $X^3-3X+2=(X-1)^2(X+2)$ and $X^3-3X-2=(X+1)^2(X-2)$. A multiple root is a coincidence between $P$ and $P'$. Exercise~\ref{ex:disc} packages that coincidence, for a cubic, as one number, the discriminant.
\end{examplex}

\begin{think}
Why must a real polynomial of odd degree have a real root? For a complex polynomial there is no sign change to use. Picture the height $|P(z)|$ over the plane. Far away the height is huge, so there is a lowest point. Why must that lowest point have height $0$?
\end{think}

\section{A root in the complex plane}

The think box is the theorem. Over $\C$, the lowest point of $|P|$ cannot sit at a positive height.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-landscape}
\caption{The height $|z^2-1|$. It rises like $|z|^2$ far away, meets $0$ at the roots $\pm1$, and has a saddle at $0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:dalembert} says this height can never have a lowest point at a positive value: from any point where $P\ne0$ there is a direction in which $|P|$ decreases.}
\label{fig:landscape}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[d'Alembert--Gauss]\label{thm:dalembert}
Every nonconstant $P\in\C[X]$ has a root. Consequently $P$ splits as $c\prod(X-x_i)$ in $\C[X]$. The irreducible polynomials of $\R[X]$ are the degree $1$ polynomials, and the degree $2$ polynomials with negative discriminant.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The minimum on a closed interval was Theorem~\ref{thm:evt} in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}. A closed disc is not an interval, so the same fact needs one extra step. A sequence in a closed disc has bounded real parts. Bolzano--Weierstrass gives a subsequence whose real parts converge, and a further subsequence whose imaginary parts converge. The modulus is continuous, so the limit stays in the disc.

Write $P=\sum_{j=0}^nc_jX^j$ with $n\ge1$ and $c_n\ne0$. Then
\[
|P(z)|\ge|z|^n\Bigl(|c_n|-\sum_{j<n}|c_j|\,|z|^{j-n}\Bigr),
\]
and the expression in parentheses tends to $|c_n|$ as $|z|\to\infty$. So there is $R>0$ with $|P(z)|>|P(0)|$ for $|z|>R$. Let $m$ be the infimum of $|P|$ on the disc $|z|\le R$, and choose $z_n$ in the disc with $|P(z_n)|<m+\frac1n$. The subsequence argument above produces a limit $z_0$ still in the disc, and continuity of $|P|$ gives $|P(z_0)|=m$. That minimum is global, because $|P|$ is larger than $|P(0)|\ge m$ outside the disc.

Suppose $P(z_0)\ne0$. Expand around $z_0$: $P(z_0+h)=P(z_0)+ch^k+h^{k+1}S(h)$ with $c\ne0$, $k\ge1$, and $S$ a polynomial. By Proposition~\ref{prop:expform} there is $u\in\C$ with $u^k=-P(z_0)/c$: write $-P(z_0)/c=\rho e^{i\theta}$ and take $u=\rho^{1/k}e^{i\theta/k}$. For real $t\in\,]0,1[$,
\[
|P(z_0+tu)|=\bigl|P(z_0)(1-t^k)+t^{k+1}u^{k+1}S(tu)\bigr|\le|P(z_0)|(1-t^k)+Mt^{k+1},
\]
with $M=\sup_{0\le t\le1}|u^{k+1}S(tu)|$. Choose $0<t<1$ with $Mt<|P(z_0)|$, which is any such $t$ if $M=0$. Then the right side is strictly less than $|P(z_0)|$. That contradicts the choice of $z_0$. So $P(z_0)=0$.

Splitting follows by induction on the degree, using Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(a) at each root. Over $\R$, take a real polynomial of degree at least $3$. It has a complex root $z$. If $z$ is real, then $X-z$ is a real factor of positive degree less than the degree of $P$. If not, $\bar z$ is also a root, because real coefficients give $\overline{P(z)}=P(\bar z)$. Then $(X-z)(X-\bar z)=X^2-2(\operatorname{Re}z)\,X+|z|^2$ lies in $\R[X]$ and divides $P$ by part (b). The quotient is real as well: dividing by this real monic polynomial inside $\R[X]$ gives a real quotient, which is also the quotient in $\C[X]$ by uniqueness of Euclidean division. So a real polynomial of degree at least $3$ is reducible over $\R$. A real quadratic is irreducible if and only if it has no real root, if and only if its discriminant is negative.
\end{proof}

Check the last sentence on two familiar polynomials. The discriminant of $X^2+1$ is $-4$, so $X^2+1$ is irreducible over $\R$. And $X^3-1=(X-1)(X^2+X+1)$; the quadratic has discriminant $-3$, so this is the complete factorisation over $\R$. Over $\C$ the quadratic splits as well.

The theorem has a long and quarrelsome history. Albert Girard stated in 1629 that an equation of degree $n$ has $n$ roots, without saying what kind of numbers they were. Jean le Rond d'Alembert gave the first serious attempt at a proof in 1746, built on a minimum of $|P|$, but without the compactness fact used above and with a lemma he did not prove. Euler, Lagrange and Laplace followed. Each of them assumed at some point that the roots exist somewhere, and then showed that those roots are complex. Gauss's doctoral dissertation of 1799 took the earlier proofs apart. Of Euler's method he wrote that he blamed the form alone: useful for finding a truth, not fit to stand in public as a proof. His own 1799 argument has gaps by modern standards. He returned to the theorem in 1816, twice, and in 1849. The short proof above is essentially the one Jean-Robert Argand, the bookkeeper of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}, published in 1814. The French call the theorem d'Alembert's. The Germans call it Gauss's. We call it both.

\section{What the coefficients know}

Once the roots exist, the coefficients know their elementary symmetric sums, and nothing more private than that.

\begin{proposition}[Vi\`ete's relations]\label{prop:viete}
If $P=a_nX^n+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\dots+a_0=a_n\prod_{i=1}^n(X-x_i)$ with $a_n\ne0$, the elementary symmetric functions $\sigma_k=\sum_{i_1<\dots<i_k}x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}$ satisfy
\[
\sigma_k=(-1)^k\,\frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}\qquad(1\le k\le n).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand $\prod_i(X-x_i)$. The coefficient of $X^{n-k}$ is obtained by choosing $-x_i$ in exactly $k$ factors, so it equals $(-1)^k\sigma_k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{examplex}
For a quadratic $X^2+bX+c$ with roots $x_1,x_2$, Vi\`ete says $x_1+x_2=-b$ and $x_1x_2=c$. That is how you check a quadratic solution in your head. For $X^3-X+1$, with roots $x_1,x_2,x_3$, Vi\`ete reads off $\sigma_1=0$, $\sigma_2=-1$ and $\sigma_3=-1$. Nobody asks you to find the roots. One of them is real, about $-1.3247$, and the other two are complex. Yet $x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2=\sigma_1^2-2\sigma_2=2$ is exact, and it took one line. Symmetric information about the roots is available without the roots. Section~\ref{sec:symmetric} turns that observation into a theorem about every symmetric polynomial.
\end{examplex}

This is also Euler's move, with the degree kept finite. The coefficient of $x$ in $\prod\bigl(1-\frac x{a_k}\bigr)$ is $-\sum\frac1{a_k}$. Read the coefficient, and you have read the sum of the reciprocals of the roots. Exercise~\ref{ex:basel} does exactly that for the numbers $\cot^2\frac{k\pi}N$.

\section{Lagrange interpolation}

Three values fixed a quadratic in Figure~\ref{fig:threepoints}. The same count builds the polynomial, not only the uniqueness.

\begin{think}
Find the polynomial $P$ of degree at most $2$ with $P(0)=1$, $P(1)=3$ and $P(2)=7$. Try to build it as $1\cdot L_0+3\cdot L_1+7\cdot L_2$, where each $L_i$ vanishes at two of the three points and equals $1$ at the third.
\end{think}

\begin{theorem}[Lagrange]\label{thm:lagrange}
Let $x_0,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb K$ be distinct and $y_0,\dots,y_n\in\mathbb K$. There is a unique $P\in\mathbb K[X]$ of degree at most $n$ with $P(x_i)=y_i$ for all $i$, namely
\[
P=\sum_{i=0}^ny_i\,L_i,\qquad L_i=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{X-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$L_i(x_j)$ is $1$ if $j=i$ and $0$ otherwise, so $P$ fits. If $\tilde P$ also fits, then $P-\tilde P$ has degree at most $n$ and $n+1$ roots, hence is zero by Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-lagrange}
\caption{Left: the Lagrange basis for the nodes $0,1,2$. Each $L_i$ equals $1$ at its own node and $0$ at the other two. Right: the interpolant of the values $1,3,7$ is $L_0+3L_1+7L_2=X^2+X+1$.}
\label{fig:lagrange}
\end{figure}

\begin{examplex}
With nodes $0,1,2$,
\[
L_0=\frac{(X-1)(X-2)}2,\qquad L_1=\frac{X(X-2)}{-1},\qquad L_2=\frac{X(X-1)}2,
\]
and
\[
P=L_0+3L_1+7L_2=\tfrac12(X^2-3X+2)-3(X^2-2X)+\tfrac72(X^2-X)=X^2+X+1.
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:lagrange} is that sum. Check the three nodes: $P(0)=1$, $P(1)=3$, $P(2)=7$. The name is not quite fair. Edward Waring published the formula in 1779, and Euler used it in 1783, before Lagrange taught it at the \'Ecole normale in 1795.
\end{examplex}

The formula answers an exact question: which polynomial of degree at most $n$ has these $n+1$ values? It does not promise that the next value is the next measurement. Every polynomial $P+c\prod(X-x_i)$ has the same values at the nodes, and at a new point it can be anything, by the choice of $c$. The workshop at the end of the chapter makes that warning numerical.

\begin{examplex}[sharing a secret]
In 1979 Adi Shamir turned Theorem~\ref{thm:lagrange} into a way to split a secret. A bank wants its vault code $s$ to be recoverable by any three of its five directors, and by no two of them. Work in the finite field $\mathbb F_p=\Z/p\Z$ for a prime $p>5$, with $s\in\mathbb F_p$. Choose $a$ and $b$ independently and uniformly from the whole field, including zero, and put $P=s+aX+bX^2$, of degree at most $2$. Label the directors by $1,\ldots,5$ in $\mathbb F_p$. None of these labels is $0$, since $p>5$. Hand director $i$ the share $(i,P(i))$. Any three directors determine $P$ by interpolation, and recover $s=P(0)$. A share at $0$ would have been the secret itself.
\end{examplex}

The secrecy is a count, not a mood. Fix the secret $s$ and two distinct directors $i$ and $j$. The map $(a,b)\mapsto\bigl(P(i),P(j)\bigr)$ is a bijection of $\mathbb F_p^2$. The three nodes $(0,s)$, $(i,y_i)$ and $(j,y_j)$ determine exactly one polynomial of degree at most $2$, because the Vandermonde factor $ij(j-i)$ is nonzero. Thus every pair of share values occurs for exactly one $(a,b)$, hence with probability $1/p^2$, whatever $s$ was. The two shares are uniformly distributed and independent of the secret. The sentence ``every candidate secret still fits these two shares'' is the same bijection read in the other order. It is not, by itself, the count of the probabilities. The finite field and the uniform choice are essential. Exercise~\ref{ex:secret} recovers a secret from three shares over the ordinary numbers, where the algebra is the same and the probability statement is not being made.

\section{Chebyshev polynomials}

Write $\cos2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1$ and $\cos3\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta$. Each multiple angle is a polynomial in $\cos\theta$. The pattern is a recurrence.

\begin{think}
Add $\cos((n+1)\theta)$ and $\cos((n-1)\theta)$ with the sum-to-product formula. What recurrence appears for $\cos n\theta$, written as a polynomial in $\cos\theta$?
\end{think}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cheb}
For each $n\in\N$ there is a unique $T_n\in\R[X]$ with $T_n(\cos\theta)=\cos n\theta$ for all $\theta$. Moreover $T_0=1$, $T_1=X$,
\[
T_{n+1}=2X\,T_n-T_{n-1},
\]
$T_n$ has degree $n$, and for $n\ge1$ its leading coefficient is $2^{n-1}$. For these $n$ the roots are $\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2n}$, with $0\le k\le n-1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From $\cos((n+1)\theta)+\cos((n-1)\theta)=2\cos\theta\cos n\theta$, the polynomials defined by the recurrence satisfy $T_n(\cos\theta)=\cos n\theta$ by induction. The degree and the leading coefficient follow from the recurrence: the leading term of $2X\,T_n$ is $2\cdot2^{n-1}X^{n+1}=2^nX^{n+1}$, and $T_{n-1}$ has lower degree. Uniqueness: two such polynomials agree on $[-1,1]$, an infinite set, so they are equal by Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}. The $n$ numbers $\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{2n}$ are distinct, and they are roots because $\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi}2=0$. Since $\deg T_n=n$, there are no others.
\end{proof}

The first few are $T_2=2X^2-1$, $T_3=4X^3-3X$, $T_4=8X^4-8X^2+1$ and $T_5=16X^5-20X^3+5X$. Now check $T_2(\cos\theta)=\cos2\theta$, which is the double-angle formula you already used. Figure~\ref{fig:chebyshev} shows $T_2,\dots,T_5$ oscillating between $-1$ and $1$. Each $T_n$ reaches $\pm1$ at $n+1$ points, with alternating signs. That restlessness is the whole minimax theorem.

Interpolating $\frac1{1+25x^2}$ at $11$ equally spaced points (rust) produces a polynomial whose error is about $1.9$. At the $11$ roots of $T_{11}$ (charcoal), the error drops to about $0.11$.}

Outside $[-1,1]$ there is no real $\theta$ with $\cos\theta=x$. The polynomial $T_n$ is still there, and it grows fast.

\begin{propositionx}[closed form]
For every $x\in\C$, with either choice of the square root,
\[
T_n(x)=\frac{\bigl(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}\bigr)^n+\bigl(x-\sqrt{x^2-1}\bigr)^n}2.
\]
In particular $T_n(\cosh t)=\cosh nt$ for real $t$, and, for each fixed $n\ge1$, $T_n(x)\sim\frac12(2x)^n$ as $x\to+\infty$.
\end{propositionx}
\begin{proof}
Let $u=x+\sqrt{x^2-1}$ and $v=x-\sqrt{x^2-1}$. Then $u+v=2x$ and $uv=x^2-(x^2-1)=1$, so $u$ and $v$ are the roots of $t^2-2xt+1$. The sequence $s_n=\frac{u^n+v^n}2$ satisfies $s_{n+1}=2xs_n-s_{n-1}$: multiply $u^2=2xu-1$ by $u^{n-1}$, do the same for $v$, and add. Since $s_0=1=T_0(x)$ and $s_1=x=T_1(x)$, induction gives $s_n=T_n(x)$. For $x=\cosh t$ with $t\ge0$, take $\sqrt{x^2-1}=\sinh t$. Then $u$ and $v$ are $e^{\pm t}$. Evenness of both sides extends the identity to $t<0$. As $x\to+\infty$, $u\sim2x$ and $v\to0$.
\end{proof}

Now Chebyshev's question. Among monic polynomials of degree $n$, how small can $\sup_{[-1,1]}|P|$ be? The monic multiple of $T_n$ is $2^{1-n}T_n$, and on $[-1,1]$ it never leaves the interval $[-2^{1-n},2^{1-n}]$. Figure~\ref{fig:equioscillation} compares it with two other monic quartics. The decisive feature is visible: $\frac18T_4$ touches its bounds $\pm\frac18$ five times, with alternating signs. The monic polynomial that stays smallest is not the one that sits still. It is the one that hits the ceiling as often as it can. Exercise~\ref{ex:minimax} shows that this equioscillation forces every other monic polynomial to stick out further. Chebyshev published the comparison in 1854. It is also why the right half of Figure~\ref{fig:chebyshev} prefers the roots of $T_{n+1}$: in the interpolation error, the factor $\prod(x-x_i)$ is then as small as a monic polynomial of that degree can be on $[-1,1]$.

\section{Integer polynomials}

Over $\C$ every nonconstant polynomial has a root. Over $\Q$ the question is different: can it be written as a product of polynomials with rational coefficients?

\begin{proposition}[rational roots]\label{prop:ratroot}
If $p/q$ in lowest terms is a root of $a_nX^n+\dots+a_0\in\Z[X]$, then $p$ divides $a_0$ and $q$ divides $a_n$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiply the equation $a_n(p/q)^n+\dots+a_0=0$ by $q^n$: $a_np^n+a_{n-1}p^{n-1}q+\dots+a_0q^n=0$. Every term but the last is divisible by $p$, so $p$ divides $a_0q^n$. Since $\gcd(p,q)=1$, Euclid's lemma in Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} gives $p\mid a_0$. Symmetrically, $q$ divides $a_np^n$. Since $\gcd(q,p)=1$, the same lemma gives $q\mid a_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{examplex}
The real cube root of $2$ is irrational. A rational root of $X^3-2$ would be an integer dividing $2$, and none of $\pm1,\pm2$ works. Likewise $X^3-X+1$ has no rational root: the candidates are $\pm1$, and both give the value $1$. A cubic with a nontrivial factorisation over $\Q$ must have a factor of degree $1$, hence a rational root. So $X^3-X+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$. The same reasoning handles every polynomial of degree $2$ or $3$. From degree $4$ on, a polynomial can split into two quadratics and have no rational root. The next two tools are for that case.
\end{examplex}

Call $P\in\Z[X]$ \emph{primitive} if its coefficients have no common prime factor.

\begin{proposition}[Gauss's lemma]\label{prop:gausspoly}
For polynomials with integer coefficients:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item A product of primitive polynomials is primitive.
\item If $P\in\Z[X]$ factors as $P=BC$ in $\Q[X]$, then $P=B_1C_1$ with $B_1,C_1\in\Z[X]$ of the same degrees as $B$ and $C$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Let $B=\sum b_iX^i$ and $C=\sum c_jX^j$ be primitive, and let $p$ be a prime. Let $i_0$ and $j_0$ be the least indices with $p\nmid b_{i_0}$ and $p\nmid c_{j_0}$. The coefficient of $X^{i_0+j_0}$ in $BC$ is $b_{i_0}c_{j_0}$ plus terms $b_ic_j$ with $i<i_0$ or $j<j_0$. Those extra terms are divisible by $p$, and $p$ does not divide $b_{i_0}c_{j_0}$. So $p$ does not divide every coefficient of $BC$.

(b) Clearing denominators and contents, write $B=\beta B_0$ and $C=\gamma C_0$ with $\beta,\gamma\in\Q$ and with $B_0,C_0\in\Z[X]$ primitive. Write $\beta\gamma=\frac uv$ in lowest terms with $v\ge1$. Then $vP=uB_0C_0$. A prime $p$ dividing $v$ does not divide $u$, so it would divide every coefficient of $B_0C_0$, contradicting (a). Thus $v=1$, and $P=(uB_0)C_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Eisenstein's criterion]\label{thm:eisenstein}
Let $P=a_nX^n+\dots+a_0\in\Z[X]$ with $n\ge1$, and let $p$ be a prime with $p\nmid a_n$, with $p\mid a_i$ for every $i<n$, and with $p^2\nmid a_0$. Then $P$ is irreducible in $\Q[X]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:gausspoly} it is enough to rule out a factorisation in $\Z[X]$. Suppose $P=BC$ with $B=\sum_{i\le r}b_iX^i$ and $C=\sum_{j\le s}c_jX^j$ in $\Z[X]$, where $r,s\ge1$ and $r+s=n$. Since $p$ divides $a_0=b_0c_0$ and $p^2$ does not divide $a_0$, the prime $p$ divides exactly one of $b_0$ and $c_0$. Say $p\mid b_0$ and $p\nmid c_0$. Not every $b_i$ is divisible by $p$, because $p$ does not divide $a_n=b_rc_s$. Let $k$ be the least index with $p\nmid b_k$. Then $1\le k\le r<n$, and
\[
a_k=b_kc_0+b_{k-1}c_1+\dots+b_0c_k\equiv b_kc_0\not\equiv0\pmod p,
\]
which contradicts $p\mid a_k$.
\end{proof}

The test is named for the second person who found it. Theodor Sch\"onemann proved it in 1846, in a paper on polynomials modulo primes. Gotthold Eisenstein found it again in 1850, while studying the lemniscate, and his name stuck. Cox's article in the sources tells the story. The test is decisive when a prime fits, and silent when none does. A second test, still in Sch\"onemann's spirit, often fills the gap.

\begin{propositionx}[reduction modulo $p$]\label{prop:reduction}
Let $P\in\Z[X]$ have degree $n$ and leading coefficient not divisible by the prime $p$. Reduce every coefficient modulo $p$ to get a polynomial $\bar P$ over the field $\Z/p\Z$. If $\bar P$ has no factorisation into two factors of degrees $r$ and $s$ with $r,s\ge1$ and $r+s=n$, then $P$ is irreducible over $\Q$.
\end{propositionx}
\begin{proof}
Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} shows that $\Z/p\Z$ is a field: Euclid's lemma excludes zero divisors, and B\'ezout's identity gives an inverse to each nonzero class. Thus degrees add under multiplication in $(\Z/p\Z)[X]$. If $P=BC$ in $\Q[X]$ with $\deg B$ and $\deg C$ at least $1$, Gauss's lemma gives such a factorisation in $\Z[X]$. Reduction modulo $p$ respects sums and products, so $\bar P=\bar B\bar C$. Since $p$ does not divide the leading coefficient of $P$, which is the product of those of $B$ and $C$, it divides neither of them. Hence $\deg\bar B=\deg B$ and $\deg\bar C=\deg C$. That is the factorisation assumed not to exist.
\end{proof}

\begin{examplex}
Reduce $X^3+X+1$ modulo $2$. It has no root in $\Z/2\Z$: both $0+0+1$ and $1+1+1$ equal $1$. A cubic factorisation would need a factor of degree $1$. So $X^3+X+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$.

A degree $4$ example needs one more check. Reduce $X^4+X+1$ modulo $2$. It has no root. A factorisation into two quadratics would use the only monic irreducible quadratic over $\Z/2\Z$, which is $X^2+X+1$, and the square of that quadratic is $X^4+X^2+1$, not $X^4+X+1$. So $X^4+X+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$. No Eisenstein prime could have said so.

The converse fails. The polynomial $X^4+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$: the shift $X\mapsto X+1$ produces $X^4+4X^3+6X^2+4X+2$, which is Eisenstein at $2$, as in the spirit of Exercise~\ref{ex:eisen}. Yet $X^4+1$ factors modulo every prime. Volume~2, \emph{The Integers}, shows why. Irreducibility modulo one prime is a sufficient test. It is not a necessary one.
\end{examplex}

\section{Symmetric functions of the roots}\label{sec:symmetric}

Vi\`ete gives the elementary symmetric functions $\sigma_k$ of the roots. The power sums $p_k=x_1^k+\dots+x_n^k$ are often what one wants, as in Exercise~\ref{ex:viete}. Newton found the passage from one family to the other. You have already used the first two lines: $p_1=\sigma_1$ and $p_2=\sigma_1^2-2\sigma_2$.

\begin{proposition}[Newton's identities]\label{prop:newtonid}
Let $x_1,\dots,x_n$ be numbers, let $\sigma_k$ be their elementary symmetric functions (with $\sigma_0=1$ and $\sigma_k=0$ for $k>n$), and let $p_k=\sum_ix_i^k$. Then for every $k\ge1$,
\[
p_k-\sigma_1p_{k-1}+\sigma_2p_{k-2}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\sigma_{k-1}p_1+(-1)^kk\,\sigma_k=0.
\]
For $k>n$ the last term is $0$, and the identity reads $p_k=\sigma_1p_{k-1}-\sigma_2p_{k-2}+\dots+(-1)^{n-1}\sigma_np_{k-n}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $P=\prod_i(X-x_i)=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\sigma_jX^{n-j}$.

\emph{Case $k>n$.} Each $x_i$ is a root of $P$, so $x_i^{k-n}P(x_i)=0$, that is, $\sum_j(-1)^j\sigma_jx_i^{k-j}=0$. Sum over $i$.

\emph{Case $k\le n$.} This is where Horner's scheme earns its keep. Write $P=(X-x_i)Q_i$. On the one hand, the product rule gives $P'=\sum_iQ_i$. On the other hand, the coefficients of $Q_i$ are computed by Horner's recurrence. Writing $Q_i=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}b_m^{(i)}X^{n-1-m}$, the recurrence starts from $b_0^{(i)}=1$ and $b_m^{(i)}=(-1)^m\sigma_m+x_ib_{m-1}^{(i)}$. By induction
\[
b_m^{(i)}=\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\sigma_jx_i^{m-j}.
\]
Compare the coefficients of $X^{n-1-m}$ in $P'=\sum_iQ_i$, for $0\le m\le n-1$. On the left the coefficient is $(n-m)(-1)^m\sigma_m$. On the right it is $\sum_ib_m^{(i)}=\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\sigma_jp_{m-j}$, and the term $j=m$ is $(-1)^m\sigma_mp_0=(-1)^mn\sigma_m$. Hence
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^j\sigma_jp_{m-j}+(-1)^mm\,\sigma_m=0,
\]
which is the identity for $k=m\le n-1$. For $k=n$, sum $P(x_i)=0$ over $i$: $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^j\sigma_jp_{n-j}+(-1)^nn\sigma_n=0$.
\end{proof}

The identities compute the $p_k$ from the $\sigma_k$, one step at a time: $p_1=\sigma_1$, then $p_2=\sigma_1p_1-2\sigma_2$, then $p_3=\sigma_1p_2-\sigma_2p_1+3\sigma_3$, and so on. Read backwards, they compute the $\sigma_k$ from the $p_k$, dividing by $k$ at each step. Exercise~\ref{ex:newtonid} runs them on $X^3-X+1$. Newton published them in the \emph{Arithmetica universalis} of 1707. Albert Girard already had the first four in 1629, in the same book that stated the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Vi\`ete says that $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$ are polynomials in the coefficients. Newton says that the power sums are polynomials in the $\sigma_k$. For a preview of the general fact, in three variables $\sum_{i\ne j}x_i^2x_j=\sigma_1\sigma_2-3\sigma_3$. The next theorem says that every symmetric polynomial has some such expression. A polynomial $F(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is \emph{symmetric} if it is unchanged by every permutation of the variables.

\begin{theorem}[fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials]\label{thm:symmetric}
Every symmetric polynomial $F\in\mathbb K[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is a polynomial in $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n$ with coefficients in $\mathbb K$: there is $G\in\mathbb K[y_1,\dots,y_n]$ with $F=G(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n)$. If $F$ has integer coefficients, so does $G$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Order the monomials $x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ \emph{lexicographically}: compare $a_1$ first, then $a_2$ in case of a tie, and so on, as in a dictionary. Every nonzero polynomial has a \emph{leading monomial}, the largest one that occurs. The leading monomial of a product is the product of the leading monomials, because the exponent vectors add and the largest sum comes from the two largest vectors. The leading monomial of $\sigma_k$ is $x_1x_2\cdots x_k$, so the leading monomial of $\sigma_1^{d_1}\sigma_2^{d_2}\cdots\sigma_n^{d_n}$ is $x_1^{d_1+\dots+d_n}x_2^{d_2+\dots+d_n}\cdots x_n^{d_n}$.

Let $F\ne0$ be symmetric, with leading monomial $c\,x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$. Since $F$ is symmetric it also contains every permuted monomial, and among these the largest in the dictionary is the one with decreasing exponents. So $a_1\ge a_2\ge\dots\ge a_n$. Put $d_k=a_k-a_{k+1}$, with $a_{n+1}=0$. These integers are nonnegative, and the product $\sigma_1^{d_1}\cdots\sigma_n^{d_n}$ has the same leading monomial as $F$, with coefficient $1$. Then $F_1=F-c\,\sigma_1^{d_1}\cdots\sigma_n^{d_n}$ is symmetric, and it is either zero or it has a strictly smaller leading monomial. Among the monomials of total degree at most $\deg F$ there are finitely many, so repeating the step ends at $0$ after finitely many rounds. Thus $F$ is the sum of the subtracted products. Every coefficient that appears is one of the leading coefficients $c$, which are integers if those of $F$ are.
\end{proof}

\begin{examplex}
The proof is an algorithm. Take $F=\sum_{i\ne j}x_i^2x_j$ in three variables. The leading monomial is $x_1^2x_2$, so $(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(2,1,0)$ and $(d_1,d_2,d_3)=(1,1,0)$. Subtract $\sigma_1\sigma_2=\sum_{i\ne j}x_i^2x_j+3x_1x_2x_3$. What is left is $-3x_1x_2x_3=-3\sigma_3$. So $F=\sigma_1\sigma_2-3\sigma_3$. For the roots of $X^3-X+1$ this gives $\sum_{i\ne j}x_i^2x_j=0\cdot(-1)-3(-1)=3$.
\end{examplex}

The theorem is the first thing Galois theory needs. Volume~3, \emph{Symmetry}, starts from it: a symmetric expression in the roots can be written in the field of the coefficients.

\section{Real roots: counting and locating}

Over $\C$ a polynomial of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ roots, multiplicities counted. Over $\R$ it has between $0$ and $n$, and the signs of the coefficients know more about that number than one might expect. Descartes noticed the count in 1637, in the \emph{G\'eom\'etrie} appended to the \emph{Discours de la m\'ethode}. Proofs appeared in the eighteenth century. Gauss gave an algebraic proof in 1828. For $P=\sum a_kX^k$ with real coefficients, let $V(P)$ be the number of \emph{sign changes} in the sequence $a_n,a_{n-1},\dots,a_0$ after the zeros are deleted, and let $Z^+(P)$ be the number of positive roots counted with multiplicity.

The rule is willing to be wrong, but only by an even number. It will not miss by one. That parity is the part you can trust before you compute a single value. Figure~\ref{fig:signs} is the count for one cubic.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch03-signs}
\caption{Sign changes for $X^3-X-1$. The coefficient sequence $+,\,-\,,\,-$ has one change, so there is exactly one positive root. For $P(-X)$ the sequence $-,\,+,\,-$ has two changes, so the number of negative roots is $0$ or $2$. A later evaluation shows that it is $0$.}
\label{fig:signs}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Descartes' rule of signs]\label{thm:descartes}
For a nonzero $P\in\R[X]$, $Z^+(P)\le V(P)$, and $V(P)-Z^+(P)$ is even.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Dividing $P$ by a power of $X$ changes neither $V$ nor $Z^+$, so we may assume $P(0)\ne0$. First, $V(P)$ is odd exactly when the leading coefficient and the constant term have opposite signs: each sign change flips the sign, and the changes must carry the first sign to the last.

\emph{Step 1: a polynomial with no positive root has $V$ even.} If $Q(0)\ne0$ and $Q$ has no root in $]0,\infty[$, then $Q$ keeps a constant sign on $[0,\infty[$, by the intermediate value theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}. That sign is the sign of the leading coefficient for large $x$, and the sign of $Q(0)$ at $0$. So the first and last coefficients agree in sign, and $V(Q)$ is even.

\emph{Step 2: multiplying by $X-a$ with $a>0$ raises $V$ by an odd number.} Let $Q=\sum_{j=0}^mb_jX^j$ with $b_m\ne0$ and $b_0\ne0$, and write $(X-a)Q=\sum_jc_jX^j$. Then $c_{m+1}=b_m$, $c_0=-ab_0$, and $c_j=b_{j-1}-ab_j$ for $1\le j\le m$. Group the nonzero coefficients of $Q$, read from $b_m$ downwards, into maximal blocks of constant sign. There are $V(Q)+1$ blocks, with alternating signs $s_1,s_2,\dots,s_{V(Q)+1}$, and $s_1$ is the sign of $b_m$. Let $b_j$ be the first, highest-index, element of a block with sign $s$. Then $b_{j+1}$ is either $0$ or of sign $-s$, unless $j=m$. In all cases $c_{j+1}=b_j-ab_{j+1}$ has the sign $s$, strictly. So the sequence of the $c_j$ contains, in decreasing order of index, nonzero elements of signs $s_1,s_2,\dots,s_{V(Q)+1}$, and then $c_0$ of sign $-s_{V(Q)+1}$. Hence $V((X-a)Q)\ge V(Q)+1$. For the parity: the first and last coefficients of $(X-a)Q$ have signs $s_1$ and $-s_{V(Q)+1}=-(-1)^{V(Q)}s_1$. They are opposite exactly when $V(Q)$ is even, so $V((X-a)Q)$ is odd exactly when $V(Q)$ is even. Thus $V((X-a)Q)-V(Q)$ is odd.

\emph{Conclusion.} Write $P=(X-a_1)\cdots(X-a_r)Q$ with $a_1,\dots,a_r>0$ the positive roots, counted with multiplicity, so $r=Z^+(P)$, and with $Q$ free of positive roots. Then $V(P)\ge V(Q)+r\ge r$ by Step~2 applied $r$ times, and $V(P)\equiv V(Q)+r\equiv r\pmod2$ by Steps~2 and~1.
\end{proof}

To count negative roots, apply the rule to $P(-X)$.

\begin{examplex}
The polynomial $P=X^3-X-1$ has signs $+,-,-$: one change, so exactly one positive root. And $P(-X)=-X^3+X-1$ has signs $-,+,-$: two changes, so $0$ or $2$ negative roots. Figure~\ref{fig:signs} is that count. Here $P'=3X^2-1$ vanishes at $\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}$, and $P\bigl(-\frac1{\sqrt3}\bigr)=\frac2{3\sqrt3}-1<0$ is the maximum of $P$ on $]-\infty,0]$. There is no negative root. The unique real root, about $1.3247$, is the plastic number. Dom Hans van der Laan called it that, and used it as a three-dimensional cousin of the golden ratio. He later dated the idea to a chapel façade of 1928. The written account begins with his lectures of 1939. Exercise~\ref{ex:descartes} counts the real roots of $X^5-3X+1$ in the same way.
\end{examplex}

The last theorem locates the roots of $P'$ from those of $P$. Over $\R$ this is Rolle's theorem, Theorem~\ref{thm:mvt} in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}: between two real roots of $P$ there is a root of $P'$. Over $\C$ there is no ``between''. There is a convex hull, and the statement is just as clean. The theorem bears the names of Gauss and F\'elix Lucas, whose papers of 1874 and 1879 developed the result.

\begin{theorem}[Gauss--Lucas]\label{thm:gausslucas}
Let $P\in\C[X]$ be nonconstant. Every root of $P'$ lies in the convex hull of the roots of $P$. That is, it is a combination $\sum_i\lambda_ix_i$ of the roots $x_i$ of $P$ with $\lambda_i\ge0$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $P=c\prod_{i=1}^n(X-x_i)$. If $z$ is a root of $P'$ and also of $P$, then it is one of the $x_i$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $P(z)\ne0$, and the product rule gives
\[
0=\frac{P'(z)}{P(z)}=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{z-x_i}=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\overline{z-x_i}}{|z-x_i|^2}.
\]
Taking conjugates, $\sum_iw_i(z-x_i)=0$ with $w_i=|z-x_i|^{-2}>0$. So $z\sum_iw_i=\sum_iw_ix_i$, and $z=\sum_i\lambda_ix_i$ with $\lambda_i=w_i\big/\sum_jw_j$.
\end{proof}

\caption{Gauss--Lucas for a polynomial of degree $5$. The roots of $P$ (filled) span a convex quadrilateral; the fifth root lies inside it. The four roots of $P'$ (circles) lie inside that quadrilateral. Each of them is a weighted average of the roots of $P$, with weights $|z-x_i|^{-2}$, which favour the nearby roots.}

\begin{think}
A physicist's reading of the proof: put a unit positive charge at each root $x_i$, in a plane where charges repel with a force proportional to $1$ over the distance, the two-dimensional law. The force at $z$ is, up to a constant factor, the conjugate of $\sum\frac1{z-x_i}$. Away from the charges, the roots of $P'$ are the equilibrium points of this field. A multiple root of $P$ is also a root of $P'$, but the field is singular there. Can a point outside the hull be in equilibrium? Every charge pushes it further out.
\end{think}

And Exercise~\ref{ex:mason} proves Fermat's last theorem for polynomials, in half a page: the same shape of statement that the integers took centuries to settle.
\end{beauty}

\section{Workshop: a fit is not a forecast}

Interpolation answers an exact question. Which polynomial of degree at most $d$ has these $d+1$ values? It does not promise that the world is a polynomial of that degree. A small change in the values need not make a small change in a root, either.

\begin{examplex}[a root that splits under a whisper]
Compare $P(x)=(x-1)^2$ and $P_\delta(x)=(x-1)^2-\delta$ with $\delta>0$. Their values differ everywhere by only $\delta$, but the double root $1$ splits into $1\pm\sqrt\delta$. For $\delta=10^{-6}$, the roots move by $10^{-3}$, a thousand times the change in the values. The graph is almost flat near a multiple root. By contrast, $L(x)=x-1$ and $L(x)-\delta$ have roots separated by only $\delta$. Multiplicity has numerical consequences.
\end{examplex}

\begin{figure}[ht]\centering\fig{review03-roots}
\caption{Solid: $(x-1)^2$. Dashed: $(x-1)^2-0.04$, with roots $0.8$ and $1.2$. A vertical change of $0.04$ moves the roots horizontally by $0.2$. The flat tangency at the double root is the reason.}
\end{figure}

Here is the forecast question in numbers. A machine returns $2,3,6$ at the inputs $0,1,2$. The polynomial $P=X^2+2$ fits, and it is the only polynomial of degree at most $2$ that does: if $Q$ were another, then $Q-P$ would have three roots and degree at most $2$. Within that model the next output, at $3$, is $P(3)=11$. Drop the degree bound and every $P+cX(X-1)(X-2)$ also fits. Its value at $3$ is $11+6c$. There is no model-free prediction. The hint, if you want to find $P$ rather than be told it, is to subtract $X^2+2$ and see what is left.

\begin{reminder}[where the root bound works]
A nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over a field has at most $d$ roots in that field. Distinctness and the field hypothesis matter. Modulo $8$, the polynomial $X^2-1$ vanishes at $1,3,5,7$. The proof fails because nonzero residues can multiply to zero: $2\cdot4=0$ modulo $8$. Even over a field, two polynomial functions need not identify their polynomials if the field is finite. Figure~\ref{fig:values} was that warning for the field with two elements.
\end{reminder}

Sum that inequality at the angles $\frac{k\pi}N$, read the sum of the squared cotangents from Vi\`ete, and let $N$ grow. Both ends tend to $\frac{\pi^2}6$, and they squeeze the series between them.

\section*{What this chapter establishes}
A polynomial over a field is determined by its coefficients, and a nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots. That one bound turns a finite check into an identity. Division finds the roots. Interpolation spends the bound on values. Chebyshev's monic polynomial of degree $n$ spends it on size: on $[-1,1]$ it stays inside $\bigl[-2^{1-n},2^{1-n}\bigr]$, and nothing monic of degree $n$ stays closer. Eisenstein decides some factorisations over $\Q$ without producing a root. The fundamental theorem of algebra puts the roots in $\C$. Over $\R$, nonreal roots arrive in conjugate pairs.

The next chapter asks a question that does not mention a polynomial. Why is $F_{n+1}F_{n-1}-F_n^2$ always $\pm1$? The answer is a determinant, read as an area. The same chapter proves that a square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, so the root bound comes back wearing a matrix.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:rem}
Find the remainder of the Euclidean division of $X^n$ by $(X-1)(X-2)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:viete}
Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be the complex roots of $X^3-X+1$. Compute $x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2$ and $x_1^3+x_2^3+x_3^3$, without finding the roots.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:simple}
Show that for $n\ge2$ the polynomial $X^n+X+1$ has only simple roots in $\C$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:lagsum}
Let $x_0,\dots,x_n$ be distinct complex numbers. Show that
\[
\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{x_i^k}{\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0\le k\le n-1,\\1&\text{if }k=n.\end{cases}
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:eisen}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $X^4+10X+5$ is irreducible over $\Q$.
\item Let $p$ be prime. Show that $\Phi_p=1+X+\dots+X^{p-1}$ is irreducible over $\Q$. \emph{Hint: look at $\Phi_p(X+1)$.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:minimax}
\emph{Chebyshev's minimax property.} Show that every monic $P\in\R[X]$ of degree $n\ge1$ satisfies
$\sup_{[-1,1]}|P|\ge2^{1-n}$, with equality for $P=2^{1-n}T_n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:basel}
\emph{The Basel problem through roots.} Let $m\ge1$ and $N=2m+1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Using $\sin N\theta=\operatorname{Im}\left((\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^N\right)$, show that for $0<\theta<\frac\pi2$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sin N\theta&=\sin^N\theta\eqbr\times\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\binom N{2j+1}\cot^{2(m-j)}\theta.\end{aligned}
\]
\item Deduce that the numbers $\cot^2\frac{k\pi}N$, $1\le k\le m$, are the roots of $\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\binom N{2j+1}X^{m-j}$, and that
$\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^m\cot^2\frac{k\pi}N=\frac{m(2m-1)}3$.
\item Using $\cot^2x<\frac1{x^2}<1+\cot^2x$ on $\left]0,\frac\pi2\right[$, prove that $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:sos}
Let $P\in\R[X]$ with $P(x)\ge0$ for all $x\in\R$. Show that there are $A,B\in\R[X]$ with $P=A^2+B^2$.
\end{exercise}

\subsection*{Further exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:horner}
Use Horner's scheme to compute $P(-2)$ for $P=X^5-2X^3+X^2-7$, and to write $P=(X+2)Q+P(-2)$ explicitly. Then check the division by multiplying back.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:multroot}
Let $n\ge3$. Show that $1$ is a root of $X^n-nX+n-1$ of multiplicity exactly $2$, and factor $X^4-4X+3$ completely over $\R$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:lagrange2k}
\emph{Bridge: interpolation and finite differences.} Let $P$ be the polynomial of degree at most $n$ with $P(k)=2^k$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$. Compute $P(n+1)$. \emph{Hint: Chapter~\ref{ch:sums} has a formula made for this.}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:secret}
Three directors hold the shares $(1,8)$, $(2,15)$ and $(3,24)$ of a secret $s=P(0)$, where $P$ has degree at most $2$. Recover $s$ with the
Lagrange formula evaluated at $0$ only (do not compute $P$), then find $P$ and check. Explain why the two shares $(1,8)$ and $(2,15)$ alone are
compatible with every value of $s$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:chebprod}
Show that for all $m,n\in\N$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $T_m(T_n(X))=T_{mn}(X)$;
\item $T_mT_n=\frac12\bigl(T_{m+n}+T_{|m-n|}\bigr)$;
\item (Return after Chapter~\ref{ch:calculus}.) $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\frac{T_m(x)T_n(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=0$ for $m\ne n$, and compute the integral for $m=n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:newtonid}
Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be the roots of $X^3-X+1$. With Newton's identities compute $p_4=\sum x_i^4$ and $p_5$, and find the monic cubic whose roots are
$x_1^2,x_2^2,x_3^2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:descartes}
Show that $X^5-3X+1$ has exactly three real roots, and locate each of them between two consecutive integers or half-integers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:disc}
\emph{The discriminant of the cubic.} Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be the roots of $P=X^3+pX+q$ and $\Delta=(x_1-x_2)^2(x_2-x_3)^2(x_3-x_1)^2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $P'(x_i)=\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)$ and deduce $\Delta=-P'(x_1)P'(x_2)P'(x_3)$.
\item Find the monic cubic whose roots are $x_1^2,x_2^2,x_3^2$, and deduce $\Delta=-4p^3-27q^2$.
\item Conclude: for real $p,q$, the cubic has three distinct real roots if $\Delta>0$, one real root if $\Delta<0$, and a multiple root if $\Delta=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:logconcave}
Let $n\ge1$ and let $P=\sum_{k=0}^na_kX^k\in\R[X]$ have $a_n\ne0$ and $n$ real roots (with multiplicity).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $P'(x)^2\ge P(x)P''(x)$ for all real $x$.
\item Show that $P'$ also has only real roots.
\item Deduce that $a_k^2\ge a_{k-1}a_{k+1}$ for $1\le k\le n-1$: the coefficients are \emph{log-concave}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:mason}
\emph{The Mason--Stothers theorem.} For $F\in\C[X]$ non-zero let $n_0(F)$ be the number of distinct roots of $F$. Let $A,B,C\in\C[X]$ be
pairwise coprime, not all constant, with $A+B=C$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $W=AB'-A'B$ is not zero, and that $W=AC'-A'C=B'C-BC'$.
\item Show that if $a$ is a root of $A$ of multiplicity $m$, then $(X-a)^{m-1}$ divides $W$. Deduce that $\frac{ABC}{\prod(X-a)}$, the product over
the distinct roots $a$ of $ABC$, divides $W$.
\item Conclude that $\max(\deg A,\deg B,\deg C)\le n_0(ABC)-1$.
\item \emph{Fermat for polynomials.} Show that for $n\ge3$ there are no pairwise coprime, non-constant $A,B,C\in\C[X]$ with $A^n+B^n=C^n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{challenge}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{A cycle of integers.} Show that there is no $P\in\Z[X]$ and distinct integers $a,b,c$ with $P(a)=b$, $P(b)=c$ and $P(c)=a$.
\item \emph{Uniqueness in Chebyshev's theorem.} Show that $2^{1-n}T_n$ is the \emph{only} monic polynomial of degree $n$ with $\sup_{[-1,1]}|P|=2^{1-n}$.
\item \emph{All cyclotomic polynomials are irreducible.} Exercise~\ref{ex:eisen}(b) does $\Phi_p$. Try $\Phi_{p^k}$ with the same idea, then find out why $\Phi_n$ for general $n$ is a different kind of problem.
\end{enumerate}
Notes at the end of the chapter.
\end{challenge}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:benford}
\emph{Leading digits.} Let $A_d$ be the set of positive integers whose decimal expansion starts with the digit $d$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $A_1$ has no density: the proportion of $n\le N$ in $A_1$ oscillates between $\frac19$ and $\frac59$.
\item Show that $A_d$ has \emph{logarithmic density} $\log_{10}(1+\frac1d)$:
\[
\frac1{\log N}\sum_{n\le N,\ n\in A_d}\frac1n\longrightarrow\log_{10}\Bigl(1+\frac1d\Bigr).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:bellasym}
\emph{The size of the Bell numbers.} Let $b_n$ be the number of partitions of an $n$-set, so that $b_n=\frac1e\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{k^n}{k!}$ (Dobi\'nski's
formula, proved in Volume~5, Chapter~2). For $n\ge3$ let $u=u_n$ be the positive root of $ue^u=n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $k^n\le\bigl(\frac n{eu}\bigr)^ne^{uk}$ for every integer $k\ge0$, and deduce that $\ln b_n\le n\bigl(u-1+\frac1u\bigr)-1$.
\item Using the single term $k=\lceil e^u\rceil$ of Dobi\'nski's formula, show that $\ln b_n\ge n\bigl(u-1+\frac1u\bigr)-O(\ln n)$.
\item With $L=\ln n$, show that $u=L-\ln L+\frac{\ln L}L+O\bigl((\frac{\ln L}L)^2\bigr)$, and deduce that
\[
\ln b_n=n\Bigl(\ln n-\ln\ln n-1+\frac{1+\ln\ln n}{\ln n}+O\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{\ln\ln n}{\ln n}\Bigr)^2\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:rem}
\idea We do not need the quotient, only the remainder, which has degree $<2$: two unknowns $a,b$. Evaluating the division identity at the roots $1$ and $2$ of the divisor kills the unknown quotient and gives two equations.
\writeup Write $X^n=(X-1)(X-2)Q+aX+b$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:polydiv}). Evaluating at $1$ and $2$: $a+b=1$ and $2a+b=2^n$. So the remainder is $(2^n-1)X+2-2^n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:viete}
\idea Symmetric expressions in the roots are polynomials in the $\sigma_k$, which Vi\`ete reads off the coefficients. For $\sum x_i^2$ use $(\sum x_i)^2=\sum x_i^2+2\sigma_2$. For cubes there is a shortcut: each root satisfies the equation, so $x_i^3$ can be replaced by a lower-degree expression.
\writeup Here $\sigma_1=0$, $\sigma_2=-1$, $\sigma_3=-1$. Then $\sum x_i^2=\sigma_1^2-2\sigma_2=2$. Each root satisfies $x_i^3=x_i-1$, so
$\sum x_i^3=\sum x_i-3=\sigma_1-3=-3$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:simple}
\idea By Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(c), a multiple root is a common root of $P$ and $P'$. Two equations in one unknown $a$: eliminate the high power $a^n$ between them to get a linear equation, which pins $a$ down; then check that this value is impossible, here by comparing moduli.
\writeup A multiple root $a$ satisfies $a^n+a+1=0$ and $na^{n-1}+1=0$. Multiplying the second by $a$, $na^n=-a$, and substituting $a^n=-a-1$ gives
$-n(a+1)=-a$, so $a=-\frac n{n-1}$. Then $|a|=\frac n{n-1}>1$, so $|a|^{n-1}>1$; but $na^{n-1}=-1$ forces $|a|^{n-1}=\frac1n<1$. Contradiction.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:lagsum}

\begin{solution}{ex:eisen}
\idea Irreducibility over $\Q$ of an integer polynomial: look for a prime that divides every coefficient but the leading one, with the constant term not divisible by its square. For (a) the coefficients $10,5$ point to $5$. For $\Phi_p$ no prime works directly (all coefficients are $1$), but a change of variable $X\mapsto X+1$ does not affect irreducibility and turns the coefficients into binomial coefficients $\binom pk$, all divisible by $p$.

\begin{solution}{ex:minimax}
\idea Equality is the easy part. For the inequality, argue by contradiction: if some monic $P$ did better than $2^{1-n}T_n$, the difference $Q$ would have degree $\le n-1$ (leading terms cancel). Now $2^{1-n}T_n$ reaches $\pm2^{1-n}$ alternately at $n+1$ points, and $P$ stays strictly inside, so $Q$ alternates in sign $n+1$ times: $n$ sign changes means $n$ roots, too many.

\begin{solution}{ex:basel}
\idea The plan of attack is in the question, but it is worth seeing why it works. Vi\`ete computes \emph{sums of roots} exactly. So we want numbers related to $\frac1{k^2}$ that are the roots of an explicit polynomial: $\cot^2\frac{k\pi}N$ are such numbers, because $\sin N\theta=0$ at $\theta=\frac{k\pi}N$ and $\sin N\theta$ is a polynomial in $\cot\theta$ (times $\sin^N\theta$). Then $\cot x\approx\frac1x$ for small $x$ connects them to $\frac{N^2}{k^2\pi^2}$, and a squeeze finishes.
\writeup (a) By de Moivre and the binomial theorem, the imaginary part of $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^N$ collects the odd powers of $i\sin\theta$:
\[
\begin{aligned}\sin N\theta&=\sum_{j=0}^m\binom N{2j+1}(-1)^j\cos^{N-2j-1}\theta\,\sin^{2j+1}\theta\\
&=\sin^N\theta\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\binom N{2j+1}\cot^{N-2j-1}\theta,\end{aligned}
\]
and $N-2j-1=2(m-j)$.

(b) For $\theta_k=\frac{k\pi}N$ with $1\le k\le m$ we have $0<\theta_k<\frac\pi2$, $\sin N\theta_k=0$ and $\sin\theta_k\ne0$, so $\cot^2\theta_k$ is a root of
$R=\sum_j(-1)^j\binom N{2j+1}X^{m-j}$. These $m$ numbers are distinct since $\cot^2$ is injective on $\left]0,\frac\pi2\right[$, and $\deg R=m$, so they are all its roots.
By Vi\`ete,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^m\cot^2\theta_k&=\frac{\binom N3}{\binom N1}=\frac{(2m+1)(2m)(2m-1)}{6(2m+1)}\eqbr=\frac{m(2m-1)}3.\end{aligned}
\]
(c) Proposition~\ref{prop:sincmp} gives $0<\sin x<x<\tan x$ on $\left]0,\frac\pi2\right[$. Squaring and inverting, and using $\frac1{\sin^2x}=1+\cot^2x$, produces $\cot^2x<\frac1{x^2}<1+\cot^2x$.
Apply it at $x=\theta_k$ and sum over $1\le k\le m$:
\[
\frac{m(2m-1)}3<\frac{N^2}{\pi^2}\sum_{k=1}^m\frac1{k^2}<m+\frac{m(2m-1)}3.
\]
Multiply by $\frac{\pi^2}{N^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{(2m+1)^2}$. Both bounds tend to $\frac{\pi^2}6$ as $m\to\infty$ (they are equivalent to $\frac{\pi^2}{4m^2}\cdot\frac{2m^2}3$), so $\sum\frac1{k^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sos}
\idea Factor $P$ over $\C$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dalembert}) and see what positivity says about the factors: real roots must have even multiplicity, non-real roots come in conjugate pairs. A pair contributes $|x-z|^2$, which is a sum of two squares; and products of sums of two squares are sums of two squares, because $|uv|^2=|u|^2|v|^2$ (the same identity as Brahmagupta's). So group all the $X-z_j$ into one complex polynomial $U+iV$.
\writeup If $P=0$ take $A=B=0$. Otherwise, a real root of odd multiplicity $m$ would make $P$ change sign there (near $a$, $P=(X-a)^mU$ with $U(a)\ne0$), so every real root has even multiplicity;
and the leading coefficient $c$ is positive since $P(x)$ has the sign of $c$ for large $x$. Over $\C$, the non-real roots come in conjugate pairs, so
\[
\begin{gathered}P=c\,R^2\prod_{j=1}^s(X-z_j)(X-\bar z_j),\qq R=\prod_i(X-a_i)^{m_i/2}\in\R[X].\end{gathered}
\]
Write $\prod_j(X-z_j)=U+iV$ with $U,V\in\R[X]$. Then $\prod_j(X-\bar z_j)=U-iV$ and the product is $U^2+V^2$.
So $P=(\sqrt c\,RU)^2+(\sqrt c\,RV)^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:chebprod}
\idea Every identity about the $T_n$ on $[-1,1]$ is an identity about cosines in disguise; and an identity between polynomials that holds on $[-1,1]$ holds everywhere (Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(b)).
\writeup Put $x=\cos\theta$. (a) $T_m(T_n(\cos\theta))=T_m(\cos n\theta)=\cos mn\theta=T_{mn}(\cos\theta)$. (b) $\cos m\theta\cos n\theta=\frac12(\cos(m+n)\theta+\cos(m-n)\theta)$, and $\cos$ is even. In both cases the two polynomials agree on $[-1,1]$, hence everywhere.

(c) First substitute on $[\varepsilon,\pi-\varepsilon]$ with $0<\varepsilon<\pi/2$, where $x=\cos\theta$ has nonzero derivative. Here $dx=-\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{1-x^2}=\sin\theta$. The transformed integrand is continuous on $[0,\pi]$, so taking $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ proves existence of the improper integral and gives
\[
\int_{-1}^1\frac{T_mT_n}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\int_0^\pi\cos m\theta\cos n\theta\,d\theta=\frac12\int_0^\pi\bigl(\cos(m+n)\theta+\cos(m-n)\theta\bigr)d\theta.
\]
For $m\ne n$ both integrals vanish ($\int_0^\pi\cos k\theta\,d\theta=0$ for $k\ne0$). For $m=n\ge1$ the value is $\frac\pi2$, and for $m=n=0$ it is $\pi$.
The $T_n$ are \emph{orthogonal} for this weight, which is why they are the right basis for approximation on $[-1,1]$ (Trefethen's book in the sources).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:newtonid}
\idea Newton's identities are a recurrence: once $p_1,p_2,p_3$ are known, each further $p_k$ is a combination of the three previous ones. For the squares, compute their elementary symmetric functions from those of the $x_i$.
\writeup $\sigma_1=0$, $\sigma_2=-1$, $\sigma_3=-1$. Then $p_1=\sigma_1=0$, $p_2=\sigma_1p_1-2\sigma_2=2$, $p_3=\sigma_1p_2-\sigma_2p_1+3\sigma_3=-3$ (as in Exercise~\ref{ex:viete}), and for $k>3$, $p_k=\sigma_1p_{k-1}-\sigma_2p_{k-2}+\sigma_3p_{k-3}=p_{k-2}-p_{k-3}$:
$p_4=p_2-p_1=2$, $p_5=p_3-p_2=-5$. (The recurrence $p_k=p_{k-2}-p_{k-3}$ is just $x_i^k=x_i^{k-2}-x_i^{k-3}$ summed.)

The squares $y_i=x_i^2$ have $\sum y_i=p_2=2$, $\sum_{i<j}y_iy_j=\sum_{i<j}x_i^2x_j^2=\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_1\sigma_3=1$ (expand $\sigma_2^2$) and $y_1y_2y_3=\sigma_3^2=1$. So they are the roots of $Y^3-2Y^2+Y-1$. Check: $p_4=\sum y_i^2=2^2-2\cdot1=2$, as found.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:disc}
\idea $\Delta$ is symmetric in the roots, so it is a polynomial in $p$ and $q$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}; the problem is to find it without expanding $(x_1-x_2)^2(x_2-x_3)^2(x_3-x_1)^2$ by brute force. The derivative gives $\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)$ for free, and the product $\prod P'(x_i)$ is a symmetric function that we can compute by passing through the polynomial with roots $x_i^2$, found in Exercise~\ref{ex:newtonid}'s way.
\writeup (a) $P=\prod_j(X-x_j)$ and the product rule give $P'=\sum_k\prod_{j\ne k}(X-x_j)$; at $X=x_i$ every term with $k\ne i$ vanishes, leaving $\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)$. Then $\prod_iP'(x_i)=\prod_{i\ne j}(x_i-x_j)$, where each pair $\{i,j\}$ contributes $(x_i-x_j)(x_j-x_i)=-(x_i-x_j)^2$: three pairs, so $\prod_iP'(x_i)=-\Delta$.

(b) With $\sigma_1=0$, $\sigma_2=p$, $\sigma_3=-q$: the squares $y_i=x_i^2$ have $\sum y_i=\sigma_1^2-2\sigma_2=-2p$, $\sum_{i<j}y_iy_j=\sigma_2^2-2\sigma_1\sigma_3=p^2$, $\prod y_i=\sigma_3^2=q^2$, so they are the roots of $R=Y^3+2pY^2+p^2Y-q^2$. Now $P'(x_i)=3x_i^2+p=3(y_i+\frac p3)$ and
\[
\prod_i\bigl(y_i+\tfrac p3\bigr)=-\prod_i\bigl(-\tfrac p3-y_i\bigr)=-R\bigl(-\tfrac p3\bigr)=-\Bigl(-\frac{p^3}{27}+\frac{2p^3}9-\frac{p^3}3-q^2\Bigr)=\frac{4p^3}{27}+q^2.
\]
Hence $\prod_iP'(x_i)=27\bigl(\frac{4p^3}{27}+q^2\bigr)=4p^3+27q^2$, and $\Delta=-4p^3-27q^2$.

(c) $\Delta=0$ iff two roots coincide. If the three roots are real and distinct, $\Delta$ is a product of positive squares, so $\Delta>0$. If there is one real root $x_1$ and a conjugate pair $x_2=z$, $x_3=\bar z$ with $z\notin\R$: $(x_2-x_3)^2=(z-\bar z)^2=(2i\operatorname{Im}z)^2<0$, while $(x_1-z)^2(x_1-\bar z)^2=|x_1-z|^4>0$; so $\Delta<0$. The three cases are exclusive and exhaustive (Theorem~\ref{thm:dalembert}), so the signs characterise them. For the $X^3-3X+a$ of the example on multiple roots: $\Delta=108-27a^2$, zero exactly for $a=\pm2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:logconcave}
\idea (a) is the sign of the derivative of $\frac{P'}P=\sum\frac1{x-x_i}$, a decreasing function between the roots. (b) is Rolle. (c) chains them: $P^{(k-1)}$ is real-rooted by (b), and (a) at $x=0$ for it is exactly the inequality between three consecutive coefficients.
\writeup (a) Write $P=c\prod_{i=1}^n(X-x_i)$ with real $x_i$. For $x$ not a root, $\frac{P'(x)}{P(x)}=\sum_i\frac1{x-x_i}$ (product rule), and differentiating,
\[
\frac{P''P-P'^2}{P^2}(x)=-\sum_i\frac1{(x-x_i)^2}<0,
\]
so $P'^2>PP''$ there. At a root of $P$ the inequality reads $P'(x)^2\ge0$. By continuity (or directly) it holds everywhere.

(b) Let $r_1<\dots<r_s$ be the distinct roots, with multiplicities $m_1,\dots,m_s$, $\sum m_j=n$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(c), $r_j$ is a root of $P'$ of multiplicity at least $m_j-1$ (and exactly that, by the same proposition applied to $P'$, but we do not need it). By Rolle's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:mvt} in Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}), $P'$ has a root strictly between $r_j$ and $r_{j+1}$ for each $j<s$. These roots are all different, so $P'$ has at least $\sum_j(m_j-1)+(s-1)=n-1=\deg P'$ real roots with multiplicity: all of them.

(c) By (b) applied repeatedly, $Q=P^{(k-1)}$ has only real roots. Its Taylor coefficients at $0$ are $Q(0)=(k-1)!\,a_{k-1}$, $Q'(0)=k!\,a_k$, $Q''(0)=(k+1)!\,a_{k+1}$. Inequality (a) at $x=0$ gives $(k!)^2a_k^2\ge(k-1)!\,(k+1)!\,a_{k-1}a_{k+1}$, that is,
\[
a_k^2\ge\frac{k+1}k\,a_{k-1}a_{k+1}.
\]
If $a_{k-1}a_{k+1}\le0$ the claim $a_k^2\ge a_{k-1}a_{k+1}$ is trivial; otherwise it follows from $\frac{k+1}k>1$. (The inequality with the factor $\frac{k+1}k$ is a consequence of Newton's sharper inequalities, and its cousin, Maclaurin's inequality between the $\sigma_k$, is an advanced outlook treated in Hardy, Littlewood and P\'olya's \emph{Inequalities}.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:mason}
\idea The whole proof rests on one observation: a root of multiplicity $m$ of a polynomial is a root of multiplicity at least $m-1$ of its derivative. So the ``Wronskian'' $AB'-A'B$ inherits almost all the multiple roots of $A$, of $B$, and (by the relation $A+B=C$) of $C$, while its degree is small. Comparing degrees gives the bound. Part (d) then falls out, because raising to the $n$-th power multiplies degrees by $n$ but does not change the number of distinct roots.
\writeup (a) First, $W=AB'-A'B=A(C'-A')-A'(C-A)=AC'-A'C$, and symmetrically $W=B'C-BC'$ (replace $A=C-B$). Suppose $W=0$. If $A$ were a non-zero constant then $AB'=0$ gives $B$ constant and then $C$ constant, excluded; so $A$ is non-constant (the same for $B$), and it has a root $a$ of some multiplicity $m\ge1$. Then $a$ is a root of $AB'$ of multiplicity at least $m$, while it has multiplicity exactly $m-1$ in $A'$ (Taylor's formula, Proposition~\ref{prop:rootsfactor}(c)); from $AB'=A'B$, $a$ must be a root of $B$, contradicting $\gcd(A,B)=1$. So $W\ne0$.

(b) $(X-a)^{m-1}$ divides $A$ and $A'$, hence $W=AB'-A'B$. The same holds for roots of $B$ (using $W=AB'-A'B$) and of $C$ (using $W=AC'-A'C$). The polynomials $\prod_a(X-a)^{m_a-1}$ built from the roots of $A$, of $B$ and of $C$ respectively are pairwise coprime, since $A,B,C$ are; so their product, which is $\frac{ABC}{\prod_a(X-a)}$ over all distinct roots of $ABC$ (the roots of $A,B,C$ are distinct from each other), divides $W$.

(c) Taking degrees in (b): $\deg A+\deg B+\deg C-n_0(ABC)\le\deg W\le\deg A+\deg B-1$, so $\deg C\le n_0(ABC)-1$. Writing $B=C-A$ and $A=C-B$ (which does not change $W$ up to sign), the same argument bounds $\deg A$ and $\deg B$.

(d) Suppose $A^n+B^n=C^n$ with $A,B,C$ non-constant and pairwise coprime; then so are $A^n,B^n,C^n$, and (c) gives $n\deg C\le n_0(A^nB^nC^n)-1=n_0(ABC)-1\le\deg A+\deg B+\deg C-1$, and similarly for $n\deg A$ and $n\deg B$. Adding the three: $n(\deg A+\deg B+\deg C)\le3(\deg A+\deg B+\deg C)-3$, impossible for $n\ge3$. (For $n=2$ there are solutions, such as $(X^2-1)^2+(2X)^2=(X^2+1)^2$, the polynomial version of Pythagorean triples.)
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Notes on the challenges}

(1) The key fact is that $a-b$ divides $P(a)-P(b)$ for $P\in\Z[X]$ and integers $a,b$ (each $a^k-b^k$ is divisible by $a-b$). So $a-b\mid b-c$, $b-c\mid c-a$ and $c-a\mid a-b$: the three differences have the same absolute value $d>0$. But they add up to $0$, and three numbers equal to $\pm d$ cannot: their sum is an odd multiple of $d$.

(2) Let $P$ be monic with $\sup|P|=2^{1-n}$ and $Q=2^{1-n}T_n-P$, of degree $\le n-1$. At the distinct points $y_k=\cos(k\pi/n)$, in decreasing order for $0\le k\le n$, we have $(-1)^kQ(y_k)\ge0$. Lagrange interpolation gives
\[
0=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{Q(y_k)}{\prod_{j\ne k}(y_k-y_j)},
\]
by comparing coefficients of $X^n$. Exactly $k$ factors in the denominator are negative, so every summand is nonnegative. Each is therefore zero. Thus $Q$ has $n+1$ distinct roots and degree at most $n-1$, so $Q=0$. This proves uniqueness without a delicate count of repeated roots.

(3) For $\Phi_{p^k}(X)=\Phi_p(X^{p^{k-1}})$, substitute $X+1$ and apply Eisenstein again. For general $n$ this particular shifted Eisenstein argument need not apply, and the proof (Gauss 1801 for prime $n$, Kronecker and Dedekind in the 1850s for all $n$) uses the structure of the roots: one shows that if $\omega$ is a root of an irreducible factor then so is $\omega^p$ for every prime $p\nmid n$, by reducing modulo $p$. Volume~2 gives it in full.

so the count never settles. Weighting by $\frac1n$ turns each block into the same contribution $\log2$.

\writeup (a) The number of $n<10^k$ starting with $1$ is $1+10+\dots+10^{k-1}=\frac{10^k-1}9$. At $N=10^k-1$ the proportion is exactly
$\frac19$. At $N=2\cdot10^k-1$ it is $\frac{(10^k-1)/9+10^k}{2\cdot10^k-1}\to\frac{10/9}2=\frac59$. So the proportion has $\liminf\le\frac19$
and $\limsup\ge\frac59$ (in fact these are the extreme values, attained at these $N$ asymptotically), and there is no density.

(b) The block $[d\cdot10^j,(d+1)10^j[$ contributes $\sum\frac1n=\log\frac{d+1}d+O(10^{-j})$ (compare with $\int\frac{dx}x$). For
$10^k\le N<10^{k+1}$ the blocks with $j<k$ contribute $k\log(1+\frac1d)+O(1)$ and the partial block at most $\log10$. Divide by
$\log N=k\log10+O(1)$.

\tools Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences} (the harmonic sum and the integral of $\frac1x$).
\leads Benford's law has been observed in tax records, physical constants and populations, and is used to detect fraud. For the sequence $2^n$ the same leading-digit law holds with ordinary density, because $n\log_{10}2$ is equidistributed modulo $1$. That argument waits for a later volume.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bellasym}
\idea Dobi\'nski's series $\sum\frac{k^n}{k!}$ has a largest term near $k=e^u$, and bounding every term by an exponential in $k$ gives an upper bound that matches the
largest term up to a factor polynomial in $n$.

\writeup (a) The function $x\mapsto x^ne^{-ux}$ on $[0,\infty[$ has derivative $x^{n-1}e^{-ux}(n-ux)$, so it is maximal at $x=\frac nu$, with value $(\frac n{eu})^n$. Hence
$k^n\le(\frac n{eu})^ne^{uk}$ and
\[
b_n\le\frac1e\Bigl(\frac n{eu}\Bigr)^n\sum_k\frac{e^{uk}}{k!}=\Bigl(\frac n{eu}\Bigr)^ne^{e^u-1}.
\]
Since $ue^u=n$, we have $\ln n-\ln u=u$ and $e^u=\frac nu$, so $\ln b_n\le n(u-1)+\frac nu-1$.

(b) Let $m=\lceil e^u\rceil$, so $m=e^u+\eta$ with $0\le\eta<1$, and $b_n\ge\frac{m^n}{e\,m!}$. First $n\ln m\ge n\ln e^u=nu$. Next let
$d_m=\ln m!-\bigl(m+\frac12\bigr)\ln m+m$, as in Exercise~\ref{ex:stirling} of Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}. For $y>0$ the function $\ln(1+y)-\frac{2y}{2+y}$ has derivative $\frac{y^2}{(1+y)(2+y)^2}>0$ and vanishes at $0$, so $\ln\bigl(1+\frac1m\bigr)>\frac2{2m+1}$. Thus $d_m-d_{m+1}=\bigl(m+\frac12\bigr)\ln\bigl(1+\frac1m\bigr)-1>0$. Since $d_1=1$, we get $d_m\le1$, and therefore $\ln m!\le m\ln m-m+\frac12\ln m+1$. The function $\psi(x)=x\ln x-x$ has derivative $\ln x$, so
$\psi(m)\le\psi(e^u)+\eta\ln m\le ue^u-e^u+\ln m$. Since $ue^u=n$, $e^u=\frac nu$ and $\ln m\le u+1\le\ln n+1$,
\[
\ln b_n\ge nu-1-\Bigl(n-\frac nu+\frac32\ln m+1\Bigr)\ge n\Bigl(u-1+\frac1u\Bigr)-\frac32\ln n-4.
\]

(c) For $n\ge3$, $u\ge1$, so $u=L-\ln u\le L$, and then $\ln u\le\ln L$, so $u\ge L-\ln L$. Write $\ell=\ln L$. Then $\frac uL=1-\frac{\ln u}L\in\bigl[1-\frac\ell L,1\bigr]$, so
$\ln u=\ell+\ln\frac uL=\ell-\frac{\ln u}L+O(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2})=\ell-\frac\ell L+O(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2})$, and $u=L-\ln u=L-\ell+\frac\ell L+O(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2})$. Also $\frac1u=\frac1L+O(\frac\ell{L^2})$. By (a) and (b),
$\ln b_n=n(u-1+\frac1u)+O(\ln n)$, and
\[
u-1+\frac1u=L-\ell-1+\frac{1+\ell}L+O\Bigl(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2}\Bigr);
\]
the error $O(\ln n)$ is absorbed, since $\frac{\ln n}n=o(\frac{\ell^2}{L^2})$.

\tools{Volume~5, Chapter~2 (Dobi\'nski's formula); Chapter~\ref{ch:sequences}, Exercise~\ref{ex:stirling} (the shape of $n!$).}
\leads The expansion in (c) converges painfully slowly (for $n=1000$ its main terms give $4400$ against $\ln b_n=4438$), while $n(u-1+\frac1u)$ is off by only $1.92$.
Moser and Wyman (1955) found the next term by the saddle point: $b_n\sim\frac{e^{n(u-1+1/u)-1}}{\sqrt{1+u}}$, and indeed $-1-\frac12\ln(1+u)=-1.916$ for $n=1000$. The
lesson: an implicitly defined quantity like $u$ is often a better scale than its own expansion.
\end{solution}
