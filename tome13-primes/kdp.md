# Description

Is there always a prime between x and x + √x? Are there infinitely many pairs of primes at bounded distance? Do the primes contain arithmetic progressions of every length? For two thousand years such questions looked hopeless. In the last hundred years, and several times in the last fifteen, some of them were answered. This book proves the answers that are known, from the prime number theorem to the research frontier.

The first part is about the zeros of the zeta function. Few of them lie far from the critical line, and this is enough to find primes in every short interval. Siegel's theorem shows that a Dirichlet L-function cannot vanish too close to 1, at the price of a constant that nobody can compute.

The second part is about averages. Sieves meet the parity problem, which explains why they cannot find primes alone. The large sieve proves the Bombieri-Vinogradov theorem, the Riemann hypothesis on average for primes in progressions. Vinogradov's estimates of exponential sums over primes and the circle method of Hardy, Littlewood and Ramanujan count partitions, sums of powers, and sums of three primes.

The third part reaches the frontier. Goldston, Pintz and Yıldırım found small gaps between primes, and Maynard proved that bounded gaps occur infinitely often. Halász's theorem describes multiplicative functions. Additive combinatorics brings the sum-product phenomenon, Freiman's and Plünnecke's theorems, Roth's theorem on progressions of length three and Behrend's surprising construction, and the Gowers norms behind the Green-Tao theorem: the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The book ends on the critical line, with Hardy's theorem that infinitely many zeros lie on it, the mean square of zeta, and Montgomery's pair correlation of the zeros, where number theory meets random matrices.

The beauty of the subject is that the primes, which no formula describes, obey laws that can be proved. They are deterministic, yet statistically as regular as a random sequence, and each chapter shows how much of that regularity can be reached, and with which idea.

Every result is proved in full, except a short list of deep theorems that are quoted, each clearly marked where it appears. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas, checked against the original sources, and has pause-and-think questions, figures, and graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
