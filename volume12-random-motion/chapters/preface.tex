\chapter*{Preface}
In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown watched pollen grains in water under his microscope and saw tiny particles jiggle without
rest, in a dance that never repeated and never stopped. In 1905 Einstein explained it by the collisions of molecules, and
Perrin soon used it to count them; in 1923 Wiener turned the dance into a mathematical object, a random continuous curve. This book is about that curve and its descendants: random motion in all its forms, from the path of a
single particle to the eigenvalues of a large random matrix, which move like particles that refuse to collide.

The first part builds Brownian motion. Gaussian processes and Kolmogorov's continuity criterion give the path; L\'evy's modulus,
the zero set of dimension one half and the law of the maximum describe its fine structure.

The second part is the calculus that such a path requires, and what it does. Continuous martingales and the It\^o integral make it
possible to differentiate along a path that has no derivative anywhere, and It\^o's formula, L\'evy's characterisation, Tanaka's
formula and the local time are the result. Brownian motion then solves the heat equation (Feynman--Kac), changes probability
without changing the paths (Girsanov), solves the Dirichlet problem, decides which dimensions are recurrent, and in the plane is
invariant under every conformal map. Stochastic differential equations model a noisy world, from the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck velocity
to the Black--Scholes price, from Bessel processes to Dyson's Brownian motion of eigenvalues. Markov semigroups, Poisson random
measures and the L\'evy--It\^o decomposition describe every process with independent increments, jumps included.

The third part is networks and random matrices. A random walk on a graph and an electric current solve the same equations:
recurrence is infinite resistance to infinity, and random walks on groups lead to Kesten's theorem. Wigner's semicircle law,
the Marchenko--Pastur law and the phase transition of a signal in noise come next; then the unitary group, where the eigenvalues
follow the sine kernel and the characteristic polynomial splits into independent factors, which give its moments in closed form.
The last chapter builds the branching random walk, the family tree behind the largest values of a random characteristic
polynomial. An appendix counts spanning trees and Eulerian circuits with the Laplacian of a graph; the networks chapter uses it.

The aim throughout is to understand why the theorems are true, and to see them at work. Every theorem is proved, with one
clearly marked exception at the research frontier: the second-order term of the maximum of the characteristic polynomial,
admitted with its sources. Where the zeros of the zeta function are compared with eigenvalues, each statement says what it is:
a theorem, a conjecture, or an observation from a computation. Pause and think boxes ask you to guess before reading on. Each
chapter ends with exercises, graded from warm-ups ($\star$) to small gems ($\star\star\star$), each with a hint and a complete
solution that first explains how to find the idea and then writes it up.

The book assumes the probability of Volumes~4 and~11, the measure theory of Volume~7, the functional analysis of Volume~8 and
the Haar measure of Volume~9. Its beauty is in the meeting of the continuous and the discrete: one random curve, invented to
explain pollen grains, turns out to govern heat, harmonic functions, option prices and electrical networks, and through Dyson's
motion it reaches the eigenvalues whose laws the last chapters prove, and through them, against all expectation, the zeros of
the zeta function.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random yet obey laws. This floor is
the circulation: It\^o's formula, and eigenvalues that move and repel. The next floor takes up the rooms that were already inside
the integers when unique factorisation failed, and the family of functions to which $\zeta$ belongs. Selberg's Gaussian law for
$\log|\zeta|$, the arithmetic twin of this book's central limit theorem for $\log|\det(I-U)|$, waits for Volume~15.
