\chapter*{Preface}
Tome~12 opens the arithmetic side of Part~III. Tome~1 met the integers of quadratic fields, saw unique factorisation fail
in $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ and restored it with ideals, and stopped at the $p$-adic numbers. This tome takes the same questions to
any number field and then beyond: which primes split, why the class group is finite, how large the units are, what
happens near one prime at a time, how analysis counts ideals and primes, which abelian extensions exist, and finally the
arithmetic of curves, from conics to elliptic curves over $\Q$ and over finite fields, and the modular forms that
govern them. These are the objects whose statistics Tome~14 studies: class groups, Frobenius elements, points on
curves, Fourier coefficients of modular forms.

The tome has four parts. Chapters~1--5 are algebraic number theory: rings of integers, the factorisation of ideals and
the splitting of primes, the geometry of numbers, units, and cyclotomic fields with Kummer's work on Fermat's Last
Theorem. Chapters~6--9 are local and analytic: extensions of $\Q_p$, quadratic forms and the local--global principle,
zeta functions with the class number formula and Chebotarev's density theorem, and ad\`eles with Tate's thesis.
Chapter~10 is class field theory. Chapters~11--14 are arithmetic geometry: plane curves and Riemann--Roch, elliptic
curves and the Mordell--Weil theorem, the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields, and modular forms.

It assumes Tomes~1, 2 and~4, the parts of Tome~5 on Dirichlet characters and $\theta$-functions, and the Lebesgue
measure of Tome~6. Where a general theory belongs to Part~II (Dedekind domains, valuations and cohomology in Tome~9,
locally compact groups in Tome~7), the case needed here is proved in place, and the general version is pointed to.

The rules of the series hold. Every statement is proved, or, if quoted for culture, labelled as a remark that says
where it is proved. There is one exception, announced where it occurs: the two main theorems of class field theory
(Artin reciprocity and the existence theorem) are stated in Chapter~10 without proof, as \emph{admitted}, and every
consequence drawn from them is marked as conditional on them. Their proofs would fill a tome of their own, and the
references are given. Every solution starts with \emph{How to find it} before the \emph{Write-up}; ``Pause and
think'' boxes ask you to guess before you read on; exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} warm-up,
\textbf{$\star\star$} for a good afternoon, \textbf{$\star\star\star$} gems, and each has a hint and a complete
solution at the end of its chapter.

Each chapter has a companion Python notebook. Number theory today is an experimental science, and the notebooks use
PARI/GP from Python (the \texttt{cypari2} package) to compute integral bases, class groups, units and point counts,
and to check every number quoted in the text. Their computer-science threads are factoring polynomials over finite
fields, lattice reduction and lattice cryptography, number-theoretic transforms, elliptic-curve cryptography and point
counting, algebraic-geometry codes, and the theta series of lattices.
