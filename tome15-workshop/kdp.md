# Description

What happens after the last theorem of a book? A mathematician reads papers, tests ideas on a computer, trusts some heuristics and not others, checks proofs, writes them down, and chooses the next problem. This final tome of the series is a workshop in all of that, and every tool in it is used on real mathematics, proved in full.

The first part looks at research from the inside. It follows one problem through its whole life, Erdős's 1931 question on sets of integers with distinct subset sums: its bounds, its constructions, its prizes, and its disproof in 2026, whose pieces the reader checks. Then it shows how to read a paper in three passes, on a two-page note about the same problem. On the way the book meets a tempting simplification that turns out to be false, a lesson every researcher learns.

The second part is four guided readings of papers where probability meets number theory. The seven-shuffles theorem of Bayer and Diaconis is proved in its sharp form. Radziwill and Soundararajan's short proof of Selberg's central limit theorem for the zeta function is mapped onto the series and extended to several points, where a log-correlated structure appears. Wood's theory of random groups is read through random symmetric matrices and the sandpile groups of random graphs. And Harper's theorem that random multiplicative functions show better than square-root cancellation is explained through a branching random walk, where the heart of the argument, a ballot theorem below a barrier, is proved completely.

The third part is the workshop proper. Experimental mathematics: digits of pi computed from the middle, and formulas discovered by lattice reduction, with the LLL algorithm proved. Heuristics: why sums of three cubes for 33 and 42 were expected to exist, how large they had to be, and the factorisation that made Booker's search possible. Proof assistants: Lean at work, and the Curry-Howard correspondence that turns proofs into programs. Writing: Jordan's theorem for polygons, proved as a model of a statement that is easy to believe and must be written with care. Then seven open problems, from Singmaster's conjecture to the lonely runner and Sendov's conjecture, each with a partial result proved, and five projects that start with a computation and end with a question nobody has answered.

The beauty of research is that the exercises run out. For the first time nobody knows the answer, and the pleasure of mathematics is at its purest. This book shows what that feels like and gives the reader everything needed to start.

The book never reproves a result of an earlier tome: it says precisely where each one is proved. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas, checked against the sources, and has pause-and-think questions, figures, and graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
