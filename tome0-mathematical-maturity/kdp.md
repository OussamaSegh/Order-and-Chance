# Description

Why is 1³ + 2³ + … + n³ always a perfect square? Why, when n guests get their hats back at random, is the chance that nobody gets their own close to 1/e, whether there are ten guests or ten million? How can a chessboard of 64 squares be cut up and reassembled into a rectangle of 65? And why should you switch doors on a game show?

Every journey in mathematics starts with the same tools, and this book sharpens them until they are a pleasure to use. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas: the schoolboy Gauss adding the numbers from 1 to 100, Bombelli daring to compute with the square root of −1, Euler treating sin x as a polynomial of infinite degree to find 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … = π²/6, Wallis guessing an infinite product for π. The arguments follow, with their assumptions and forward references made explicit.

Eight chapters cover sums, binomial coefficients and induction; complex numbers and the roots of unity; polynomials; inequalities; sequences, series and limits; integrals and Taylor's formulas; linear algebra; and counting, with the first steps in probability. Each tool comes with the problems that make it shine: roots of unity that turn a sum of binomial coefficients into a picture, one inequality between means that settles a dozen optimisation problems, a single family of integrals that yields Wallis's product, the constant in Stirling's formula and the Gaussian integral.

The proofs include results a refresher often quotes: the intermediate value theorem, Stirling's formula with its constant, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, the spectral theorem, the law of large numbers. The few foundations assumed are named and proved later in the series. Every chapter rebuilds its tools from scratch before using them, so no half-remembered course is required. Most of the pages are exercises, graded from warm-ups to gems, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea and only then writes it down. Pause-and-think boxes ask you to guess before you read on, challenge boxes open doors to the research frontier, more than forty figures drawn to scale show what the formulas mean, portraits show the people, and each chapter ends with sources for every date and anecdote.

Eight new guided workshops explore false patterns, complex-number navigation, sensitive polynomial roots, the cost of imbalance, certified decimals, Taylor error bounds, least squares and Bayesian testing. Each includes a worked example, a mission, hints, a complete solution and a warning about a tempting mistake. Eight new vector graphs and three NASA/SpaceX photographs connect the ideas to pictures and applications. A final field guide helps choose the next tool.

Every chapter has a companion Python notebook in which the mathematics is tested, drawn and put to work, with exact checks of the new examples.

This volume opens a series that climbs from these foundations through number theory, algebra and probability to the place where they meet: the statistical behaviour of the prime numbers.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
