When a problem looks unfamiliar, first translate it into a question you can ask precisely. A good tool is often a change of language rather than a new formula.

\begin{description}
\item[A finite sum.] Write the first and last terms. Try a difference $F(k+1)-F(k)$, a double count, or the binomial basis. Do not lose an endpoint in a change of index.
\item[A complex expression.] Decide whether addition (coordinates) or multiplication (modulus and argument) dominates. Arguments are defined modulo $2\pi$; roots require all possible arguments.
\item[A polynomial identity.] Bound the degree of the difference, then exhibit enough distinct roots. If the coefficient domain is not a field, revisit the root-counting argument before using it.
\item[An inequality.] Identify the sign of every denominator. Homogeneity may remove a scale. Find the equality case before calculating: it often reveals the correct square or tangent.
\item[A sequence.] Prove it stays in its domain. Search for monotonicity, an invariant interval or a Cauchy estimate. Solving the fixed-point equation identifies possible limits, not convergence.
\item[A series.] Test the term first, then positivity or absolute convergence. A term tending to zero is necessary and insufficient. For a decimal, control the tail; for a rearrangement, justify the operation.
\item[An integral.] Distinguish a finite interval from an improper endpoint. Apply substitutions to truncated intervals first and justify the limiting step. A finite Taylor formula with a bounded remainder often avoids a more difficult interchange theorem.
\item[A matrix.] Ask for the kernel, image and invariant subspaces before expanding determinants. Similarity changes coordinates, not the map. An orthogonal projection is a best approximation; its residual is a certificate.
\item[A probability.] Specify outcomes, probabilities and the information revealed. Count ordered or unordered objects consistently. Add expectations freely; multiply probabilities only under the right independence hypothesis.
\end{description}

\section*{How to read a proof actively}
Cover the next line and predict what would make the current line useful. If you cannot, ask for a small example and a boundary case. At the end, remove one hypothesis and try to break the conclusion. This is not vandalism: it is how you learn which wall holds up the roof.

For a second reading, revisit the chapter workshops. Each has a complete worked example, a mission with a staged hint, a solution and a warning about a plausible wrong argument. Try to explain the warning to an imaginary friend who is bright, impatient and very fond of dividing by zero. If your friend survives the explanation, try a real one.

\section*{Computers as experimental partners}
Use exact checks where possible, draw examples, and compare numerical approximations against proved error bounds. The calculations can detect a mistake in an identity or an implementation; they cannot establish a universal theorem from finitely many cases. Floating-point output also carries rounding error. A symbolic equality, a truncation bound and a plotted approximation are three different kinds of evidence; keep their labels attached.

\section*{An investigation: binary words on a circle}
Here is a final problem whose useful language changes as the investigation
progresses. For $n\ge2$, choose a binary word $x_1\cdots x_n$, with each $x_i$
equal to $0$ or $1$. Require that no two adjacent entries are both $1$, including
the pair $(x_n,x_1)$. Positions are labelled: $001$ and $010$ are different words.
How many words satisfy the rule? What is the probability that a uniformly chosen
binary word does so?

\subsection*{Experiment and formulate}
For $n=2$, the words are $00,01,10$: three. For $n=3$, they are $000,001,010,100$:
four. For $n=4$, there are seven. These numbers suggest a recurrence, but a list
of counts does not prove one. Breaking the circle at a position leaves a condition
at each end; remember both ends rather than guessing how the ordinary path count
changes. This is the same habit as strengthening an induction hypothesis: keep
enough information for the next step.

\begin{think}
Use states $0,1$ for the most recently written symbol. Which transitions are
allowed? Write a $2\times2$ matrix with entry $1$ for an allowed transition and
$0$ for a forbidden one. What does an entry of its square count? Which entries
should be added to close a walk into a circle?
\end{think}

\subsection*{Hints}
The transition matrix is $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
The $(i,j)$ entry of $M^r$ counts walks of $r$ transitions from state $i$ to state
$j$. Prove this by induction from the multiplication rule. A circular word gives
a closed walk of $n$ transitions, with its starting position fixed, so use the
trace. The eigenvalues solve $X^2-X-1=0$. The probability question then uses a
count of all $2^n$ words, followed by a limit of a geometric sequence.

\subsection*{Complete solution}
\idea The constraint only remembers the current symbol. Encode that finite memory
as a matrix. Matrix multiplication sums over the possible intermediate symbols;
the diagonal enforces the closing condition. Diagonalisation then turns a
combinatorial count into two powers whose asymptotic sizes are easy to compare.

\writeup Index rows and columns by $0,1$. The allowed transitions are $0\to0$,
$0\to1$, and $1\to0$, giving the displayed matrix $M$. For $r=1$ its entries
count one-step walks. If $(M^r)_{ij}$ counts $r$-step walks, then
$(M^{r+1})_{ij}=\sum_{k=0}^1(M^r)_{ik}M_{kj}$ counts their disjoint extensions
according to the penultimate state $k$. Induction proves the interpretation.

A word $x_1\cdots x_n$ satisfying the rule is exactly the closed walk
$x_1\to x_2\to\cdots\to x_n\to x_1$. No rotation is performed or forgotten;
the symbol at position $1$ is the initial state. Thus the number is
$L_n=\operatorname{tr}(M^n)$. The distinct eigenvalues are
$\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $\psi=(1-\sqrt5)/2$, so $M$ is diagonalizable and
\[
L_n=\varphi^n+\psi^n.
\]
This formula also gives $L_0=2$ and $L_1=1$ for closed walks. These are convenient
initial values for the recurrence $L_{n+2}=L_{n+1}+L_n$, since both eigenvalues
satisfy $t^2=t+1$. The interpretation of $n=0$ is a zero-step walk with a chosen
state, not an empty circular word. Keeping the interpretation separate prevents
an erroneous initial count.

Under uniform probability on $\{0,1\}^n$ the required probability is
\[
p_n=\frac{L_n}{2^n}
=\left(\frac\varphi2\right)^n+\left(\frac\psi2\right)^n
\sim\left(\frac\varphi2\right)^n\longrightarrow0,
\]
because $|\psi|<\varphi<2$. The equivalence follows by dividing by the leading
term: the quotient is $1+(\psi/\varphi)^n\to1$. An exact expression and its
asymptotic approximation are now clearly distinguished.

\subsection*{A tempting wrong turn, and a new question}
Dividing $L_n$ by $n$ does not count words up to rotation. Some words return to
themselves after fewer than $n$ rotations: $0000$ has one distinct rotation,
whereas $0001$ has four. Equivalence classes need not have equal sizes. Finding
the count of unlabelled circles requires another idea, developed in Volume~2.
We have reached a new problem by identifying the assumption that an attractive
shortcut would need. That is a useful place to stop an investigation.
