# Description

The numbers a + b√-5 do not factor uniquely: 6 is 2 × 3 and also (1 + √-5)(1 - √-5). Dedekind's answer was to factor ideals instead, and unique factorisation came back. The carries you learned in school are a cocycle, and the cohomology of a group explains why they cannot be avoided. And a deck of n cards shuffled by random swaps needs about ½ n ln n of them, about 103 for 52 cards, to become random, and the change from ordered to random happens suddenly. This book is about the algebra behind these facts: the structures that modern mathematics is built from, and the representations that make groups computable.

It begins with the language. Categories, functors and adjunctions organise the constructions of algebra, and tensor products turn bilinear problems into linear ones. Then commutative algebra: Hilbert's basis theorem and Nullstellensatz, which make systems of polynomial equations into geometry; localisation and integral extensions; and Dedekind domains, with the class group that measures the failure of unique factorisation.

Two chapters of homological algebra follow. Projective resolutions define Tor and Ext, with the long exact sequences and the universal coefficient theorem; group cohomology proves Hilbert's Theorem 90, parametrises Pythagorean triples, classifies group extensions, and shows that carrying is a 2-cocycle.

The last four chapters are about representations. Wedderburn's theorem decomposes group algebras into matrix algebras; induction and Mackey's formula build representations from subgroups; characters prove Burnside's theorem on groups of order p^a q^b and Frobenius' theorem. Young diagrams give every irreducible representation of the symmetric group, with the Robinson-Schensted correspondence and the hook length formula, proved by a random walk inside the diagram. Fourier analysis on the symmetric group then proves the Diaconis-Shahshahani theorem: about ½ n ln n random transpositions shuffle n cards, with a sharp cutoff. Finally the Peter-Weyl theorem and Weyl's character formula describe the representations of the unitary groups, and the moments of the trace of a random unitary matrix turn out to count permutations by their longest increasing subsequence.

Every theorem is proved in full. Every chapter opens with the story and the people behind the idea, and ends with exercises chosen for their beauty, each with a hint and a solution that explains how the argument is found.

Written for the reader who wants to understand, not to pass an examination.
