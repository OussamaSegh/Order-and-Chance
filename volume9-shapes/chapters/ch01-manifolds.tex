\chapter{Smooth Manifolds}

\begin{center}\itshape
On a small patch of a sphere you can describe a point with two coordinates. Near the north pole one pair works; near the equator another pair is more convenient. Can calculus survive these changes of coordinates? A smooth manifold is a space on which it can. Its local coordinate systems need not fit into one global picture, but they must agree smoothly wherever they overlap.
\end{center}

On 10 June 1854 Bernhard Riemann gave his qualifying lecture at G\"ottingen. Of his three proposed topics, Gauss chose the third: ``the hypotheses which lie at the foundations of geometry''. Dedekind's account describes how deeply the lecture impressed Gauss. The text appeared in 1868, after Riemann's death.

Riemann's \emph{Mannigfaltigkeit}, a ``manifoldness'', was a collection whose nearby objects could be specified by continuously varying numbers. Geometry would come from measuring small displacements within that collection, rather than from an assumed surrounding Euclidean space. Chapter~4 develops this idea through lengths and curvature. First we need to say precisely how local coordinates fit together.

\portrait{riemann}{Bernhard Riemann (1826--1866). Photograph, 1863; photographer unrecorded. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

Weyl's 1913 treatment of Riemann surfaces defined spaces through overlapping coordinate neighbourhoods. Whitney's 1936 work established the smooth manifold framework in general dimensions and proved embedding theorems linking it to submanifolds of Euclidean space. We will prove a compact version using coordinate functions and smooth cutoffs; the stronger bounds will be quoted explicitly.

A two-link robot arm supplies a concrete example. Its two joint angles describe a torus of configurations, while the hand moves in the plane. Some configurations let the hand move in two independent directions; at others, those directions collapse into one. Derivatives detect that distinction. The inverse and implicit function theorems turn derivative information into local coordinate descriptions.

\section{Calculus in \texorpdfstring{$\R^n$}{R\^{}n}, quickly}

Dimensions are nonnegative integers. The space $\R^0$ consists of one point; its empty coordinate list and empty determinant, equal to one, follow the usual linear-algebra conventions. We allow empty open sets, where assertions about all their points are vacuous. On positive-dimensional Euclidean spaces use the Euclidean norm $|x|$ and operator norm $\|A\|=\sup_{|v|\le1}|Av|$.

\begin{definition}
For open $U\subset\R^n$, a map $f:U\to\R^m$ is \emph{differentiable at $a$} if a linear map $Df(a)$ satisfies
\[
f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)h+o(|h|)\quad(h\to0).
\]
It is \emph{$C^1$} when its derivative exists everywhere and depends continuously on the point. Inductively it is \emph{$C^k$} when $Df$ is $C^{k-1}$, and \emph{smooth}, or $C^\infty$, when it is $C^k$ for every finite $k$.
\end{definition}

The derivative's matrix is the Jacobian $(\partial_jf_i)$: take increments along the coordinate axes in the definition. In dimension zero there is only the unique map from the zero tangent space, so differentiability has no conditions to check.

\begin{proposition}[chain rule]\label{prop:chain}
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g$ at $f(a)$, then $gf$ is differentiable at $a$ and
\[
D(gf)(a)=Dg(f(a))Df(a).
\]
Composing $C^k$ maps gives a $C^k$ map.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $A=Df(a)$, $B=Dg(f(a))$, and write $f(a+h)-f(a)=k=Ah+\varepsilon(h)|h|$, where $\varepsilon(h)\to0$. Then $|k|\le(\|A\|+1)|h|$ for small $h$. The second differentiability expansion gives
\[
g(f(a)+k)-g(f(a))-BAh=B\varepsilon(h)|h|+\eta(k)|k|,
\]
with $\eta(k)\to0$; define the remainder as zero at $k=0$. Both terms are $o(|h|)$, proving the formula. For regularity, $D(gf)=(Dg\circ f)Df$. Matrix multiplication is bilinear and smooth, so induction on $k$ makes this derivative $C^{k-1}$ whenever $f,g$ are $C^k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:partials}
If all first partial derivatives of $f:U\to\R^m$ exist and are continuous, then $f$ is $C^1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Treat one component at a time. Near $a$, a sufficiently small coordinate box lies in $U$. Move from $a$ to $a+h$ along its coordinate directions, with $a^{(j)}=a+\sum_{i\le j}h_ie_i$. The one-variable mean value theorem gives
\[
f(a+h)-f(a)=\sum_{j=1}^nh_j\partial_jf(c_j),
\]
where $c_j$ lies on the $j$th segment (a zero increment contributes zero). Each $c_j\to a$, so continuity replaces $\partial_jf(c_j)$ by $\partial_jf(a)+o(1)$. The finite sum of errors is $o(|h|)$, proving differentiability with the continuous Jacobian. A zero-dimensional domain or target makes the assertion immediate.
\end{proof}

For vector-valued maps the mean value theorem need not give a single intermediate derivative: $t\mapsto e^{it}$ has equal values at $0,2\pi$ but never has zero derivative. A norm estimate does survive.

\begin{lemma}[mean value inequality]\label{lem:mvi}
If $f:U\to\R^m$ is $C^1$, $[x,y]\subset U$, and $\|Df\|\le L$ on that segment, then $|f(y)-f(x)|\le L|y-x|$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $f(y)=f(x)$, including every map to $\R^0$, the assertion is immediate. Otherwise choose $u=(f(y)-f(x))/|f(y)-f(x)|$. Apply the one-variable mean value theorem to $g(t)=\langle u,f(x+t(y-x))\rangle$. The chain rule gives $|g'(t)|\le L|y-x|$, so
$|f(y)-f(x)|=g(1)-g(0)\le L|y-x|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:glnopen}
The invertible matrices $\mathrm{GL}_n(\R)$ form an open subset of $M_n(\R)$, and inversion is smooth there. If $\|A-I\|\le1/2$, then $A$ is invertible with $\|A^{-1}\|\le2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The determinant is a polynomial, so its nonzero set is open. Cramer's rule expresses inverse entries as polynomials divided by that nonzero determinant, giving smoothness. Also
\[
|Av|\ge|v|-|(A-I)v|\ge\tfrac12|v|.
\]
Thus $A$ is injective, hence invertible in finite equal dimensions; putting $v=A^{-1}w$ gives the inverse bound. For $n=0$ the unique empty matrix is the identity and inverse, and the assertions hold directly.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The inverse function theorem}

Near a point, a differentiable map looks like its derivative. If that linear map is invertible and nearby derivatives remain close to it, we can solve $f(x)=y$ by a contraction. That closeness is why continuity of the derivative matters.

\begin{definition}
For open $U,V\subset\R^n$, a \emph{$C^k$ diffeomorphism} is a bijection $f:U\to V$ such that both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are $C^k$. A \emph{diffeomorphism} without a specified $k$ means a smooth one.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[inverse function theorem]\label{thm:inverse}
Let $f:U\to\R^n$ be $C^k$, where $U$ is open and $k\in\{1,2,\ldots,\infty\}$. If $a\in U$ and $Df(a)$ is invertible, there are open neighbourhoods $V$ of $a$ and $W$ of $f(a)$ for which $f|_V:V\to W$ is a $C^k$ diffeomorphism. For the inverse $g:W\to V$, differentiation gives $Dg(y)=Df(g(y))^{-1}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The zero-dimensional case is a map between single points. Otherwise translate the source and target and multiply by $Df(a)^{-1}$. It suffices to prove the result with $a=f(a)=0$ and $Df(0)=I$.

Choose $r>0$ with $\bar B(0,2r)\subset U$ and $\|Df(x)-I\|\le1/2$ there. The map $\phi(x)=x-f(x)$ is $1/2$-Lipschitz on this convex ball by the mean value inequality. Hence
\[
|f(x)-f(x')|\ge|x-x'|-|\phi(x)-\phi(x')|\ge\tfrac12|x-x'|,
\]
so $f$ is injective there. To find a preimage for $|y|<r$, set $T_y(x)=y+\phi(x)$. For $|x|\le2r$,
\[
|T_y(x)|\le|y|+\tfrac12|x|<2r.
\]
Thus $T_y$ is a contraction of the complete closed ball into itself. Banach's theorem (Volume~7, Theorem~2.12) gives a unique fixed point, lying in the open ball, and its fixed-point equation is $f(x)=y$.

Take $W=B(0,r)$ and $V=B(0,2r)\cap f^{-1}(W)$. They are open neighbourhoods and $f:V\to W$ is bijective. The preceding lower bound gives its inverse $g$ the estimate $|g(y)-g(y')|\le2|y-y'|$, in particular continuity.

Fix $x=g(y)$ and $A=Df(x)$. The matrix lemma makes $A$ invertible with inverse norm at most two. For small target increment $u$ write $h=g(y+u)-x$, so $|h|\le2|u|$. Differentiability of $f$ gives $u=Ah+\varepsilon(h)|h|$, and therefore
\[
g(y+u)-g(y)-A^{-1}u=-A^{-1}\varepsilon(h)|h|=o(|u|).
\]
This proves the inverse derivative formula. Its right side is continuous, so $g$ is $C^1$. If $g$ is $C^j$ with $j<k$, the formula, smooth matrix inversion and $Df\in C^{k-1}$ make $Dg$ a $C^j$ map, hence $g$ is $C^{j+1}$. Induct to $k$, or to every finite order when $k=\infty$, then undo the normalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
Set $f(x)=x+2x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$. Its derivative at zero is one, but at $x=1/(2\pi j)$ its derivative is $-1$. Why can it be injective on no interval about zero? Which uniform derivative estimate in the proof is unavailable?
\end{think}

\begin{theorem}[implicit function theorem]\label{thm:implicit}
Let $F:U\to\R^m$ be $C^k$, $k\ge1$ or $k=\infty$, on open $U\subset\R^n\times\R^m$. Suppose $F(a,b)=0$ and the derivative $D_yF(a,b)$ in the last $m$ variables is invertible. There are open neighbourhoods $A$ of $a$, $B$ of $b$, with $A\times B\subset U$, and a $C^k$ map $h:A\to B$ such that
\[
F(x,y)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=h(x)\qquad((x,y)\in A\times B).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Retain the $x$ coordinates and replace $y$ by the equations: put $\Phi(x,y)=(x,F(x,y))$. Its derivative is the invertible block matrix
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\D_xF&D_yF\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ at $(a,b)$. The inverse theorem gives a diffeomorphism $\Phi:V\to W$ near $(a,b)$ and $(a,0)$. Its inverse keeps the first coordinate, so write it as $(x,z)\mapsto(x,\psi(x,z))$.

Choose product neighbourhoods $A_0\times B\subset V$ of $(a,b)$ and restrict to the open set
\[
A=\{x\in A_0:(x,0)\in W,\ \psi(x,0)\in B\}.
\]
It contains $a$. Set $h(x)=\psi(x,0)$. The inverse identities give $F(x,h(x))=0$. Any other zero $(x,y)\in A\times B$ has the same $\Phi$ image $(x,0)$, and injectivity on $V$ forces $y=h(x)$. This also covers zero-sized coordinate blocks with their usual empty-matrix conventions.
\end{proof}

\section{Submanifolds of \texorpdfstring{$\R^N$}{R\^{}N}}\label{sec:submanifolds}

\begin{definition}
A subset $M\subset\R^N$ is a \emph{$k$-dimensional submanifold} if every $p\in M$ has an ambient open neighbourhood $U$ and a diffeomorphism $\varphi:U\to V\subset\R^N$ such that
\[
\varphi(M\cap U)=V\cap(\R^k\times\{0\}).
\]
Thus smooth coordinates turn $M$ locally into a coordinate plane.
\end{definition}

For a sphere, equations are easier to write than a straightening map. For a graph, a parametrization is easier still. The next theorem lets us use whichever description the problem supplies, provided its derivative and topology meet the stated conditions.

\begin{theorem}[four descriptions]\label{thm:fourdesc}
For $p\in M\subset\R^N$ and $0\le k\le N$, the following local descriptions are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Straightening.} A diffeomorphism $\varphi:U\to V$ of ambient open sets, with $p\in U$, satisfies $\varphi(M\cap U)=V\cap(\R^k\times\{0\})$.
\item \emph{Equations.} On some open $U\ni p$ there is a smooth $F:U\to\R^{N-k}$ with $M\cap U=F^{-1}(0)$ and $DF(x)$ surjective at every $x\in M\cap U$.
\item \emph{Graph.} After permuting ambient coordinates, there are open $\Omega\subset\R^k$, $B\subset\R^{N-k}$ and smooth $g:\Omega\to B$ such that $p\in\Omega\times B$ and $M\cap(\Omega\times B)=\{(x,g(x)):x\in\Omega\}$.
\item \emph{Parametrization.} There are open $\Omega\subset\R^k$, ambient open $U\ni p$, and smooth $\psi:\Omega\to\R^N$ such that $\psi$ is injective, each $D\psi(x)$ is injective, its image is $M\cap U$, and $\psi:\Omega\to M\cap U$ is a homeomorphism.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently any one of these descriptions at every point characterizes a $k$-dimensional submanifold.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a)$\Rightarrow$(b): take $F$ to be the last $N-k$ components of $\varphi$. Its zero set is the straightened plane's inverse image. Those rows of the invertible derivative $D\varphi$ are independent, giving surjectivity.

(b)$\Rightarrow$(c): choose $N-k$ independent columns of $DF(p)$ and permute them to the last positions. The derivative in those variables is invertible. The implicit theorem produces product neighbourhoods on which the zeros are the graph of a smooth $g$ taking values in the chosen second neighbourhood.

(c)$\Rightarrow$(d): set $\psi(x)=(x,g(x))$, then undo the coordinate permutation. Its derivative has an identity block, so is injective; projection onto the first coordinates is its continuous inverse on the image.

(d)$\Rightarrow$(a): write $\psi(x_0)=p$. Choose $k$ independent rows of $D\psi(x_0)$ and move them to the first positions. The map
\[
\Theta(x,z)=\psi(x)+(0,z),\qquad (x,z)\in\Omega\times\R^{N-k},
\]
has invertible derivative at $(x_0,0)$: its blocks are
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}D_1\psi&0\\D_2\psi&I\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with $D_1\psi$ invertible. Restrict it to a diffeomorphism $V'\to U'$ about those points.

We must exclude parts of $M$ whose parameters lie outside this inverse neighbourhood. The set $\Omega_0=\{x:(x,0)\in V'\}$ is open, so the homeomorphism hypothesis makes $\psi(\Omega_0)$ open in $M\cap U$. Write it as $M\cap O$ for ambient open $O\subset U$. Set $U''=U'\cap O$ and $V''=V'\cap\Theta^{-1}(U'')$. A point of $M\cap U''$ has the form $\psi(x)=\Theta(x,0)$ with $(x,0)\in V'$, and hence in $V''$. Conversely every such zero-$z$ point maps into $M\cap U''$. Thus $\Theta^{-1}:U''\to V''$ straightens exactly the desired part of $M$. Empty minors in the cases $k=0$ or $k=N$ are identities on zero-dimensional spaces, so the same argument includes them.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
The map $\psi(t)=(\sin2t,\sin t)$ on $0<t<2\pi$ is injective and has nonzero derivative, but its image crosses itself at the origin. Exercise~\ref{ex:nosubmanifold} will prove that the image is not a submanifold there. The parameter near zero approaches the image of $t=\pi$. Which topological hypothesis in (d) fails?
\end{think}

A zero-dimensional submanifold consists of isolated points; a full-dimensional one is open in the ambient space. For a nonempty submanifold, its dimension will be recovered as a tangent-space dimension in Section~\ref{sec:tangent}. The empty set satisfies the local definition vacuously, so its dimension, when specified, is an assigned convention.

\begin{example}[spheres]\label{ex:spheres}
For $n\ge0$, the sphere $S^n\subset\R^{n+1}$ is the zero set of $F(x)=|x|^2-1$. At a zero,
$DF(x)h=2\langle x,h\rangle$ is a nonzero linear form and hence surjective. Description (b) gives an $n$-dimensional submanifold, including the two isolated points of $S^0$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[the orthogonal group]\label{ex:on}
Symmetric $n\times n$ matrices form a real vector space $\mathrm{Sym}_n$ of dimension $n(n+1)/2$. For $F(X)=X^\top X-I$, the orthogonal matrices are $F^{-1}(0)$, and expansion gives
\[
DF(X)H=H^\top X+X^\top H.
\]
If $X^\top X=I$ and $S$ is symmetric, choose $H=XS/2$; then $DF(X)H=S$. The derivative is onto, so $\mathrm O(n)$ is a submanifold of dimension
$n^2-n(n+1)/2=n(n-1)/2$. It is closed and bounded, since its columns have unit length, hence compact. The positive-determinant subset $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ is open in $\mathrm O(n)$ and has the same dimension. In dimension zero both groups consist of the single empty identity matrix. For $n=2$, $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ is a circle; for $n=3$ its dimension matches ``an axis (two parameters) and an angle (one)''.
\end{example}

Chapter~7 will identify $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ with $\R P^3$ through the quaternion double cover. For now the equations alone have supplied its manifold structure.

\begin{example}[Cassini ovals]\label{ex:cassini}
For
\[
f(x,y)=(x^2+y^2)^2-2(x^2-y^2)=|z-1|^2|z+1|^2-1,
\]
the level $f=c$ fixes the product of distances to $\pm1$ at $\sqrt{1+c}$ when $c\ge-1$. Its gradient is
\[
\nabla f=(4x(x^2+y^2-1),\ 4y(x^2+y^2+1)).
\]
It vanishes only at $0$ and $(\pm1,0)$, with respective values $0,-1,-1$. Every nonempty level with $c>-1$, $c\ne0$, is therefore a smooth curve.

Polar coordinates give the component count: for $u=x^2+y^2>0$, the equation is $u^2-2u\cos2\theta=c$. If $c>0$, exactly one root is positive for every angle, giving one oval. If $-1<c<0$, positive roots exist in two angular intervals about the positive and negative horizontal axes; the two radial roots meet at each interval's ends, giving two ovals. At $c=0$ the branches form Bernoulli's crossing lemniscate; the tangent-velocity argument of Exercise~\ref{ex:nosubmanifold} also rules out a smooth submanifold at this crossing. At $c=-1$ only $(\pm1,0)$ remain, a zero-dimensional submanifold; below $-1$ the level is empty. A vanishing derivative of one defining function does not rule out a manifold: description (b) asks for the existence of a suitable defining function.
\end{example}

\section{Abstract manifolds}\label{sec:abstract}

The torus can be drawn in $\R^3$ as a doughnut or in $\R^4$ as $S^1\times S^1$. Its local calculus does not need either drawing. The space of lines through the origin of $\R^3$ is another example where coordinates are easier to supply than an ambient picture.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{topological $n$-manifold} is a Hausdorff space with a countable open basis in which each point has an open neighbourhood $U$ homeomorphic to an open subset of $\R^n$. We call this homeomorphism $\varphi:U\to\varphi(U)$ a \emph{chart}. Two charts are \emph{compatible} when both transition maps
\[
\varphi'\varphi^{-1}:\varphi(U\cap U')\to\varphi'(U\cap U'),\qquad
\varphi\varphi'^{-1}
\]
are smooth on their domains. An \emph{atlas} is a covering family of pairwise compatible charts.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:maxatlas}
Every atlas $\mathcal A$ lies in a unique maximal atlas: the family $\bar{\mathcal A}$ of all charts compatible with every chart in $\mathcal A$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take two charts in $\bar{\mathcal A}$ and a point in their overlap. Choose a chart $\varphi_0$ of $\mathcal A$ about that point. Locally their transition factors as
\[
\varphi'\varphi^{-1}=(\varphi'\varphi_0^{-1})(\varphi_0\varphi^{-1}),
\]
a composition of smooth maps; the inverse factors in the reverse direction. Smoothness is local, so the two charts are compatible everywhere on the overlap. Any atlas extending $\mathcal A$ consists of charts in this family. Hence the family is itself the unique maximal extension.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{smooth manifold} is a topological manifold with a maximal atlas, its \emph{smooth structure}. Giving any atlas determines that structure. A map $f:M\to N$ is \emph{smooth} if it is continuous and all coordinate expressions $\psi f\varphi^{-1}$ are smooth where defined. A \emph{diffeomorphism} is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse.
\end{definition}

It is enough to check one source chart near each point and one target chart near its image: other choices compose with smooth transitions. Each chart is itself a diffeomorphism onto its Euclidean coordinate domain. A zero-manifold is discrete and, by its countable basis, at most countable. The empty manifold is allowed with an assigned dimension.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:subisman}
A $k$-dimensional submanifold $M\subset\R^N$, with its subspace topology, is a smooth manifold. For each straightening $\varphi:U\to V$, its chart is $\pi\varphi|_{M\cap U}$, where $\pi$ projects to the first $k$ coordinates. The restriction to $M$ of a smooth ambient map is smooth for this structure.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The Hausdorff property and countable basis pass from Euclidean space to its subspace. The chart image is the open set $\{x:(x,0)\in V\}$, and its inverse is $x\mapsto\varphi^{-1}(x,0)$. Transitions have the form $x\mapsto\pi\varphi'\varphi^{-1}(x,0)$, so are smooth. An ambient smooth map $f$ restricts in these coordinates to $x\mapsto f(\varphi^{-1}(x,0))$, also smooth.
\end{proof}

We will also use the corresponding target fact. A map into an embedded submanifold is smooth exactly when its ambient composite is smooth. In a target straightening chart the ambient expression takes values $(g(x),0)$, and its first coordinates $g$ are exactly the submanifold coordinates. The same observation works in a manifold chart once embedded submanifolds are defined below.

\begin{example}[products and tori]
Product charts on $M\times N$ have product transition maps, hence give dimension $\dim M+\dim N$. The product is Hausdorff and has a countable product basis. In particular $T^n=(S^1)^n$ is an $n$-manifold. As a subset of $\R^{2n}$ it is also defined by $x_{2j-1}^2+x_{2j}^2=1$; the derivatives of these $n$ equations are independent, giving the same smooth structure by the product charts. The empty product $T^0$ is one point.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[projective space]\label{ex:rpn}
The real projective space $\R P^n$ consists of lines in $\R^{n+1}$, with $[x]=[x_0:\cdots:x_n]$ identifying $x$ and $\lambda x$ for $\lambda\ne0$. Give it the quotient topology from $\R^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$. The quotient map $\pi$ is open, since $\pi^{-1}\pi(O)=\bigcup_{\lambda\ne0}\lambda O$ is open for open $O$.

On $U_i=\{[x]:x_i\ne0\}$ define $\varphi_i([x])=(x_j/x_i)_{j\ne i}$. This is a homeomorphism to $\R^n$: its inverse inserts $1$ in position $i$ and applies the quotient map, while its forward continuity follows from the quotient property. To change from chart $i$ to chart $j$, divide the inserted coordinate list by its nonzero $j$th coordinate and omit that coordinate. These rational expressions and their inverses are smooth.

The $n+1$ chart domains give a countable basis. To check Hausdorffness, use the continuous map
\[
[x]\longmapsto\frac{xx^\top}{|x|^2}.
\]
It is injective because its matrix is the orthogonal projection onto the line $\R x$. Distinct image matrices have disjoint neighbourhoods, whose inverse images separate the original points. Thus $\R P^n$ is a smooth $n$-manifold. It is compact, being the image of $S^n$ under $\pi$; for $n=0$ it is one point.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Local Euclidean coordinates alone do not force the Hausdorff property. Glue two real lines along every nonzero point. The resulting line has two origins with identity chart transitions, but every neighbourhood of one origin meets every neighbourhood of the other. The sequence $1/j$ converges to both. The countable-basis condition is separate too; among other things it will give us a countable collection of coordinate cutoffs for global constructions.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Bump functions and partitions of unity}

A real analytic function on a connected open domain that vanishes on a nonempty open subset vanishes everywhere. Smooth functions can instead be one near a point and zero outside a small neighbourhood. These cutoffs let us extend local constructions and add them without disturbing distant points.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bump}
The function $h(t)=e^{-1/t}$ for $t>0$ and $h(t)=0$ for $t\le0$ is smooth. Consequently
\[
\beta(t)=\frac{h(t)}{h(t)+h(1-t)}
\]
is smooth, equals zero for $t\le0$ and one for $t\ge1$. The function $b(x)=\beta(2-|x|)$ on $\R^n$ is smooth, lies between zero and one, equals one on $\bar B(0,1)$ and vanishes outside $B(0,2)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $t>0$, every derivative of $h$ has the form $P(1/t)e^{-1/t}$ for a polynomial $P$, by differentiation and induction. It tends to zero faster than any positive power of $t$. Starting with continuity at zero, the quotient $h^{(j)}(t)/t\to0$ proves that the next derivative exists there and equals zero. Its positive-side formula also tends to zero, so that derivative is continuous. Induction proves smoothness across zero.

The denominator defining $\beta$ is positive, because $t$ and $1-t$ cannot both be nonpositive. Its values and bounds follow immediately. The norm is smooth off zero; near zero $b$ is constant one, so no norm singularity remains. In $\R^0$, $b$ is simply one on its single point, with compact support.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[partitions of unity]\label{thm:partition}
For every open cover $(O_i)_{i\in I}$ of a smooth manifold $M$, there is a countable family of smooth $\rho_j:M\to[0,1]$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
\item every support $\operatorname{supp}\rho_j=\overline{\{\rho_j\ne0\}}$ is compact and lies in some $O_i$;
\item every point has a neighbourhood meeting only finitely many supports;
\item $\sum_j\rho_j=1$.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The empty manifold admits the empty family. Otherwise small coordinate balls with their closures inside a chart have compact closures in $M$; Hausdorffness makes those closures closed. Restrict a countable basis to its members lying in such balls. This is still a basis, all of whose members have compact closure. Enumerate it as $B_1,B_2,\ldots$, repeating members if it is finite.

Build compact sets $K_m$ exhausting $M$. Begin with $K_1=\bar B_1$. Given $K_m$, choose $j\ge m+1$ so that $B_1,\ldots,B_j$ cover it, and let $K_{m+1}=\bigcup_{i\le j}\bar B_i$. Then $K_m\subset\operatorname{int}K_{m+1}$ and $B_m\subset K_m$, so the $K_m$ exhaust $M$. Put $K_0=K_{-1}=\emptyset$.

The compact layer $A_m=K_m\setminus\operatorname{int}K_{m-1}$ lies in the open region $W_m=\operatorname{int}K_{m+1}\setminus K_{m-2}$. About each $p\in A_m$ choose a chart centred at zero and a small $r$ such that the closed coordinate ball of radius $2r$ lies inside some $O_i\cap W_m$. Scale the preceding bump to this ball and extend it by zero outside the chart. Its compact support stays inside the chart, so this extension is smooth. It equals one on the smaller radius-$r$ ball. Finitely many smaller balls cover $A_m$; retain their bumps. Doing this for each layer produces a countable family $\sigma_j$ whose positive regions cover $M$.

The family is locally finite. If a point lies in $\operatorname{int}K_m$, that neighbourhood misses every $W_{m'}$ with $m'\ge m+2$, and there are only finitely many retained bumps in the earlier layers. Thus $\sigma=\sum_j\sigma_j$ is smooth and everywhere at least one. Set $\rho_j=\sigma_j/\sigma$. Normalization preserves supports and local finiteness and makes their sum one.
\end{proof}

\section{Tangent spaces}\label{sec:tangent}

For a sphere, a curve's velocity at $p$ lies in the plane perpendicular to $p$. The following calculation turns that picture into a definition independent of any particular equations.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tangentsub}
Let $M\subset\R^N$ be a $k$-dimensional submanifold. At $p\in M$, let $T_pM$ be the set of velocities $\gamma'(0)$ of smooth curves in $M$ with $\gamma(0)=p$, defined on an interval about zero. For any local equation map $F$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:fourdesc}(b) and parametrization $\psi$ from (d), with $\psi(x_0)=p$,
\[
T_pM=\operatorname{im}D\psi(x_0)=\ker DF(p).
\]
It is a $k$-dimensional vector space, so a nonempty submanifold determines its dimension.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every $v\in\R^k$ gives the curve $\psi(x_0+tv)$ with velocity $D\psi(x_0)v$. Conversely, along any curve in $M$, the local equation $F\gamma=0$ gives $DF(p)\gamma'(0)=0$. Therefore
\[
\operatorname{im}D\psi(x_0)\subset T_pM\subset\ker DF(p).
\]
The first and last spaces have dimension $k$, by injectivity of $D\psi$ and surjectivity of $DF$. They must coincide, proving the claim even though we had not initially assumed the middle set was a vector space.
\end{proof}

Thus $T_pS^n=p^\perp$, and $T_I\mathrm O(n)$ consists of skew-symmetric matrices $H^\top+H=0$. Chapter~7 will use this latter space as a Lie algebra.

On an abstract manifold there is no surrounding vector space for a velocity to belong to. Instead record how that velocity differentiates smooth functions. Along a curve, $f\mapsto(f\gamma)'(0)$ is linear and obeys the product rule.

\begin{definition}
A \emph{tangent vector at $p$} on a smooth manifold $M$ is a linear map $v:C^\infty(M)\to\R$ satisfying the Leibniz rule
\[
v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+g(p)v(f).
\]
Such maps, also called derivations at $p$, form the vector space $T_pM$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:derivlocal}
A tangent vector kills constant functions, and $v(f)=v(g)$ whenever $f=g$ on a neighbourhood of $p$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The identity $v(1)=v(1\cdot1)=2v(1)$ gives $v(1)=0$, then linearity handles all constants. If $f-g$ vanishes on open $O\ni p$, choose a chart bump $\chi$ equal to one at $p$, supported in $O$. The function $\chi(f-g)$ vanishes everywhere, so the product rule gives
$0=\chi(p)v(f-g)+(f-g)(p)v(\chi)=v(f-g)$.
\end{proof}

This lemma lets us differentiate local coordinate functions: multiply them by a bump equal to one near $p$, extend by zero, and the answer is independent of the extension.

\begin{lemma}[Hadamard]\label{lem:hadamard}
For smooth $f$ on a ball $B\subset\R^n$ centred at $a$, there are smooth $g_i$ with $g_i(a)=\partial_if(a)$ and
\[
f(x)=f(a)+\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-a_i)g_i(x).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Integrate along the segment from $a$ to $x$:
\[
f(x)-f(a)=\int_0^1\sum_i(x_i-a_i)\partial_if(a+t(x-a))\,\dd t.
\]
Take $g_i$ to be the integral of the corresponding partial derivative. On each smaller closed ball, all derivatives of the integrand are continuous on its product with $[0,1]$, so differentiation under the integral proves smoothness. At $x=a$ the integral equals $\partial_if(a)$. In dimension zero the empty sum states that a function on a point is constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tangentbasis}
For a chart $(U,\varphi)$ about $p$, with coordinates $(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$, the derivations
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_p:f\longmapsto
\partial_i(f\varphi^{-1})(\varphi(p))
\]
form a basis of $T_pM$. Consequently $T_pM$ has dimension $n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
They satisfy Leibniz by the ordinary partial-derivative product rule. Translate coordinates so $\varphi(p)=0$ and choose $B(0,\varepsilon)\subset\varphi(U)$. A bump $\chi$ equal to one near $p$ and with compact support inside this ball gives globally smooth functions $X^j=\chi x^j$, extended by zero. Their coordinate partials at $p$ are $\delta_{ij}$, proving independence.

For any $v\in T_pM$, apply Hadamard's lemma to $f\varphi^{-1}$. With local $g_i$ as there, extend $G_i=\chi(g_i\varphi)$ by zero. Near $p$,
$f=f(p)+\sum_iX^iG_i$. Locality and the Leibniz rule imply
\[
v(f)=\sum_i v(X^i)G_i(p)
=\sum_i v(X^i)\partial_i(f\varphi^{-1})(0),
\]
since $X^i(p)=0$. Thus the coordinate derivations span. For $n=0$, every function is locally constant at $p$, so every derivation is zero and the empty basis has dimension zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{differential} of smooth $F:M\to N$ is
\[
dF_p:T_pM\to T_{F(p)}N,\qquad (dF_pv)(g)=v(gF).
\]
\end{definition}

Composition and Leibniz show that this really is a tangent vector at $F(p)$ and depends linearly on $v$. Applying the definition twice gives $d(GF)_p=dG_{F(p)}dF_p$. In coordinate bases its matrix is the Jacobian of the coordinate expression of $F$. Thus differentials of diffeomorphisms are invertible, and the smooth dimension of a nonempty manifold does not depend on its compatible chart choice.

For an embedded submanifold, the velocity and derivation descriptions agree. If $\gamma'(0)=w$ and $\gamma=\psi c$ locally, then $c=\psi^{-1}\gamma$ is smooth: in a straightening neighbourhood the inverse parametrization is smooth by the four descriptions. The linear map $D\psi(x_0)$ is an isomorphism from $\R^k$ onto its image $T_pM$; write its inverse on that image as $L$. Then
\[
(f\gamma)'(0)=D(f\psi)(x_0)Lw.
\]
This depends only on $w$, satisfies Leibniz, and is injective in $w$ because the restricted ambient coordinate functions recover its components. Both tangent spaces have dimension $k$, so it is an isomorphism. We can use either description.

\section{The regular value theorem}

\begin{definition}
For smooth $F:M\to N$, a point $p$ is \emph{regular} if $dF_p$ is surjective, and \emph{critical} otherwise. A value $q\in N$ is \emph{regular} if every point of $F^{-1}(q)$ is regular, including an empty fibre; it is \emph{critical} otherwise. An \emph{immersion} has injective differentials everywhere, a \emph{submersion} has surjective differentials everywhere, and an \emph{embedding} is an immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
A subset $S$ of an $n$-manifold $M$ is a \emph{$k$-dimensional embedded submanifold} if every point of $S$ has a chart $(U,\varphi)$ of $M$ with
\[
\varphi(S\cap U)=\varphi(U)\cap(\R^k\times\{0\}).
\]
\end{definition}

Projecting these straightening charts to their first $k$ coordinates gives $S$ a smooth structure, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:subisman}. Inclusion is smooth with injective differential; in coordinates it is $x\mapsto(x,0)$. A map taking values in $S$ is smooth exactly when its composite with inclusion is smooth, by the same first-coordinate argument used earlier.

\begin{theorem}[regular value theorem]\label{thm:regularvalue}
Let $F:M\to N$ be smooth with dimensions $m,n$. If $q$ is a regular value and $S=F^{-1}(q)$ is nonempty, then $S$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $m-n$, and its tangent space, identified by inclusion, is $T_pS=\ker dF_p$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At $p\in S$, surjectivity forces $m\ge n$. Choose charts centred at $p,q$ and restrict the source so its image lies in the target chart. The coordinate expression $G$ has zero set corresponding to $S$ and surjective derivative at its zeros. The equations-to-straightening part of Theorem~\ref{thm:fourdesc} supplies the required local chart. The dimension is $m-n$.

Since $F$ is constant on $S$, differentiating its composite with inclusion gives $T_pS\subset\ker dF_p$. Both spaces have dimension $m-n$, so they coincide. An empty fibre is still a regular-value fibre by definition, but no nonempty manifold of negative dimension is being asserted. When $n=0$, all differentials are onto and the fibres are open subsets of $M$, consistent with dimension $m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:embedding}
An embedding $F:M\to N$ has embedded-submanifold image, and is a diffeomorphism onto that image. If $M$ is compact, every injective immersion $M\to N$ is an embedding.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose source and target charts near $p,F(p)$. The coordinate map is an injective immersion, and its image is locally open in the full image because $F$ is a homeomorphism onto that image. The parametrization-to-straightening argument in Theorem~\ref{thm:fourdesc} applies, after restricting target neighbourhoods to exclude other image pieces. It supplies embedded charts in which $F$ is $x\mapsto(x,0)$. The inverse on the image is projection and hence smooth.

For compact $M$, a continuous injection into Hausdorff $N$ sends closed sets, which are compact, to compact and hence closed sets. Its inverse on the image is continuous. Thus an injective immersion is an embedding. Empty source cases are vacuous.
\end{proof}

\section{Sard's theorem in equal dimensions}

A derivative can lose rank at many source points; a constant map loses rank everywhere when its target has positive dimension. The images of those points occupy much less space. Sard's theorem makes that statement precise. The equal-dimensional case needed in Chapter~4 has a short $C^1$ proof.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:nullsets}
A $C^1$ map $f:U\to\R^n$ on open $U\subset\R^n$ sends every Lebesgue-null subset $Z$ to a null subset.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $n=0$, Lebesgue measure is counting measure on a point, and the only null set is empty. Suppose $n\ge1$. Cover $U$ by countably many closed cubes lying inside $U$; it is enough to consider $Z\cap Q$ for one such cube. Compactness bounds $\|Df\|$ on $Q$ by some $L\ge1$, and the mean value inequality makes $f$ $L$-Lipschitz there.

For $\varepsilon>0$, nullness and outer regularity (Volume~7, Proposition~7.12) give a countable cube cover $C_j$ of $Z\cap Q$, with side lengths $s_j$ and $\sum_js_j^n<\varepsilon$. One may obtain it from the dyadic-cube construction used there. For each nonempty $C_j\cap Q$, the image has diameter at most $L\sqrt n\,s_j$ and fits inside an axis-aligned cube of side $2L\sqrt n\,s_j$. Thus the image of $Z\cap Q$ has outer measure at most $(2L\sqrt n)^n\varepsilon$. Let $\varepsilon$ tend to zero, then take the countable union over $Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Sard, equal dimensions]\label{thm:sard}
For a $C^1$ map $f:U\to\R^n$ on open $U\subset\R^n$, let $C=\{x:\det Df(x)=0\}$. Then $f(C)$ has Lebesgue measure zero.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $n=0$, the empty derivative has determinant one, so $C$ is empty. Let $n\ge1$. Again restrict to a closed cube $Q\subset U$, of side $\ell$. Put $M=\max_Q\|Df\|$. For any $\varepsilon>0$, uniform continuity of $Df$ on $Q$ (Volume~7, Theorem~4.9) gives a distance $\delta>0$ within which derivative differences have norm at most $\varepsilon$. Subdivide $Q$ into $k^n$ cubes of diameter $d=\sqrt n\,\ell/k\le\delta$.

If a subcube $Q'$ contains a critical point $c$, subtract the linear map $Df(c)$ from $f$. Its derivative differs from zero by at most $\varepsilon$ on $Q'$, so
\[
|f(x)-f(c)-Df(c)(x-c)|\le\varepsilon|x-c|\le\varepsilon d
\qquad(x\in Q').
\]
The singular linear image lies in a hyperplane $H$, and has norm at most $Md$. Consequently $f(Q')$ lies inside the $\varepsilon d$-neighbourhood of a radius-$Md$ disc in $f(c)+H$. In coordinates with last axis normal to $H$, this fits in a box with $n-1$ sides $2(M+\varepsilon)d$ and one side $2\varepsilon d$. Rotation invariance of Lebesgue measure (Volume~7, Theorem~9.7) gives
\[
\lambda^*(f(Q'))\le2^n(M+\varepsilon)^{n-1}\varepsilon d^n.
\]
Only cubes containing critical points are needed, and there are at most $k^n$ of them. Since $k^nd^n=n^{n/2}\ell^n$,
\[
\lambda^*(f(C\cap Q))\le
2^n(M+\varepsilon)^{n-1}n^{n/2}\ell^n\varepsilon.
\]
Let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. This proves nullness on each $Q$, then on all of $U$ by the countable cube cover.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
At a critical point the derivative puts a whole small cube into a hyperplane. The nonlinear error thickens that flat image by only $\varepsilon d$. Its volume is therefore proportional to $\varepsilon d^n$, rather than merely $d^n$. Summing over roughly $d^{-n}$ cubes leaves a bound proportional to $\varepsilon$, which can be made arbitrarily small. The critical points may fill the source, but their values cannot fill an open region of a positive-dimensional target.
\end{beauty}

A subset $Z\subset N$ is \emph{null on the manifold} when its image in every target chart is Lebesgue-null. The preceding null-set lemma applied to transition maps makes this independent of the chosen atlas.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:sardmfd}
For a smooth map between manifolds of equal dimension, the critical values form a null set and the regular values are dense in the target.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A countable basis lets us choose countably many source charts covering $M$: choose a containing chart for each basis member that lies in some chart domain. Fix a target chart $(V,\psi)$. In each source chart $\varphi_i$, restrict to the preimage of $V$ and use the equal-dimensional theorem for $\psi F\varphi_i^{-1}$. Invertible chart differentials preserve the condition of being critical. Thus the critical values in $V$ are a countable union of null coordinate sets, hence null. In positive dimension a nonempty open set has positive measure, so the complement is dense. In dimension zero every differential is the surjective map between zero vector spaces, so every value is regular. Empty source or target cases satisfy the assertions as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
We quote the general Morse--Sard theorem: for $n\ge1$, a $C^r$ map $\R^m\to\R^n$ has null critical values if $r\ge\max(m-n+1,1)$. Morse proved the scalar-target case in 1939 and Sard the general result in 1942. The equal-dimensional proof above does not establish the general case. Whitney's 1935 example of a $C^1$ function on $\R^2$, nonconstant along a connected arc of critical points, shows why more smoothness can be needed: its critical values contain an interval. When $m<n$, every differential fails to be surjective, and Exercise~\ref{ex:minisard} proves nullness of the whole image directly. A zero-dimensional target has no critical points.
\end{remark}

\section{Every compact manifold lives in some \texorpdfstring{$\R^N$}{R\^{}N}}

\begin{theorem}[Whitney, easy version]\label{thm:whitney}
Every compact smooth manifold embeds into some Euclidean space $\R^N$ and is therefore diffeomorphic to an embedded submanifold there.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The empty manifold has its unique embedding into $\R^0$. For nonempty $M$ of dimension $n$, choose about each point a chart $(U_i,\varphi_i)$ and a bump $\rho_i$ supported compactly inside that chart, equal to one on a smaller open $W_i$. Compactness lets finitely many $W_i$, $1\le i\le s$, cover $M$.

Extend $\rho_i\varphi_i$ and $\rho_i$ by zero off the chart. They are smooth because their supports stay compactly inside it. Define
\[
F=(\rho_1\varphi_1,\ldots,\rho_s\varphi_s,\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_s)
:M\to\R^{sn+s}.
\]
The last coordinates record which cutoffs are active; they prevent chart values zero from being confused with points outside that chart.

If $F(p)=F(q)$, choose $i$ with $p\in W_i$. Then $\rho_i(q)=\rho_i(p)=1$, so $q$ lies in the same chart, and equality of the $\rho_i\varphi_i$ coordinates gives $\varphi_i(p)=\varphi_i(q)$. Thus $p=q$. Near $p\in W_i$, these coordinates equal the chart itself, so $dF_p$ is injective. We have an injective immersion from a compact manifold, hence an embedding by Proposition~\ref{prop:embedding}. In dimension zero the coordinate blocks are empty, but the cutoff coordinates still distinguish the finitely many points, and injectivity of the differential from the zero tangent space is automatic.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Whitney's 1936 theorem gives the much sharper embedding into $\R^{2n+1}$, for compact and noncompact smooth $n$-manifolds. For the compact case, the construction above can be followed by successive projections: appropriate Sard arguments exclude directions that identify two points or kill a tangent direction while the ambient dimension is too large. The noncompact case requires an additional global construction; neither sharper theorem is proved here.

In 1944 Whitney obtained embeddings into $\R^{2n}$ for $n\ge1$. The restriction excludes zero-dimensional counterexamples such as two points, which cannot embed into the single point $\R^0$. The projective plane illustrates the strength of the positive-dimensional bound: it embeds into $\R^4$ by Exercise~\ref{ex:rp2}, but does not embed into $\R^3$, a fact we quote without proof.
\end{remark}

Charts let us do calculus without choosing an ambient picture. The compact embedding proves that this viewpoint still describes familiar submanifolds somewhere in Euclidean space, while leaving us free to choose coordinates suited to the problem. For the robot arm, its configuration torus and its hand-position map serve different jobs: one describes possible states, the other the output of each state.

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A sphere's coordinate patch is not flat as a geometric surface, but its coordinates take values in a Euclidean open set, where ordinary calculus applies. Smooth transitions make those local calculations agree. The tangent space can be found from parametrized velocities, kernels of equation derivatives, or derivations on functions; all three descriptions give the same linear information.

Invertible derivatives supplied inverse coordinates, and surjective derivatives supplied level-set coordinates. The arm's singular configurations show why the rank condition matters. Sard's equal-dimensional theorem explains why their output values are small in measure, while smooth bumps let us combine local coordinates into the compact embedding.

A tangent vector so far belongs to one point. Chapter~2 chooses vectors smoothly at every point and follows their arrows to build flows. It will also ask which spheres can carry such an arrow field without a zero.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:cubeinverse}
\emph{Local inverses and their limits.} (a) Prove that $x\mapsto x^3$ is a smooth bijection of $\R$ whose inverse is not differentiable at zero. (b) For $f(x,y)=(e^x\cos y,e^x\sin y)$, show that the derivative is invertible everywhere but $f$ is not injective. Describe the inverses supplied locally by the inverse function theorem and relate them to the complex exponential.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:matrixgroups}
\emph{Special and unitary matrices.} Let $n\ge1$. (a) Show that $\mathrm{SL}_n(\R)=\{\det=1\}$ has dimension $n^2-1$ as a submanifold of $M_n(\R)$. (b) Show that $\mathrm U(n)=\{X\in M_n(\C):X^*X=I\}$ is a real submanifold of dimension $n^2$. (c) Prove that $\det:\mathrm U(n)\to S^1$ is a submersion and deduce that $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ has dimension $n^2-1$. (d) Find their tangent spaces at the identity.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:twolinkarm}
\emph{The hand of a two-link arm.} Let $\ell_1>\ell_2>0$, and use angles modulo $2\pi$ on the configuration torus. The hand-position map is
\[
E(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\ell_1e^{i\theta_1}+\ell_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\in\C=\R^2.
\]
(a) Find its critical points. (b) Prove that its critical values are the circles $|w|=\ell_1\pm\ell_2$. (c) Show that the open annulus between them has two preimages per point, each boundary circle has one, and all other points have none, as in Figure~\ref{fig:arm}.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:nosubmanifold}
\emph{A cone and a crossing.} (a) Show that $x^2+y^2=z^2$ is not a submanifold of $\R^3$ near zero, of any dimension. Use velocities of curves through the vertex. (b) Show that $\psi(t)=(\sin2t,\sin t)$ is an injective immersion on $0<t<2\pi$, but its image is not a submanifold at zero. Identify the missing hypothesis in Theorem~\ref{thm:fourdesc}(d).
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:stereographic}
\emph{Coordinates from a pole.} On $S^n$, $n\ge0$, let $N=(0,\ldots,0,1)$ and $S=-N$. For $x=(x',x_{n+1})$ set
\[
\sigma_N(x)=\frac{x'}{1-x_{n+1}},\qquad
\sigma_S(x)=\frac{x'}{1+x_{n+1}}
\]
on the respective punctured spheres. (a) Prove that $\sigma_N^{-1}(y)=(2y,|y|^2-1)/(1+|y|^2)$. (b) Compute $\sigma_S\sigma_N^{-1}(y)=y/|y|^2$ on the overlap. (c) Prove that these charts give the sphere's submanifold smooth structure, and explain the zero-dimensional case.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:minisard}
\emph{A smooth curve cannot fill a square.} If $f:U\subset\R^m\to\R^n$ is $C^1$ on an open set and $m<n$, prove that $f(U)$ is null. Include $m=0$. Compare this with the continuous space-filling map in Volume~7, Exercise~4.7.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rp2}
\emph{A projective plane in four dimensions.} For $v=(x,y,z)\in S^2$ define
\[
\tilde F(v)=(x^2-y^2,xy,xz,yz).
\]
(a) Prove that it induces a smooth $F:\R P^2\to\R^4$. (b) Recover $x^2+y^2$ and the matrix $vv^\top$ from its value, proving injectivity on projective points. (c) Prove that $F$ is an immersion and hence an embedding of a compact surface.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-defining-function}
\emph{The set can be smooth when the equation is not regular.} Put $g(x,y)=x^4+y^4-1$, and denote its zero set by $M=g^{-1}(0)$. (a) Prove that $M$ is a compact smooth curve diffeomorphic to $S^1$. (b) Compute its tangent at $(a,b)$ and an explicit local graph near $(1,0)$. (c) For $G=g^2$, find all critical values and describe each level type as its value varies. Explain why zero fails to be a regular value of $G$ even though $G^{-1}(0)=M$ is smooth.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-rank-matrix-chart}
\emph{Matrices of a fixed rank.} For $0\le r\le\min(m,n)$, let $\mathcal R_r\subset M_{m,n}(\R)$ consist of rank-$r$ matrices. (a) On a neighbourhood where an $r\times r$ minor $A$ is invertible, write $X=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and show that rank $r$ is equivalent to $D=CA^{-1}B$. Prove that $\mathcal R_r$ is a manifold of dimension $r(m+n-r)$. (b) Compute its tangent space at $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}I_r&0\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. (c) If $0<r<\min(m,n)$, show that the set of matrices of rank at most $r$ is not a submanifold near zero. Treat $r=0$ separately.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-transverse-intersections}
\emph{When two submanifolds cross cleanly.} Embedded submanifolds $M,N\subset\R^q$, of dimensions $m,n$, meet \emph{transversely} if $T_pM+T_pN=\R^q$ at each $p\in M\cap N$. (a) If the intersection is nonempty and transverse, prove it is an embedded submanifold of dimension $m+n-q$, with tangent $T_pM\cap T_pN$. Use local equation maps and verify their combined surjectivity. (b) Apply this to $S^2$ and the plane $z=c$. Explain the two tangent cases and why nontransversality does not by itself rule out a manifold intersection.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-proper-exhaustion}
\emph{A smooth function measuring escape.} A continuous map is \emph{proper} if inverse images of compact sets are compact. (a) Use a locally finite partition of unity with compact supports to build a smooth proper $f:M\to[1,\infty)$ on every nonempty smooth manifold. Try $f=\sum_jj\rho_j$. (b) Prove that a sequence leaves every compact subset of $M$ exactly when $f$ tends to infinity along it; deduce that $f$ is unbounded if $M$ is noncompact. (c) For compact $K\subset O$, $O$ open in $M$, construct a smooth $\chi:M\to[0,1]$ equal to one near $K$ and compactly supported in $O$. Use it to extend a smooth function on $O$ to all of $M$, agreeing near $K$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:cubeinverse}
\idea An invertible derivative guarantees a local differentiable inverse. A bijection can lack that derivative condition, and local inverses at every point can still belong to a map that repeats globally.
\writeup (a) The cube function is smooth, strictly increasing and has limits of both signs at infinity, so it is bijective. Its inverse is the real cube root. At zero its difference quotient is $y^{1/3}/y=y^{-2/3}\to\infty$, so it is not differentiable. The missing hypothesis is $f'(0)\ne0$. Indeed a differentiable inverse would give $f'(0)g'(0)=1$ by the chain rule, impossible.

(b) The Jacobian is
\[
Df=\begin{pmatrix}e^x\cos y&-e^x\sin y\\e^x\sin y&e^x\cos y\end{pmatrix},
\qquad\det Df=e^{2x}>0.
\]
Yet replacing $y$ by $y+2\pi$ leaves $f$ unchanged. In complex notation $f(x,y)=e^{x+iy}$. Near any source point, the inverse theorem gives a branch of the logarithm on a sufficiently small target neighbourhood avoiding zero. These different branches need not agree globally; the periodicity already prevents a global inverse for this map. The obstruction to a single logarithm branch on $\C^*$ was discussed in Volume~8, Chapter~14.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:matrixgroups}
\idea Differentiate each defining equation and produce a direction that changes it arbitrarily. At the identity the same derivative gives the tangent-space equation.
\writeup (a) For $\det X=1$,
\[
\left.\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\det((1+t)X)\right|_{t=0}=n\ne0.
\]
Thus the determinant derivative is a nonzero linear functional, hence surjective onto $\R$. The four descriptions give dimension $n^2-1$. The restriction $n\ge1$ matters: the empty determinant for $n=0$ is constant one, so the formula would incorrectly give dimension $-1$ for a point.

(b) Hermitian matrices have $n$ real diagonal entries and $n(n-1)/2$ arbitrary complex entries above the diagonal, a total real dimension $n^2$. For $F(X)=X^*X-I$, expansion gives $DF(X)H=H^*X+X^*H$. At a unitary $X$, every Hermitian $S$ is the image of $H=XS/2$. The zero set is therefore a submanifold of real dimension $2n^2-n^2=n^2$.

(c) The determinant of a unitary matrix has modulus one. Its ambient polynomial formula and the target-submanifold coordinate fact make $\det:\mathrm U(n)\to S^1$ smooth. The curve $e^{it}X$ is unitary and its determinant is $e^{int}\det X$, with nonzero tangent vector $in\det X$. Since the target tangent is one-dimensional, the differential is onto. The regular-value theorem makes $\mathrm{SU}(n)$ an embedded submanifold of dimension $n^2-1$. Combining its local straightening charts with those of $\mathrm U(n)$ gives the same embedded description in the full matrix space.

(d) Expanding the determinant by permutations gives $\det(I+tH)=1+t\operatorname{tr}H+O(t^2)$. Its derivative at $I$ is the trace, so
\[
T_I\mathrm{SL}_n(\R)=\{H:\operatorname{tr}H=0\},\qquad
T_I\mathrm U(n)=\{H:H^*+H=0\}.
\]
On the second space the trace is purely imaginary; its zero kernel is
$T_I\mathrm{SU}(n)=\{H:H^*+H=0,\ \operatorname{tr}H=0\}$. These spaces become $\mathfrak{sl}_n,\mathfrak u(n),\mathfrak{su}(n)$ in Chapter~7.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twolinkarm}
\idea Each derivative column is a hand velocity caused by moving one joint. When the links line up, those two velocities point along one line instead of spanning the plane.
\writeup (a) In local angle coordinates the derivative columns are $a=iE$ and $b=i\ell_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$. Two complex vectors are real-linearly dependent exactly when $\operatorname{Im}(a\bar b)=0$. Here
\[
a\bar b=\ell_2(\ell_1e^{-i\theta_2}+\ell_2),\qquad
\operatorname{Im}(a\bar b)=-\ell_1\ell_2\sin\theta_2.
\]
Thus the critical configurations have $\theta_2=0$ or $\pi$ modulo $2\pi$.

(b) At these configurations, $|E|=|\ell_1+\ell_2e^{i\theta_2}|$ is respectively $\ell_1+\ell_2$ or $\ell_1-\ell_2$. Varying $\theta_1$ sweeps every point on the corresponding circle.

(c) A preimage of $w$ must satisfy
\[
\cos\theta_2=\frac{|w|^2-\ell_1^2-\ell_2^2}{2\ell_1\ell_2}.
\]
There are two angle solutions in the open annulus, one on each boundary and none elsewhere. For each solution, $u=\ell_1+\ell_2e^{i\theta_2}$ is nonzero because $\ell_1>\ell_2$, and $|u|=|w|$. Thus $e^{i\theta_1}=w/u$ determines the other angle uniquely. The two interior solutions are the elbow-up and elbow-down configurations. At the boundary they merge, and the radial hand velocity is lost. The two critical-value circles are null sets, consistent with Sard.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:nosubmanifold}
\idea Velocities in a submanifold must lie in one linear tangent space. Enough independent velocities force full ambient dimension, which would make the set open.
\writeup (a) For each $\alpha$, the curve $t\mapsto(t\cos\alpha,t\sin\alpha,t)$ lies in the cone through zero. The velocities for $\alpha=0,\pi/2,\pi$ are independent and span $\R^3$. If the cone were a submanifold near zero, its tangent space would be three-dimensional. A full-dimensional submanifold is open there. But every neighbourhood of zero contains $(0,0,t)$, $t\ne0$, which is not on the cone. This is a contradiction.

(b) The derivative $(2\cos2t,\cos t)$ never vanishes: $\cos t=0$ makes $\cos2t=-1$. For injectivity, if $\sin s=\sin t$, then either $s=t$ or $s=\pi-t$ modulo $2\pi$. In the second case equality of $\sin2s,\sin2t$ forces $\sin2t=0$. The possibilities $t=\pi/2,3\pi/2$ give $s=t$ again; $t=\pi$ would require $s=0$ or $2\pi$, outside the domain. Thus there are no distinct equal-image parameters.

The image contains the smooth curve $(\sin2t,\sin t)$ for small positive and negative $t$: use the original parameters $t$ and $t+2\pi$, respectively, and use $\pi$ at $t=0$. Its velocity at zero is $(2,1)$. The original curve near parameter $\pi$ has velocity $(2,-1)$. These are independent, so a submanifold image would be open near zero. Yet any image point with second coordinate zero has parameter $\pi$ and first coordinate zero; all $(\varepsilon,0)$ with $\varepsilon\ne0$ are missing. Hence the image is not open. The failed hypothesis is homeomorphism onto the image: $\psi(1/j)\to\psi(\pi)$ while $1/j$ does not approach $\pi$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:stereographic}
\idea Solve the line-from-the-pole construction explicitly, then use its smooth formulas to compare atlases.
\writeup (a) For the proposed $x$, its squared norm is
\[
\frac{4|y|^2+(|y|^2-1)^2}{(1+|y|^2)^2}=1,
\]
and $1-x_{n+1}=2/(1+|y|^2)>0$, giving $\sigma_N(x)=y$. Conversely $y=\sigma_N(x)$ satisfies
$|y|^2=(1+x_{n+1})/(1-x_{n+1})$, which determines $x_{n+1}$ and then $x'=(1-x_{n+1})y$. The map and inverse are continuous by their formulas, giving a chart.

(b) On the overlap $y\ne0$, substitution yields $1+x_{n+1}=2|y|^2/(1+|y|^2)$ and hence $\sigma_S\sigma_N^{-1}(y)=y/|y|^2$. This smooth map is its own inverse.

(c) The chart map is the restriction of the ambient smooth expression $x'/(1-x_{n+1})$. Its inverse has ambient smooth components by (a), and takes values in $S^n$, so it is smooth into the sphere by the target-coordinate fact after Proposition~\ref{prop:subisman}. Thus it is a diffeomorphism for the sphere's submanifold structure, and its transitions with the straightening charts are smooth in both directions. The maximal atlas is the same. For $n=0$, each punctured sphere is one point mapping to $\R^0$; the overlap is empty, so no value of $y/|y|^2$ at zero is required.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:minisard}
\idea Add variables that the map ignores. Its lower-dimensional source then sits inside a null subset of an equal-dimensional source, where the null-set lemma applies.
\writeup Put $G(x,z)=f(x)$ on $U\times\R^{n-m}\subset\R^n$. It is $C^1$, and $U\times\{0\}$ lies in a hyperplane since $n-m>0$. That set is null, by Fubini or a direct box cover, so Lemma~\ref{lem:nullsets} makes $f(U)=G(U\times\{0\})$ null. This includes $m=0$: the source is empty or a point and the image has at most one point in positive-dimensional $\R^n$.

The continuous square-filling curve of Volume~7 cannot be $C^1$. Nor can it be Lipschitz: the same cube-cover argument shows that the image of a bounded interval under a Lipschitz map to the plane is null (subdivide into intervals of length $\delta$, giving order $\delta^{-1}$ image boxes of area order $\delta^2$). Continuity alone does not supply that linear distance bound.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rp2}
\idea Quadratic coordinates ignore the sign of $v$ but retain its line. Their derivative must be checked separately; recovering the line continuously does not itself prove immersion.
\writeup (a) Every component is even, so $\tilde F(-v)=\tilde F(v)$ and the quotient map induces a well-defined continuous $F$. On a projective chart choose the representative on the unit sphere with the chart's distinguished coordinate positive. For example on $z\ne0$ that section is
\[
(u,s)\longmapsto\frac{(u,s,1)}{\sqrt{1+u^2+s^2}}.
\]
It is smooth, and composing it with $\tilde F$ gives the coordinate expression of $F$. The other charts work identically, proving smoothness.

(b) Write $\tilde F(v)=(a,b,c,d)$. The identity $(x^2+y^2)^2=a^2+4b^2$ gives
\[
x^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+4b^2}+a}{2},\quad
y^2=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+4b^2}-a}{2},\quad z^2=1-x^2-y^2.
\]
Together with $xy=b,xz=c,yz=d$, these determine $vv^\top$, the orthogonal projection onto $\R v$. Its image determines $[v]$, proving injectivity. We use the square root only for uniqueness, not for differentiating an inverse at zero.

(c) Let $h\perp v$ and $D\tilde F(v)h=0$. Its component equations are
\[
2xh_1-2yh_2=0,\quad yh_1+xh_2=0,\quad zh_1+xh_3=0,\quad zh_2+yh_3=0.
\]
For $P=hv^\top+vh^\top$, the last three make its off-diagonal entries zero. Put $s=xh_1=yh_2$. Orthogonality gives $zh_3=-2s$, so $P=\operatorname{diag}(2s,2s,-4s)$. But $Pv=h|v|^2+v\langle h,v\rangle=h$. Hence $h=(2sx,2sy,-4sz)$ and $s=2sx^2=2sy^2$. If $s\ne0$, this forces $x^2=y^2=1/2$, so $z=0$, contradicting $zh_3=-2s$. Thus $s=0$, $P=0$ and $h=0$.

The sphere-to-projective quotient is locally a diffeomorphism by the same chart sections as in (a), so this injective tangent derivative proves that $F$ is an immersion. Its compact source makes it an embedding by Proposition~\ref{prop:embedding}. Its image is the required compact surface in $\R^4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-defining-function}
\idea A regular equation supplies a good local coordinate, but the same zero set can be described by an equation whose first derivative hides all that information.
\writeup (a) On $M$, $Dg=(4x^3,4y^3)$ cannot vanish, since that would force $x=y=0$ and $g=-1$. Thus $M$ is a one-dimensional submanifold. It is closed and bounded, hence compact. Define
\[
\Phi:S^1\to M,\qquad \Phi(u)=\frac{u}{(u_1^4+u_2^4)^{1/4}}.
\]
The denominator is positive and smooth near $S^1$; the target-coordinate fact makes $\Phi$ smooth into $M$. Its inverse is $p\mapsto p/|p|$, also smooth, and the compositions are identities by positive homogeneity. Hence $M$ is diffeomorphic to the circle.

(b) The tangent at $(a,b)\in M$ is $\{(u,v):a^3u+b^3v=0\}$. Near $(1,0)$ choose the positive root
\[
x=(1-y^4)^{1/4},\qquad |y|<1.
\]
It is smooth near zero, with derivative zero there, giving the vertical tangent line at $(1,0)$.

(c) Since $DG=2gDg$, the critical points are $M$ and the origin. Their values are zero and one. Negative levels are empty. For $0<c<1$, the equations $x^4+y^4=1\pm\sqrt c$ give two disjoint scaled copies of $M$. At $c=0$ there is just $M$, at $c=1$ there is the outer curve $x^4+y^4=2$ together with the isolated origin, and for $c>1$ only the outer curve remains. Every value outside $\{0,1\}$ is regular, including empty negative levels. At zero all derivatives of $G$ vanish along its fibre, so it is not a regular value. The regular-value theorem is a sufficient criterion for the equation being used; it does not say that a submanifold cannot admit a different, singular defining equation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-rank-matrix-chart}
\idea An invertible minor identifies the independent rows and columns. Every remaining entry is then forced by those rows and columns, leaving three freely variable blocks.
\writeup (a) When $A$ is invertible, block elimination gives
\[
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}
X
\begin{pmatrix}I&-A^{-1}B\\0&I\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&D-CA^{-1}B\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both multipliers are invertible, so the rank equals $r+\operatorname{rank}(D-CA^{-1}B)$. It is $r$ exactly when $D=CA^{-1}B$. Smooth inversion makes this a graph over the open set of $A,B,C$ with $\det A\ne0$. These are ambient block coordinates, so the four descriptions give an embedded submanifold of dimension
\[
r^2+r(n-r)+(m-r)r=r(m+n-r).
\]
Every rank-$r$ matrix has such a minor, after row and column permutations, which are invertible linear changes of ambient coordinates. For $r=0$ the graph consists only of the zero matrix, with dimension zero; this also covers zero matrix sizes. For maximal $r$, zero-sized blocks simply impose no extra equations.

(b) At $A=I_r$, $B=C=D=0$, the derivative of $CA^{-1}B$ is zero because every first-order term still contains $B$ or $C$. The tangent consists of all block matrices $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}H_{11}&H_{12}\\H_{21}&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, with no restriction on the first three blocks, as the dimension count predicts.

(c) If $r\ge1$, every line $tE_{ij}$ lies in the rank-at-most-$r$ set. Their velocities at zero span all $mn$ ambient directions. If this set were a submanifold near zero, its tangent would have full dimension and it would be open there. But matrices of rank $r+1$, formed by putting a small nonzero scalar on $r+1$ diagonal positions, lie arbitrarily near zero and outside the set. This contradiction proves singularity at zero for $0<r<\min(m,n)$. For $r=0$ the set is the single zero matrix and is a valid zero-dimensional submanifold.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-transverse-intersections}
\idea Equations for both submanifolds must remain independent when combined. Transversality says exactly that their normal directions have no common nonzero vector.
\writeup (a) At a point $p$ of the intersection, choose local equation maps $F$ for $M$ and $G$ for $N$, with surjective derivatives to $\R^{q-m}$ and $\R^{q-n}$. Shrink to a common ambient neighbourhood. Their row spaces are $(T_pM)^\perp$ and $(T_pN)^\perp$: each annihilates the relevant kernel and has its full codimension. Since
\[
(T_pM+T_pN)^\perp=(T_pM)^\perp\cap(T_pN)^\perp,
\]
transversality makes these row spaces intersect trivially. Their combined rows are therefore independent, so $D(F,G)$ is surjective at every intersection point. Its zero set is locally $M\cap N$. The regular-value theorem gives dimension
$q-((q-m)+(q-n))=m+n-q$, which is necessarily nonnegative at any transverse intersection point. Its tangent is the combined kernel $\ker DF\cap\ker DG=T_pM\cap T_pN$. The local statements provide the embedded-submanifold charts over the whole intersection.

(b) The equation normals for the sphere and plane are $2(x,y,z)$ and $(0,0,1)$. On their intersection these are independent exactly when $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$. For $|c|<1$ the intersection is the circle $x^2+y^2=1-c^2$, $z=c$, transverse and one-dimensional. At $c=\pm1$ the intersection is a single pole, the normals are parallel and transversality fails. That point is still a zero-dimensional submanifold, though the transverse dimension formula would have given one. For $|c|>1$ there is no intersection. Thus failure of this sufficient hypothesis does not settle whether the intersection is a manifold or what its dimension is.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-proper-exhaustion}
\idea A locally finite sum is smooth without convergence estimates near a point. Increasing weights force small function values to occur inside only finitely many compact supports.
\writeup (a) Apply the partition theorem to the cover $\{M\}$ to get $\rho_1,\rho_2,\ldots$; pad a finite family with zeros if necessary. Set $f=\sum_jj\rho_j$. Local finiteness makes it smooth and $f\ge\sum_j\rho_j=1$. For any real $R$, choose a positive integer $N>R$. Outside the first $N$ supports all active weights are at least $N+1$, so $f\ge N+1>R$. Therefore
\[
\{f\le R\}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^N\operatorname{supp}\rho_j.
\]
The sublevel is closed and the right side is compact, so the sublevel is compact. The inverse image of any compact subset of $[1,\infty)$ is a closed subset of a sufficiently large compact sublevel, proving properness.

(b) If $p_i$ leaves every compact set, it eventually lies outside each compact sublevel, so $f(p_i)\to\infty$. Conversely $f$ is bounded on any compact set, so divergence of its values eventually excludes that set. If $M$ were noncompact but $f$ bounded above, one sublevel would equal $M$, contradicting its compactness. Compact manifolds cause no problem: every continuous real-valued function on them is proper, and no sequence can leave the whole compact manifold.

(c) Choose at each point of $K$ a bump $\chi_i$ supported compactly in $O$ and equal to one on a smaller neighbourhood. Finitely many such smaller neighbourhoods cover $K$. Then
\[
\chi=1-\prod_i(1-\chi_i)
\]
is smooth, lies in $[0,1]$, equals one near $K$, and is supported in the finite compact union of those supports inside $O$. If $K$ is empty, take $\chi=0$. For smooth $a:O\to\R$, define $\chi a$ on $O$ and zero elsewhere. The compact support inside $O$ makes the extension smooth across its boundary, and it agrees with $a$ near $K$.
\end{solution}
