\chapter{Quadratic forms and quadrics}\label{geometry:ch:quadrics}

\begin{center}\itshape
A demonstration of the theorem that every homogeneous quadratic polynomial is reducible by real orthogonal substitutions\dots
\upshape(J.~J.~Sylvester, title of a paper in the \emph{Philosophical Magazine}, 1852)
\end{center}

The saddle $z=xy$ has curved cross-sections, yet it contains straight lines.
Fix $x=2$, and its equation becomes the line $z=2y$. Fix $y=3$ instead,
and it becomes $z=3x$. Two families of straight lines cover the same
curved surface. An equation of degree two can describe more than a sphere.

Vladimir Shukhov used another such surface in his water tower at the 1896
Nizhny Novgorod exhibition. Its hourglass-shaped frame was made of straight
bars arranged in two crossing families. The surface is a hyperboloid of
one sheet. His later Shabolovka radio tower in Moscow, completed in 1922,
stacks six hyperboloid sections to a height of 150 metres.
The geometry lets straight members form a curved frame.

\photograph{shukhov-tower}{Shukhov's first hyperboloid water tower at the 1896 Nizhny Novgorod exhibition, with straight crossing bars forming its curved frame.}{Shukhov's first hyperboloid tower, Nizhny Novgorod, 1896. Photograph attributed to Andrei Karelin; published in the 1897 exhibition album. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.}

A symmetric matrix will tell us which quadric we have. Over the real
numbers, we can change coordinates until its quadratic form is a sum of
positive and negative squares. Sylvester's law says that the numbers of
these squares cannot change. Over other fields there may be no notion of
positive or negative. There we use vectors on which the form vanishes,
and Witt's cancellation theorem lets us separate the resulting pieces.

This appendix uses linear algebra, the spectral theorem and Gram volumes
from Volume~1, and finite fields from Volume~3. The final point counts
show how the choice of field changes the geometry: one finite quadric
contains many lines, while another contains none.

\begin{think}
On $z=xy$, find the two lines through $(1,1,1)$ by fixing one coordinate.
Could a third line through that point also stay on the saddle? Substituting
a parametrised line turns the question into a polynomial identity.
\end{think}

\section{Symmetric bilinear forms}

The expression $x^2-y^2$ records both lengths of vectors and their
interaction. Its associated expression in two vectors is
$x_1x_2-y_1y_2$. Keeping that interaction makes coordinate changes easier
to follow.

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over a field $K$ of
characteristic different from $2$. A symmetric bilinear form is a function
$\beta:V\times V\to K$, linear in each argument and satisfying
$\beta(u,v)=\beta(v,u)$. Its quadratic form is $q(v)=\beta(v,v)$.
The identity
\[
 2\beta(u,v)=q(u+v)-q(u)-q(v)
\]
recovers $\beta$ from $q$. The characteristic assumption permits this
division by $2$. It remains in force except for the Segre construction
in Section~\ref{geometry:sec:ruled}, which works over every field.

In a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, write $A=(\beta(e_i,e_j))$.
This symmetric matrix is the Gram matrix of the form, and $q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax$.
If the new basis has columns $P$ in the old coordinates, substitution
$x=Py$ changes the matrix to $P^{\mathsf T}AP$. Two forms are
\emph{isometric}, or equivalent, when a linear isomorphism takes one to
the other in this way. This is a change of basis, so $P$ must be invertible.

The rank of $A$ is unchanged. Its kernel is the \emph{radical}
$V^\perp=\{v:\beta(v,w)=0\text{ for every }w\in V\}$.
The form is \emph{nondegenerate} if this radical is zero, equivalently
if $\det A\ne0$. These definitions include the zero-dimensional space:
its empty matrix has determinant $1$ and its radical is zero.

For a subspace $W$, put
$W^\perp=\{v:\beta(v,w)=0\text{ for all }w\in W\}$.
If the restriction to $W$ is nondegenerate, then
$V=W\oplus W^\perp$. To see the dimension assertion, map
$v\in V$ to the functional $\beta(v,\cdot)|_W$ on $W$.
Its restriction to $W$ is an isomorphism onto $W^*$, so the full map
is onto and its kernel $W^\perp$ has dimension $n-\dim W$.
Also $W\cap W^\perp=0$ by nondegeneracy on $W$.
The notation $W\perp U$ for such a direct sum says that its two summands
are orthogonal: $\beta(W,U)=0$.

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal bases]\label{geometry:prop:orthbasis}
Every quadratic form on a finite-dimensional space has a basis of
pairwise orthogonal vectors. In that basis it has the expression
$q(x)=a_1x_1^2+\cdots+a_nx_n^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Induct on the dimension. In dimension zero the empty basis works.
If $q$ is identically zero, polarisation gives $\beta=0$, so every basis
works. For a nonzero form, take $e_1$ such that $q(e_1)\ne0$.
The restriction to $Ke_1$ is nondegenerate, so the preceding decomposition
gives $V=Ke_1\oplus e_1^\perp$.
By induction, $e_1^\perp$ has an orthogonal basis. Adjoining $e_1$
produces an orthogonal basis of $V$.
\end{proof}

Write $\langle a_1,\ldots,a_n\rangle$ for the resulting diagonal form.
Replacing $e_i$ by $\lambda e_i$ multiplies $a_i$ by $\lambda^2$.
Over $\C$, every nonzero coefficient can therefore be changed to $1$,
so rank classifies forms of a fixed dimension.
Over $\R$, a nonzero coefficient can be changed to $1$ or $-1$.
For example, $4x^2-9y^2$ becomes $X^2-Y^2$ with $X=2x$, $Y=3y$.
Can a more complicated coordinate change alter the number of minus signs?

\section{Sylvester's law of inertia}

A real form is positive definite on a subspace if it is positive on each
nonzero vector of that subspace. This gives a coordinate-free way to count
the positive squares.

\begin{theorem}[Sylvester's law of inertia]\label{geometry:thm:sylvester}
Suppose a real quadratic form has an orthogonal basis in which
\[
 q(x)=x_1^2+\cdots+x_p^2-x_{p+1}^2-\cdots-x_{p+m}^2,
 \qquad p+m\le n.
\]
Then $p$ is the largest dimension of a subspace on which $q$ is positive
definite, and $m$ is the largest dimension of a subspace on which it is
negative definite. In particular, both integers are independent of the basis.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The span of $e_1,\ldots,e_p$ is positive definite, so the largest dimension
is at least $p$. On $N=\operatorname{span}(e_{p+1},\ldots,e_n)$ the
form is nonpositive, including any zero directions.
A positive-definite subspace $W$ can meet $N$ only in $0$.
Thus $\dim W+\dim N\le n$, giving $\dim W\le p$.
Apply this argument to $-q$ to obtain the assertion about $m$.
It also covers $p=0$ or $m=0$: the zero subspace is allowed.
\end{proof}

The pair $(p,m)$ is the \emph{signature}. Its sum is the rank; the remaining
$n-p-m$ coordinates span the radical. For forms on spaces of the same
dimension, equality of signatures is therefore equivalent to isometry.
The spectral theorem (Volume~1, Theorem~7.9) gives another way to read
these numbers: $p$ counts positive eigenvalues of $A$ and $m$ counts
negative eigenvalues, each with multiplicity.

A projective quadric is the zero locus of a nonzero homogeneous quadratic
form. Multiplying that form by a nonzero scalar does not change its zero
locus. In particular, changing its sign swaps $p$ and $m$ without changing
the geometry.

\begin{corollary}\label{geometry:cor:realconics}
A nondegenerate real projective conic is either empty or projectively
equivalent to $x^2+y^2=z^2$.
A nondegenerate real projective quadric surface in $\mathbb P^3(\R)$ is
either empty or projectively equivalent to one of
$x^2+y^2+z^2=w^2$ and $x^2+y^2=z^2+w^2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Nondegeneracy excludes zero diagonal coefficients. After changing the
sign, assume $p\ge m$. In three variables the possibilities are
$(3,0)$ and $(2,1)$. The first has no nonzero real zero; the second gives
the stated conic. In four variables the possibilities are $(4,0)$,
$(3,1)$ and $(2,2)$, giving respectively the empty locus and the two
stated surfaces.
\end{proof}

The two nonempty surfaces differ even projectively. For signature $(3,1)$,
a projective line on the quadric would come from a two-dimensional vector
subspace on which $q$ vanishes. Such a subspace must meet the
three-dimensional positive-definite subspace nontrivially, which is
impossible. The surface of signature $(2,2)$ does have lines, as
Section~\ref{geometry:sec:ruled} will show.

\section{Quadric surfaces in space}

An affine quadric in $\R^3$ has equation
\[
 x^{\mathsf T}Mx+2b^{\mathsf T}x+c=0,
 \qquad \widehat A=\begin{pmatrix}M&b\\b^{\mathsf T}&c\end{pmatrix},
\]
where $M$ is a nonzero symmetric $3\times3$ matrix.
The matrix $\widehat A$ represents its homogenised form in four variables.
Call the affine quadric nondegenerate when $\widehat A$ is invertible.
This does not require $M$ itself to be invertible: a paraboloid is an
example where it is not.

\begin{theorem}\label{geometry:thm:quadrics}
Every nonempty nondegenerate real affine quadric surface can be put by an
affine change of coordinates into exactly one of the following forms:
\[
\begin{gathered}
 x^2+y^2+z^2=1,\qquad x^2+y^2-z^2=1,\qquad x^2-y^2-z^2=1,\\
 z=x^2+y^2,\qquad z=x^2-y^2.
\end{gathered}
\]
They are the ellipsoid, the one-sheeted and two-sheeted hyperboloids,
and the elliptic and hyperbolic paraboloids, respectively.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{An invertible quadratic part.}
Substitute $x=u-M^{-1}b$. Completing the square gives
$u^{\mathsf T}Mu=c'$, where $c'=b^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}b-c$.
Block elimination gives
\[
 \det\widehat A=\det M\,(c-b^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}b),
\]
so $c'\ne0$. Divide by $c'$ and apply Sylvester's law to $M/c'$.
After rescaling coordinates we obtain $\pm x^2\pm y^2\pm z^2=1$.
Three, two or one positive signs give the first three surfaces.
No positive sign would give an empty locus and is excluded.

\emph{A singular quadratic part.}
Appending one row and one column can raise rank by at most two.
Since $\widehat A$ has rank four, $M$ has rank at least two, and hence
exactly two in this case. Diagonalise it as
$\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,0)$, $d_1d_2\ne0$.
Complete squares in $x,y$ to obtain
$d_1x^2+d_2y^2+2b_3z+c''=0$.
Here $b_3\ne0$, since otherwise $\widehat A$ would have a zero third
row and column. Set $Z=-2b_3z-c''$, so the equation is $Z=d_1x^2+d_2y^2$.
Rename $Z$ as $z$. Rescale $x,y$ and, if necessary, reverse the sign
of $z$. This gives the last two forms.

\emph{Distinguish the forms.}
For a quadratic equation, affine changes act on $M$ by congruence;
multiplying the equation by a nonzero scalar can also reverse both signs.
The rank and unordered signature of $M$ distinguish the ellipsoid,
the pair of hyperboloids, and each paraboloid type.
The remaining two hyperboloids differ by connectedness.
For the one-sheeted form, the parametrisation
$(\sqrt{1+z^2}\cos\theta,\sqrt{1+z^2}\sin\theta,z)$ shows it is
connected. For the two-sheeted form, $x=\pm\sqrt{1+y^2+z^2}$ gives
exactly two connected components. An affine bijection cannot change that.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
There are also degenerate forms: cones, cylinders, pairs of planes, lines
and points. Including the empty real loci gives the classical list of
seventeen affine quadric types; Berger's \emph{Geometry II}, Chapter~15,
describes that extended classification. The theorem above concerns only
the nonempty nondegenerate surfaces.
\end{remark}

Projective coordinates join some of these affine shapes. The ellipsoid,
two-sheeted hyperboloid and elliptic paraboloid all have projective
signature $(3,1)$; they meet the plane at infinity in no point, a conic,
or one tangent point, respectively. The one-sheeted hyperboloid and
hyperbolic paraboloid both have signature $(2,2)$. Their intersections
with infinity are a conic and a pair of lines.

\section{Ruled quadrics}\label{geometry:sec:ruled}

To see the lines, change coordinates to
$x_0=x-z$, $x_3=x+z$, $x_1=w-y$, $x_2=w+y$.
Then $x_0x_3-x_1x_2=x^2+y^2-z^2-w^2$.
We can therefore study the split projective quadric as
\[
 Q=\{[x_0:x_1:x_2:x_3]:x_0x_3=x_1x_2\}.
\]
Place the coordinates in a matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_0&x_1\\x_2&x_3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
The equation says that its determinant vanishes. Since a projective point
is nonzero, these are exactly the rank-one matrices, up to a scalar.

\begin{theorem}[The Segre quadric]\label{geometry:thm:segre}
Over any field $K$, the map
\[
 \sigma:\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1\longrightarrow Q,\qquad
 \sigma([s:t],[u:v])=[su:sv:tu:tv]
\]
is a bijection. Fixing either factor and varying the other gives a line.
Within either of these two families, distinct lines are disjoint.
Lines from different families meet in exactly one point.
Every line contained in $Q$ belongs to one of these families.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The displayed matrix factors as the column $\binom st$ times the row
$(u\ v)$. Conversely, every rank-one matrix has a one-dimensional
column space and a one-dimensional row space, so it has this factorisation.
The two projective factors are uniquely determined by these spaces.
This proves well-definedness and bijectivity.

For a fixed nonzero column, varying the row gives a two-dimensional
vector subspace of matrices and hence a projective line. The same holds
with row and column interchanged. Bijectivity shows that two lines with
different fixed columns are disjoint, as are two with different fixed
rows. A fixed column and fixed row determine exactly one point, proving
the cross-family intersection assertion.

Finally let a line in $Q$ be spanned by independent rank-one matrices
$c_1r_1,c_2r_2$. If both columns and both rows were independent, choose
vectors $v_1,v_2$ dual to the rows, so $r_i(v_j)=\delta_{ij}$.
Then $(c_1r_1+c_2r_2)v_j=c_j$, giving rank two, a contradiction.
Thus the columns are proportional or the rows are proportional.
In either case their span is one of the stated two-dimensional subspaces,
so the line belongs to the corresponding family.
\end{proof}

The bars on the one-sheeted hyperboloid can be written down directly.
At $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)$ the two directions are
$(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,1)$ and $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,-1)$.
Along either line,
\[
 (\cos\theta-s\sin\theta)^2+(\sin\theta+s\cos\theta)^2-s^2=1.
\]
Thus the line stays on the surface for every real $s$.
The saddle makes the same structure visible with less algebra: its lines
$x=c,z=cy$ and $y=c,z=cx$ form the two families.

\begin{beauty}
Three pairwise skew projective lines force a unique quadric, as
Exercise~\ref{geometry:ex:skewlines} proves. The lines meeting all three
make up its other ruling. Three suitably chosen bars therefore determine
the projective surface of a Shukhov frame.
\end{beauty}

\section{Isotropic vectors and Witt's theorem}

We again assume that the field has characteristic different from $2$.

Over $\R$, $x^2+y^2$ vanishes only at $0$. Over $\C$, it vanishes at
$(1,i)$. A nonzero vector $v$ with $q(v)=0$ is called \emph{isotropic}.
A form with no such vector is \emph{anisotropic}. This distinction makes
sense without ordering the field.

The hyperbolic plane $\mathbb H$ is the form $2xy$ on $K^2$.
Its matrix is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Putting $x=X-Y$ and $y=(X+Y)/2$ gives $2xy=X^2-Y^2$, so
$\mathbb H\cong\langle1,-1\rangle$. Its two coordinate axes consist
of isotropic vectors. One such vector in a nondegenerate space forces
an entire hyperbolic plane.

\begin{lemma}\label{geometry:lem:hyperbolic}
If a nondegenerate form on $V$ has an isotropic vector $e$, then $e$
belongs to a hyperbolic plane $H\subset V$. Moreover
$V=H\oplus H^\perp$, and the form on $H^\perp$ is nondegenerate.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $e\ne0$ is not in the radical, choose $f'$ with $\beta(e,f')=1$.
Put $f=f'-q(f')e/2$. Because $q(e)=0$, this replacement keeps $\beta(e,f)=1$ and gives
\[
 q(f)=q(f')-q(f')\beta(f',e)+q(f')^2q(e)/4=0.
\]
Thus $e,f$ are independent and have Gram matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Their span $H$ is a nondegenerate hyperbolic plane, giving the direct sum.
A vector in the radical of $H^\perp$ is orthogonal to $H^\perp$ and
to $H$, hence to all of $V$. Nondegeneracy on $V$ makes it zero.
\end{proof}

We need an isometry that moves vectors of the same nonzero value onto
each other. For $q(w)\ne0$, reflection in $w^\perp$ is
\[
 \tau_w(x)=x-\frac{2\beta(x,w)}{q(w)}w.
\]
It fixes $w^\perp$ and sends $w$ to $-w$.
Expanding $q(\tau_wx)$ cancels the two terms involving
$\beta(x,w)^2$, giving $q(\tau_wx)=q(x)$.
Also $\tau_w^2=\mathrm{id}$, so it is an invertible isometry.
These statements do not require the whole form to be nondegenerate.

\begin{lemma}\label{geometry:lem:transitive}
If $q(u)=q(v)\ne0$, an isometry that is a product of at most two
reflections takes $u$ to $v$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The identity
$q(u-v)+q(u+v)=4q(u)\ne0$ shows that at least one reflection direction
is available. If $q(u-v)\ne0$, then
$q(u-v)=2\beta(u,u-v)$, so $\tau_{u-v}(u)=v$.
Otherwise $q(u+v)\ne0$, and the same calculation gives
$\tau_{u+v}(u)=-v$. Following it by $\tau_v$ gives $v$.
This includes $u=v$, when the identity also works as an empty product.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Witt cancellation]\label{geometry:thm:witt}
Let $q,q_1,q_2$ be quadratic forms, with $q$ nondegenerate.
An isometry $q\perp q_1\cong q\perp q_2$ implies $q_1\cong q_2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Diagonalise $q$ with nonzero coefficients. If its dimension is zero,
the conclusion is already the hypothesis. Otherwise it is enough to
cancel one nondegenerate line at a time.
Transport the given isometry to a common space with decompositions
$V=Ku\perp W_1=Kv\perp W_2$, where $q(u)=q(v)=a\ne0$.
Here the notation $q(u)$ denotes the common ambient form.
The complements are exactly $u^\perp$ and $v^\perp$, even if their
restrictions are degenerate. The preceding lemma gives an isometry
$\sigma$ with $\sigma(u)=v$. It carries $u^\perp$ onto $v^\perp$,
so its restriction identifies $W_1$ with $W_2$. Iterating proves cancellation.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Witt decomposition]\label{geometry:cor:wittindex}
Every nondegenerate form is isometric to $m\mathbb H\perp q_0$, where
$q_0$ is anisotropic. The integer $m$, called the Witt index, and the
isometry class of $q_0$ are unique.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If an isotropic vector exists, split off a hyperbolic plane using
Lemma~\ref{geometry:lem:hyperbolic}. Repeat on its nondegenerate complement.
The dimension drops by two at each step, so the process ends with an
anisotropic space, possibly the zero-dimensional space.
For uniqueness, suppose
$m\mathbb H\perp q_0\cong m'\mathbb H\perp q'_0$ and $m\le m'$.
Cancel $m\mathbb H$. If $m'>m$, the remaining right side contains an
isotropic vector while $q_0$ does not, a contradiction.
Thus $m=m'$ and cancellation also gives $q_0\cong q'_0$.
\end{proof}

For real signature $(p,m)$, pairing a positive and a negative square gives
$\min(p,m)$ hyperbolic planes, leaving a definite form.
Over $\Q$ the anisotropic pieces require arithmetic information.
The Hasse--Minkowski theorem, quoted here, says that a rational quadratic
form is isotropic exactly when it is isotropic over $\R$ and over each
$p$-adic field $\Q_p$. We do not use that theorem in this appendix.
Volume~13 develops the local arithmetic and returns to conics.

\section{Quadrics over finite fields}

For a finite field $\mathbb F_q$ of odd order, an anisotropic nondegenerate
form can have at most two variables. A count of squares proves this
without classifying the field's forms first.

\begin{proposition}\label{geometry:prop:chevalley}
Every nondegenerate quadratic form in at least three variables over
$\mathbb F_q$, for $q$ odd, is isotropic.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Diagonalise and retain three nonzero diagonal terms, $ax^2+by^2+cz^2$.
The set of squares in $\mathbb F_q$ has $(q+1)/2$ elements: zero,
and $(q-1)/2$ nonzero values since each has the two distinct roots $x,-x$.
Both sets
$\{ax^2:x\in\mathbb F_q\}$ and
$\{-c-by^2:y\in\mathbb F_q\}$ therefore have $(q+1)/2$ elements.
Their total size is $q+1$, so they overlap. At an overlap,
$ax^2+by^2+c=0$. The vector $(x,y,1)$ is nonzero and isotropic.
Extend it by zero coordinates for the original form.
\end{proof}

A nondegenerate four-dimensional form consequently splits as
$\mathbb H\perp N$, where $N$ is a nondegenerate plane.
If $N$ is isotropic it is another hyperbolic plane. This gives the
\emph{hyperbolic quadric}, of Witt index two. Otherwise $N$ is anisotropic,
giving the \emph{elliptic quadric}, of Witt index one.

\begin{theorem}\label{geometry:thm:countquadric}
Over $\mathbb F_q$ with $q$ odd, a nondegenerate hyperbolic quadric in
$\mathbb P^3$ has $(q+1)^2$ points. A nondegenerate elliptic quadric has
$q^2+1$ points.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Rescale one coordinate of the hyperbolic plane and write the form
$xy+N(z,w)$. For a fixed value of $N$, the equation $xy=-N$ has
$2q-1$ solutions if $N=0$ and $q-1$ otherwise.

If $N$ is anisotropic, only $(z,w)=(0,0)$ gives zero, so there are
$(2q-1)+(q^2-1)(q-1)$ zero vectors of the four-dimensional form,
including the zero vector. Remove that vector and divide by $q-1$,
the number of nonzero scalar representatives of each projective point.
The count is $2+(q^2-1)=q^2+1$.

If $N=zw$, it vanishes at $2q-1$ pairs and is nonzero at $(q-1)^2$
pairs. The vector count is therefore
$(2q-1)^2+(q-1)^2(q-1)$. Removing zero and dividing gives
\[
 \frac{(2q-1)^2-1}{q-1}+(q-1)^2
 =4q+(q-1)^2=(q+1)^2.
\]
\end{proof}

The hyperbolic answer also follows from its two projective-line
coordinates: $\#\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_q)=q+1$.
It has $2(q+1)$ lines, the two rulings. The elliptic quadric has no line,
as Exercise~\ref{geometry:ex:linesFq} proves. Its points form an
\emph{ovoid}: no three are collinear, since a line meeting a quadric in
three points would lie entirely on it.

nonzero vectors have value zero? Over $\R$ signs answer much of it;
over a finite field a pigeonhole count supplies isotropic vectors.
Appendix~F goes beyond degree two, where a matrix no longer classifies
the whole curve, but a determinant can still locate intersections.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:signxyz}
Determine the signature of $xy+yz+zx$ and exhibit an orthogonal basis.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:hessian}
Let $f:\R^n\to\R$ be $C^2$ and let $a$ be a critical point with Hessian
$H$. Prove that positive definiteness of $H$ makes $a$ a strict local
minimum. If $H$ has at least one positive and one negative eigenvalue,
prove that $a$ is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:sylvestercrit}
Prove Sylvester's criterion: a real symmetric matrix is positive definite
if and only if each leading principal minor is positive.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:binarydisc}
Classify $ax^2+2bxy+cy^2$ as definite, indefinite or degenerate according
to whether $b^2<ac$, $b^2>ac$ or $b^2=ac$.
Relate the classification to the roots of $at^2+2bt+c$.
Include the cases $a=0$ and the zero form, explaining any root at infinity.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:gram}
For $v_1,\ldots,v_k\in\R^n$, let $G=(v_i\cdot v_j)$.
Show that $G$ is positive semidefinite and has the rank of this family.
Prove that $\det G$ is the square of its parallelotope's $k$-volume,
taken to be zero for a dependent family, including $k>n$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:lorentz}
For $q(t,x,y,z)=t^2-x^2-y^2-z^2$, suppose $q(u)>0$.
Show that $\beta(u,v)^2\ge q(u)q(v)$ for every $v$,
and determine exactly when equality holds.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:conicsclass}
List the real projective conics defined by nonzero quadratic forms in
three variables, up to projective equivalence. Include degenerate forms.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:saddlelines}
Find every straight line on $z=xy$ through $(a,b,ab)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:wittFp}
Find the Witt index of $x^2+y^2+z^2$ over $\R$, over $\Q$ and over
$\mathbb F_p$ for an odd prime $p$. Give an isotropic vector for
$p=3,5,7$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:conicFq}
Prove that a nondegenerate conic over $\mathbb F_q$, $q$ odd, has $q+1$
points.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:linesFq}
Count the lines on the hyperbolic quadric over $\mathbb F_q$, $q$ odd,
and show that an elliptic quadric contains no line.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:skewlines}
Let $K$ be infinite, and let $L_1,L_2,L_3$ be pairwise skew lines in
$\mathbb P^3(K)$. Prove that a unique quadric surface contains all three.
Show that it is nondegenerate and that the lines meeting all three are
exactly the lines of its other ruling.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:v9-schur-complement}
\emph{Eliminating a block.} Let $A$ be an invertible real symmetric matrix,
$B$ a rectangular matrix and $C$ symmetric. Prove that the form with matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\B^{\mathsf T}&C\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
is equivalent to the orthogonal sum with matrices $A$ and
$C-B^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}B$. Deduce an exact criterion for the full matrix
to be positive definite. Apply it to
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0&1\\0&3&1\\1&1&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:v9-maximal-isotropic}
\emph{How large can a zero subspace be?} Let a real form have signature
$(p,m)$ and radical dimension $r$. Prove that the largest dimension of
a subspace on which the form vanishes identically is $r+\min(p,m)$.
Construct a subspace attaining it. Explain why a nondegenerate form of
signature $(3,1)$ has no projective line of zeros.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:v9-finite-tangent-plane}
\emph{A tangent plane counts differently.} For the hyperbolic and elliptic
quadrics over $\mathbb F_q$, $q$ odd, show that a tangent plane meets the
quadric in respectively $2q+1$ points and one point. At a point represented
by an isotropic vector $e$, use the plane $\mathbb P(e^\perp)$ and identify
the form on $e^\perp/Ke$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:v9-rulings-under-map}
\emph{An isometry that swaps the rulings.} Over any field $K$, represent the split quadric by
rank-one $2\times2$ matrices $X$. Prove that $X\mapsto AXB$, for
$A,B\in\mathrm{GL}_2(K)$, preserves both rulings, while $X\mapsto X^{\mathsf T}$
interchanges them. Describe the induced maps on the two projective-line
coordinates. Which of these maps fix every point of the quadric?
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:signxyz}
\idea Completing squares gives both the signs and the basis vectors.
\writeup With $x=u+v$, $y=u-v$, the form becomes
$u^2-v^2+2uz=(u+z)^2-v^2-z^2$.
Thus its signature is $(1,2)$, and the new coordinate directions give
$e_1=(1,1,0)$, $e_2=(1,-1,0)$, $e_3=(-1,-1,1)$.
Their form values are $1,-1,-1$; the diagonal expression shows that all
mixed bilinear products vanish. The matrix is $(J-I)/2$, whose eigenvalues
$1,-1/2,-1/2$ give the same signature by the spectral theorem
(Volume~1, Theorem~7.9).
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:hessian}
\idea A quadratic term of fixed sign dominates a smaller-order remainder.
\writeup Taylor's formula at the critical point gives
$f(a+h)-f(a)=h^{\mathsf T}Hh/2+o(|h|^2)$.
If the least eigenvalue is $\lambda>0$, choose the neighbourhood so that
the remainder has absolute value at most $\lambda|h|^2/4$.
Then the difference is at least $\lambda|h|^2/4>0$ for $h\ne0$.
If there are eigenvectors $v_+,v_-$ of positive and negative eigenvalues,
substitute $h=tv_+$ and $h=tv_-$. For all sufficiently small nonzero $t$,
the two differences have opposite signs. Neither local extremum is possible.
In dimension zero the minimum assertion is vacuous and the mixed-sign
case cannot arise. A semidefinite Hessian alone does not settle the issue:
$x^2+y^4$ and $x^2-y^4$ have the same Hessian at $0$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:sylvestercrit}
\idea Restriction proves necessity; inertia and the determinant prove sufficiency.
\writeup If $A$ is positive definite, each leading block represents its
restriction to a coordinate subspace, hence has positive eigenvalues and
positive determinant. Conversely, induct on the matrix size.
The empty matrix gives the vacuous base case, and size one is immediate.
Assume the leading block of size $n-1$ is positive definite by induction.
Sylvester's law gives at least $n-1$ positive eigenvalues for $A$.
The last leading minor is $\det A>0$, so no eigenvalue is zero and the
number of negative eigenvalues is even. It is at most one, hence zero.
Thus all eigenvalues are positive and $A$ is positive definite.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:binarydisc}
\idea The determinant decides whether the two real eigenvalues have opposite signs.
\writeup Their product is $ac-b^2$. A positive product gives two
nonzero eigenvalues of the same sign, hence definiteness. A negative
product gives signature $(1,1)$. A zero product gives degeneracy.
For the zero form every direction is a zero; a nonzero degenerate form
has rank one and one projective zero direction, counted twice.
An indefinite form has two distinct projective zero directions, while
a definite form has none.

In the chart $y\ne0$, put $t=x/y$ to get $at^2+2bt+c=0$.
If $a\ne0$, the discriminant $4(b^2-ac)$ gives the usual two, zero,
or double real roots. If $a=0,b\ne0$, there is one finite root
$-c/(2b)$ and the other projective root is $[1:0]$ at infinity.
If $a=b=0,c\ne0$, the only projective root is the double root at
infinity, although the constant polynomial has no finite root.
For $a=b=c=0$, every finite parameter and the point at infinity are zeros.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:gram}
\idea The kernel of the Gram matrix records exactly the linear relations.
\writeup Let $M$ have columns $v_i$. Then
$x^{\mathsf T}Gx=|Mx|^2\ge0$, and $\ker G=\ker M$:
$Gx=0$ implies $|Mx|^2=0$, while $Mx=0$ plainly implies $Gx=0$.
Thus the ranks agree. If the family is dependent, both $\det G$ and
its $k$-volume are zero. This includes $k>n$.
If it is independent, choose an orthonormal basis of its $k$-dimensional
span. The coordinate matrix $M'$ is square, has the same Gram matrix,
and gives volume $|\det M'|$ (Volume~1, Theorem~7.10).
Hence $\det G=(\det M')^2$. For $k=0$, the empty determinant and
zero-dimensional volume are both $1$, consistent with the formula.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:lorentz}
\idea The complement of a positive vector has only negative directions.
\writeup The form has signature $(1,3)$. The orthogonal decomposition
$V=\R u\perp u^\perp$ and Sylvester's law give signature $(0,3)$
on $u^\perp$. Decompose $v=\lambda u+w$, where $w\in u^\perp$.
Orthogonality gives $\beta(u,v)=\lambda q(u)$ together with
$q(v)=\lambda^2q(u)+q(w)$. Thus
\[
 \beta(u,v)^2-q(u)q(v)=-q(u)q(w)\ge0.
\]
Since $q(u)>0$ and the complement is negative definite, equality holds
exactly when $w=0$, that is, exactly when $v$ is a multiple of $u$.
This includes $v=0$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:conicsclass}
\idea A nonzero scalar can reverse all signs, so identify opposite signatures.
\writeup In dimension three the nonzero possibilities, up to swapping
the signs, are $(3,0)$, $(2,1)$, $(2,0)$, $(1,1)$ and $(1,0)$.
They give respectively the empty nondegenerate conic
$x^2+y^2+z^2=0$, the circle $x^2+y^2=z^2$, the single point
$x^2+y^2=0$, the two lines $x^2-y^2=0$, and the double line $x^2=0$.
The point is $[0:0:1]$; the two lines are distinct.
The zero form is excluded by the definition of a projective conic:
its zero locus would be the whole plane, not another conic type.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:saddlelines}
\idea An entire line satisfies the surface equation only if every polynomial
coefficient vanishes.
\writeup Substitute $(a+su,b+sv,ab+sw)$, where $(u,v,w)\ne0$.
Comparing powers of $s$ gives $w=av+bu$ and $uv=0$.
If $u=0$, nonzero direction forces $v\ne0$ and the line is
$x=a,z=ay$, with direction $(0,1,a)$.
If $v=0$, it is $y=b,z=bx$, with direction $(1,0,b)$.
Both $u=v=0$ would also force $w=0$, so there is no third case.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:wittFp}
\idea In dimension three, finding one isotropic vector determines the index.
\writeup Two hyperbolic planes would require dimension four, so the
index is zero or one. Over $\R$ and $\Q$, a sum of squares vanishes
only at zero, giving index zero. Over $\mathbb F_p$, the form is
nondegenerate for odd $p$ and Proposition~\ref{geometry:prop:chevalley}
makes it isotropic, giving index one.
For $p=3,5,7$, examples are $(1,1,1)$, $(1,2,0)$ and $(1,2,3)$,
whose squared norms are $3,5,14$, respectively, zero modulo the
corresponding prime. Each vector is nonzero.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:conicFq}
\idea An isotropic vector supplies the point needed to parametrise a conic.
\writeup The conic's ternary form is nondegenerate, so the finite-field
proposition gives a nonzero zero vector and hence a projective point.
The normal form of Appendix~D, Theorem~\ref{geometry:thm:normalform},
identifies it with $y^2=xz$. The parametrisation
$[s:t]\mapsto[s^2:st:t^2]$ is a bijection from
$\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_q)$, as proved there.
There are $q$ points $[s:1]$ and one point $[1:0]$, so the count is $q+1$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:linesFq}
\idea The split quadric has two line coordinates; an anisotropic complement
prevents a second isotropic direction at a point.
\writeup The Segre theorem gives exactly two families of $q+1$ lines
on the hyperbolic quadric, with no further lines.
For the elliptic quadric, write its form as $\mathbb H\perp N$ with
$N$ anisotropic. At any point $[e]$, split off another hyperbolic plane
$H'=\langle e,f\rangle$. Witt cancellation identifies $H'^\perp$
with $N$. Since $\beta(e,f)=1$,
$e^\perp=Ke\oplus N$, and $q(\lambda e+w)=q(w)$ there.
Its only projective zero is $[e]$, by anisotropy of $N$.
If a line through $[e]$ lay on the quadric, polarisation on its vector
plane would put it in $\mathbb P(e^\perp)$, which has no second zero
point. Thus no such line exists, and no line lies on the elliptic quadric.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:skewlines}
\idea First force existence by counting coefficients. Then the opposite
ruling forces every competing form to be a scalar multiple.
\writeup Quadratic forms in four variables have dimension ten.
Choose three distinct points on each $L_i$. Vanishing at these nine
points gives at most nine linear conditions, so some nonzero form $f$
vanishes at all nine. A binary quadratic with three projective roots
is zero; hence $f$ vanishes identically on each line.

Suppose $f$ were degenerate. Extend scalars to an algebraic closure;
the three lines remain skew, since their defining linear subspaces
still have zero intersection. A rank-two or rank-one quadratic splits
as two planes or a double plane. Two of three contained lines would
then belong to the same plane and meet, which is impossible.
For rank three, the radical is a line $Kr$ in the four-dimensional
vector space. A line on the quadric must contain $[r]$: otherwise its
two-dimensional totally isotropic vector space would inject into the
nondegenerate three-dimensional quotient $V/Kr$. In a nondegenerate
three-space a totally isotropic space $U$ satisfies $U\subset U^\perp$,
so $\dim U\le3-\dim U$, excluding dimension two.
Thus all lines on the rank-three cone meet its vertex, also impossible.
Rank zero is excluded because $f\ne0$. The remaining form $f$ is therefore nondegenerate.

Its vanishing on a line gives a totally isotropic plane $U\subset V$.
Choose $e\in U\setminus\{0\}$ and split off a hyperbolic plane
$H=\langle e,f'\rangle$ with $\beta(e,f')=1$.
The kernel of $\beta(\cdot,f')$ on $U$ is a line. A nonzero $u$ in
that kernel is also orthogonal to $e$, by polarisation on $U$.
Hence $u\in H^\perp$ is isotropic. This nondegenerate two-dimensional
complement is another hyperbolic plane. Thus the quadric is split and
can be written $x_0x_3=x_1x_2$ over $K$.
The three disjoint lines belong to the same ruling by the Segre theorem.

Each line of the other ruling meets all three, in distinct points.
Conversely, a line meeting all three has three distinct zeros of $f$,
so lies on the quadric and belongs to the other ruling.
If another quadratic form $g$ vanishes on $L_1,L_2,L_3$, its restriction
to every such transversal has three zeros and vanishes identically.
Thus $g$ vanishes on the whole split quadric.
Substitute $x=(su,sv,tu,tv)$. The polynomial $g(su,sv,tu,tv)$ vanishes
for all $s,t,u,v\in K$ (including zero-factor cases by homogeneity).
Since $K$ is infinite, it is the zero polynomial.
Of the ten quadratic monomials, only $x_0x_3$ and $x_1x_2$ yield the
same monomial, $stuv$. All other coefficients vanish, and these two
coefficients are opposite. Therefore $g$ is a multiple of
$x_0x_3-x_1x_2$, proving uniqueness.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:v9-schur-complement}
\idea Complete a square with a whole block of variables.
\writeup For $u=x+A^{-1}By$, expand to get
\[
 x^{\mathsf T}Ax+2x^{\mathsf T}By+y^{\mathsf T}Cy
 =u^{\mathsf T}Au+y^{\mathsf T}(C-B^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}B)y.
\]
The change $(x,y)\mapsto(u,y)$ is invertible, with
$x=u-A^{-1}By$. Thus this is an equivalence of forms.
The sum is positive definite exactly when both restrictions are positive
definite: necessity follows by taking one block zero, and sufficiency
by adding the two nonnegative values, at least one strictly positive
on a nonzero pair. For the given matrix, $A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$
and $B=(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$, so its complement is
$c-1/2-1/3=c-5/6$. It is positive definite precisely when $c>5/6$.
At equality the signature is $(2,0)$ with one-dimensional radical;
for $c<5/6$ it is $(2,1)$.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:v9-maximal-isotropic}
\idea A zero vector cannot have a positive part without a matching negative part.
\writeup Use Sylvester coordinates $(x,y,z)$ with
$q=|x|^2-|y|^2$ and radical coordinates $z\in\R^r$.
For a subspace $U$ on which $q=0$, project onto the $(y,z)$ coordinates.
The kernel consists of $(x,0,0)$ with $|x|^2=0$, so the projection is
injective and $\dim U\le m+r$. Projecting onto $(x,z)$ similarly gives
$\dim U\le p+r$. Thus $\dim U\le r+\min(p,m)$.
The radical together with the vectors $e_i+f_i$, $1\le i\le\min(p,m)$,
attains this bound: their positive and negative coordinate contributions
cancel for every linear combination.
For signature $(3,1)$ and $r=0$, the bound is one.
A projective line would require a two-dimensional zero subspace,
so the projective quadric has no line.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:v9-finite-tangent-plane}
\idea Remove the chosen isotropic direction and inspect the residual binary form.
\writeup Split $V=\langle e,f\rangle\perp N$ with
$q(e)=q(f)=0$ and $\beta(e,f)=1$.
Then $e^\perp=Ke\oplus N$, and the form there is $q(w)$ on
$\lambda e+w$. In the hyperbolic case Witt cancellation makes $N$
a hyperbolic plane. Its zero vectors form two distinct lines $Ku,Kv$.
The projective zeros in $e^\perp$ are consequently the two lines
$\mathbb P(Ke\oplus Ku)$ and $\mathbb P(Ke\oplus Kv)$.
Each has $q+1$ points and they meet only in $[e]$, giving $2q+1$.
In the elliptic case $N$ is anisotropic, so a zero vector must have
$w=0$. Only $[e]$ remains. Equivalently the form induced on
$e^\perp/Ke$ is split in the first case and anisotropic in the second.
The tangent-plane equation is $\beta(e,x)=0$, obtained by taking the
linear term of $q(e+tx)$; characteristic different from two is used here.
\end{solution}
\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:v9-rulings-under-map}
\idea Left multiplication acts on the column coordinate, and right
multiplication acts on the row coordinate.
\writeup If $X=cr$, then $AXB=(Ac)(rB)$ has rank one and is nonzero.
The induced map is $([c],[r])\mapsto([Ac],[rB])$.
It therefore keeps each family of fixed-factor lines within the same
family. Transposition gives $X^{\mathsf T}=r^{\mathsf T}c^{\mathsf T}$,
so it swaps the two coordinates and interchanges the families.

The map $X\mapsto AXB$ fixes every quadric point exactly when both
projective maps are identities. An invertible $2\times2$ matrix acting
trivially on a projective line fixes $[1:0]$ and $[0:1]$, so is diagonal;
fixing $[1:1]$ then makes its two diagonal entries equal.
Thus $A$ and $B$ must both be scalar, which also plainly suffices.
Transposition does not fix every point: the rank-one matrices
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and its
transpose represent different points. Indeed any map formed by
transposition followed by invertible left and right multiplication still
swaps the two distinct ruling families, so cannot fix all points.
\end{solution}
