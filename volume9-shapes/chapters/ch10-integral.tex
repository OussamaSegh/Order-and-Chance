\chapter{Integral Geometry}

\begin{center}\itshape
A line can cross a curve once, several times or not at all. Count the crossings over a suitable family of lines and the total measures the curve's length. The question is how to measure the family without giving special treatment to one position or direction.
\end{center}

Bertrand's chord problem makes the choice visible. In his 1889 \emph{Calcul des probabilités}, he asked for the chance that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Uniform endpoints give $1/3$; uniform distance from the centre gives $1/2$; a uniform midpoint in the disk gives $1/4$. Volume~4, Theorem~5.11 computed these laws. Bertrand's verdict, ``none of the three is false, none is correct'', concerns the missing specification: ``at random'' has not yet named a probability measure.

Poincaré's 1896 treatment singles out a measure by symmetry. Moving or rotating an entire configuration should not change the measure of its lines. This condition forces a measure proportional to $dp\,d\theta$, where $p$ is a signed perpendicular offset and $\theta$ a normal angle. It is an infinite measure, so it is not a probability law on all lines. Restricting to lines that hit a fixed disk and normalizing does give a probability law, and it gives Bertrand's answer $1/2$.

Jaynes used this symmetry model in 1973 to discuss straw-tossing experiments. The model assumes that the sampling mechanism has no preferred position or direction before conditioning on a hit. That is a meaningful additional assumption, rather than a reason the other two precisely specified experiments have wrong answers. His reported experiment and argument are listed in the sources.

Counting with this measure leads to Crofton's length formula. The same calculation measures the perimeter of a convex set, the intersections of moving curves, and the area of a solid's shadow. Appendix~J returns to these examples through polygonal needles and widths of convex sets.

\section{The measure of lines}

For $u_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, write
\[
\ell(\theta,p)=\{x\in\R^2:\langle x,u_\theta\rangle=p\}.
\]
An \emph{oriented line} is specified by $\theta$ modulo $2\pi$ and $p\in\R$; its direction is the normal turned by $+\pi/2$. Thus the oriented line space $\mathcal L^+$ is a cylinder. Forgetting orientation identifies $(\theta,p)$ with $(\theta+\pi,-p)$. The unoriented line space $\mathcal L$ is parametrized once by $0\le\theta<\pi$, $p\in\R$, with this identification at the seam.

A rotation by $\alpha$ takes the parameters to $(\theta+\alpha,p)$. A translation by $v$ takes them to $(\theta,p+\langle v,u_\theta\rangle)$. Both preserve $d\theta\,dp$: the first shifts the angle, and the second shifts each offset fibre. The following theorem says that local finiteness and these symmetries leave only an overall scaling freedom.

\begin{theorem}[uniqueness of the measure on lines]\label{thm:linesunique}
A Borel measure $\nu$ on $\mathcal L^+$ that is finite on compact sets and invariant under rotations and translations has the form $c\,d\theta\,dp$, with $c\ge0$. On $\mathcal L$, every such invariant locally finite Borel measure likewise has the form $c\,d\theta\,dp$ in the parameters $0\le\theta<\pi$, $p\in\R$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A spatial translation does not shift every offset by the same amount: it shifts it by $\langle v,u_\theta\rangle$. We first recover a uniform offset shift by restricting to small angular arcs.

Let $a$ be bounded Borel on the circle, $b\in C_c(\R)$, and $t\in\R$. Partition the angle circle into half-open arcs $I_j$ of length $\delta$, centred at $\theta_j$. Translation by $tu_{\theta_j}$ and invariance give
\[
\int a(\theta)\mathbf1_{I_j}(\theta)b(p+t\cos(\theta-\theta_j))\,d\nu
=\int a(\theta)\mathbf1_{I_j}(\theta)b(p)\,d\nu.
\]
For $\theta\in I_j$, the shift differs from $t$ by at most $|t|\delta^2/8$. Let $\omega$ be a modulus of uniform continuity for $b$. All these integrands have support in one compact cylinder $Q$, independently of the partition. Sum over $j$ to obtain
\[
\left|\int a(\theta)b(p+t)\,d\nu-\int a(\theta)b(p)\,d\nu\right|
\le\|a\|_\infty\omega(|t|\delta^2/8)\nu(Q).
\]
Let $\delta\to0$; local finiteness makes the right side tend to zero.

For a Borel angular set $I$, define $m_I(B)=\nu(I\times B)$. The preceding identity with $a=\mathbf1_I$ shows invariance of its integrals against compactly supported continuous functions under every offset translation. Approximating closed-interval indicators by continuous functions with common compact support gives translation invariance on closed intervals. In particular all singleton masses are equal and must be zero, since infinitely many equal positive atoms in a compact interval contradict local finiteness. Thus half-open intervals have the same translation invariance as well.

A locally finite translation-invariant measure $m$ on $\R$ is a multiple of Lebesgue measure: subdivide $[0,1)$ into $n$ equal intervals to obtain $m([0,1/n))=m([0,1))/n$, extend to rational lengths by addition and then to real lengths by monotone limits. Interval uniqueness from Volume~7, Theorem~7.5 extends the equality to Borel sets. Hence $\nu(I\times J)=\kappa(I)|J|$, where $\kappa(I)=\nu(I\times[0,1))$ is a finite measure on the angle circle. Rotation invariance and the same equal-arc subdivision argument give $\kappa(I)=c|I|$. Rectangles form a generating $\pi$-system, and both measures are finite on a countable cylinder cover, so the equality extends to all Borel sets.

For unoriented lines, lift a measure by assigning the sum of a test function over the two orientations of each line. The lift is invariant and locally finite, hence is $c\,d\theta\,dp$ on $\mathcal L^+$. For a nonnegative test function $F$ on $\mathcal L$, its pullback has lifted integral $2\int F\,d\nu$. Its cylinder integral over $0\le\theta<2\pi$ is twice that over $0\le\theta<\pi$. Cancelling the two factors of $2$ gives the asserted unoriented formula.
\end{proof}

Fix the normalization $d\ell=dp\,d\theta$ on $0\le\theta<\pi$. Its relation to Haar measure needs care. For a fixed initial oriented line, a motion can be parametrized by its resulting $(\theta,p)$ and a translation coordinate $s$ along the resulting line. Motion Haar measure is $d\theta\,dp\,ds$. Integrating out $s$ over all of $\R$ gives infinite mass to every positive-measure family of lines. Thus direct pushforward is not the locally finite line measure. Instead the line measure is the quotient factor $d\theta\,dp$, with the stabilizing along-line translation measured separately by $ds$. These coordinates give the factorization explicitly, without an abstract quotient-integration theorem.

\begin{corollary}[Bertrand's chord, answered]\label{cor:bertrand}
The lines meeting the unit disk have measure $2\pi$. Under their normalized invariant measure, $|p|$ is uniform on $[0,1]$, and the chord has length greater than $\sqrt3$ with probability $1/2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A hit means $-1\le p\le1$, so the measure is $\pi\cdot2$. After normalization, $\theta$ and $p$ are independent and uniform on their respective intervals. The chord length is $2\sqrt{1-p^2}$, exceeding $\sqrt3$ exactly when $|p|<1/2$.
\end{proof}

The other experiments have different offset densities. Uniform endpoints give $2/(\pi\sqrt{1-p^2})$ for $|p|\in(0,1)$, while a uniform midpoint gives density $2p$. These are well-defined circle-based laws, but neither is the restriction of a motion-invariant locally finite measure on all lines. Exercise~\ref{ex:bertrandlaws} derives them and the invariant chord-length distribution.

\section{Crofton's formula}

For a parametrized curve $c$, let $n(c\cap\ell)$ count the parameters that put $c$ on $\ell$. It counts different traversals separately, including retraced arcs. Values may be infinite. Endpoints shared by consecutive pieces are counted once; the exceptional lines through those endpoints have measure zero, so either additive convention gives the same integral.

\begin{lemma}[counting preimages]\label{lem:banach}
For a $C^1$ function $g:[0,1]\to\R$, the function $p\mapsto\#g^{-1}(p)$ is Borel measurable, with values in the nonnegative integers and $\infty$, and
\[
\int_{\R}\#g^{-1}(p)\,dp=\int_0^1|g'(t)|\,dt.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The open set where $g'\ne0$ in $(0,1)$ is a countable disjoint union of intervals $J_k$. On each interval $g'$ has constant sign, so $g$ is strictly monotone and its image has length $\int_{J_k}|g'|$. Critical values have measure zero by the one-dimensional case of Theorem~\ref{thm:sard}; the two endpoint values are also null. Away from these values, every preimage lies in exactly one $J_k$. Therefore the count is $\sum_k\mathbf1_{g(J_k)}(p)$ almost everywhere. Tonelli gives the desired identity because $g'=0$ outside the union, apart from endpoints.

To verify measurability even on critical values, divide $[0,1)$ into dyadic half-open cells and add the singleton $\{1\}$. Count the cells whose image contains $p$. Each cell image is an interval and hence Borel. The counts increase with refinement. For any finite collection of distinct preimages, a fine enough partition puts them in different cells. Their increasing limit is thus the full count, including $\infty$.
\end{proof}

For Crofton we also need joint measurability in angle and offset. Use the same cell construction for $g_\theta(t)=\langle c(t),u_\theta\rangle$. For a compact parameter interval, the set of pairs $(\theta,p)$ attained by $(\theta,g_\theta(t))$ is compact. A half-open cell is a countable union of compact intervals, so its attained set is Borel as well. The increasing cell-count limit is therefore jointly Borel. This justifies the two-variable Tonelli step rather than merely assuming it from separate measurability.

\begin{theorem}[Crofton, 1868]\label{thm:crofton}
A piecewise $C^1$ planar curve of length $L$ satisfies
\[
\int_{\mathcal L}n(c\cap\ell)\,d\ell=2L.
\]
Thus half the line measure, counted with traversal multiplicity, equals length.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First let $c$ be $C^1$ on $[0,1]$. Apply the preimage lemma to $g_\theta(t)=\langle c(t),u_\theta\rangle$, then Tonelli using the joint measurability just proved:
\[
\int_{\mathcal L}n\,d\ell
=\int_0^1\int_0^\pi|\langle c'(t),u_\theta\rangle|\,d\theta\,dt.
\]
For a nonzero vector $c'(t)$ the inner integral is $|c'(t)|\int_0^\pi|\cos(\theta-\varphi)|d\theta=2|c'(t)|$. For a zero vector it is also twice its length, namely zero. Integrating gives $2L$. For finitely many $C^1$ pieces, add the formulas; lines through the finitely many joining points form a null family.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Buffon's noodle]\label{cor:noodle}
For $d>0$, a piecewise $C^1$ curve of length $L$ thrown on parallel lines spaced $d$ apart, with independent uniform direction and offset modulo $d$, has mean crossing count $2L/(\pi d)$, counted with traversal multiplicity.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Equivalently fix the curve and choose a line normal $\theta$ uniformly in $[0,\pi)$ and an offset $p_0$ uniformly in $[0,d)$. The ruled lines are $\ell(\theta,p_0+kd)$, $k\in\Z$. Tonelli and the partition of the offset axis give
\[
\mathbb E[N]=\frac1{\pi d}\int_0^\pi\int_0^d
\sum_{k\in\Z}n(c\cap\ell(\theta,p_0+kd))\,dp_0\,d\theta
=\frac1{\pi d}\int_{\mathcal L}n\,d\ell
=\frac{2L}{\pi d}.
\]
\end{proof}

For a straight needle with $0\le L\le d$, the count is zero or one almost surely, so the formula is also its crossing probability, recovering Volume~4, Theorem~5.12. A bent curve can cross several times even if its total length is short; its expected count is still fixed by length, but its probability of any hit need not equal that count. Random-direction crossing counts can estimate length in images; Steinhaus' longimeter used this principle. A fixed small set of directions can give a biased estimate if the curve has preferred directions, so the sampling rule matters in this application too.

\section{Convex sets}

Let $K$ be a compact convex planar set with nonempty interior and piecewise $C^1$ boundary, of perimeter $L(K)$. Its width $w_K(\theta)$ is the length of its projection onto the normal line $\R u_\theta$. We need to know why almost every line hitting $K$ crosses its boundary exactly twice, without using the separation theorem from a later appendix.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:convexline}
If $x_0\in\operatorname{int}K$ and $y\in K$, then $[x_0,y)\subset\operatorname{int}K$. A line through an interior point therefore meets $\partial K$ in exactly two points. Lines meeting $K$ but missing its interior form a null family for $d\ell$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose a disk $B(x_0,r)\subset K$. At $z=(1-\lambda)x_0+\lambda y$, $0\le\lambda<1$, convexity gives
$B(z,(1-\lambda)r)=(1-\lambda)B(x_0,r)+\lambda y\subset K$. This proves the first assertion and also that $K$ is the closure of its interior. A line through $x_0$ cuts $K$ in a compact segment $[a,b]$ with $a\ne b$. Applying the first assertion towards each endpoint puts $(a,b)$ in the interior, while points just past the endpoints are outside. Thus exactly $a,b$ are boundary points on the line.

For each $\theta$, the projection of $K$ is an interval $[h_-(\theta),h_+(\theta)]$. Its interior is the projection of $\operatorname{int}K$: the latter is an open interval, convex and dense in the full projection. Hence the only hitting offsets missing the interior are its two endpoints. The functions $h_\pm$ are continuous, because the maximum and minimum of $x\cdot u_\theta$ over bounded $K$ change by at most $\max_K|x|\,|u_\theta-u_\varphi|$. Their graphs are Borel and have zero measure by Fubini.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Crofton and Cauchy]\label{thm:cauchycrofton}
For such a $K$, the measure of lines meeting $K$ equals its perimeter, and
\[
L(K)=\int_0^\pi w_K(\theta)\,d\theta.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Almost every hitting line meets the boundary twice by the lemma; nonhitting lines meet it zero times. Crofton applied to a single traversal of the boundary gives
\[
2L(K)=2\int_{\mathcal L}\mathbf1_{\ell\cap K\ne\varnothing}\,d\ell
=2\int_0^\pi w_K(\theta)\,d\theta.
\]
Divide by $2$.
\end{proof}

Constant width $w$ therefore gives perimeter $\pi w$. The polygon proof and extension to all compact convex sets, including segments and points, appear later in Appendix~J. The line interpretation already gives useful conditional probabilities: for two bodies $K'\subset K$ of the type above, a normalized invariant line hitting $K$ hits $K'$ with probability $L(K')/L(K)$. A segment of length $s$ has hitting-line measure $2s$, directly from Crofton for a segment, since almost every hitting line meets it once. No perimeter convention for degenerate bodies is needed for that observation.

For Exercise~\ref{ex:twolines}, we shall also need the planar isoperimetric inequality before reaching Appendix~J. Here is a local proof using the periodic Wirtinger inequality already proved in Volume~5 with Fourier series. Parametrize the positively oriented boundary by arc length $s\in[0,L]$ and translate so that both coordinate means are zero. Green's theorem from Chapter~3 and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
2A=\int_0^L\det(c,c')\,ds
\le\left(\int_0^L|c|^2ds\int_0^L|c'|^2ds\right)^{1/2}.
\]
The periodic Wirtinger inequality is
$\int|c|^2\le(L/(2\pi))^2\int|c'|^2$. Since $|c'|=1$ almost everywhere, this yields $2A\le L^2/(2\pi)$, or $4\pi A\le L^2$. These integral identities apply to the piecewise smooth boundary and its absolutely continuous arc-length parametrization; the Fourier proof of Wirtinger extends to it by piecewise smooth approximation in the function-and-first-derivative square-integral norm. Such approximation can be obtained by periodic convolution, which preserves the means and converges with the first derivative in $L^2$, as in Volume~8's approximation arguments.

Equality in Wirtinger permits only first harmonics, $c(s)=a\cos(2\pi s/L)+b\sin(2\pi s/L)$. The unit-speed identity forces $|a|=|b|$ and $a\cdot b=0$, by comparing its sine and cosine terms of double frequency. Thus equality gives a circle, whose interior is a disk. Conversely a disk attains equality. Thus a disk is the unique equality shape, up to its centre and radius.

\section{Moving curves and shadows}

Represent an orientation-preserving rigid motion by $g(x)=R_\alpha x+v$, with $0\le\alpha<2\pi$ and $v\in\R^2$. Its Haar measure is $dg=dv\,d\alpha$. Left multiplication rotates or translates $v$ and shifts the angle; right multiplication shifts $v$ by a vector depending on $\alpha$ and shifts the angle. Both coordinate changes have absolute Jacobian $1$. The total measure is infinite, so an integral against $dg$ is a weighted total over motions, not a probability expectation without further conditioning.

\begin{theorem}[Poincaré, 1896]\label{thm:poincare}
For polygonal curves $C_1,C_2$ of lengths $L_1,L_2$, with intersections counted by traversal multiplicity,
\[
\int n(C_1\cap gC_2)\,dg=4L_1L_2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Consider first segments of lengths $a,b$. At a fixed rotation, a translate of the second meets the first exactly for translation vectors in the difference of the two segments. When their directions are not parallel this is a parallelogram of area $ab|\sin\beta|$, where $\beta$ is their angle. Apart from boundary translations there is exactly one intersection. Parallel directions and degenerate segments contribute zero to the translation integral, so the same formula applies. As the rotation runs through $2\pi$, $\beta$ does too, giving
\[
\int n(s\cap gs')\,dg=ab\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin\beta|\,d\beta=4ab.
\]
For polygonal curves, sum over pairs of segments. Intersections involving a vertex or overlapping parallel segments occur in a null set of motions; traversal counting handles repeated segments without changing the sum. Each pair's incidence set is Borel in the motion parameters, so the finite sum is measurable. The resulting total is $4(\sum a)(\sum b)=4L_1L_2$.
\end{proof}

We quote the extension to finite-length rectifiable curves with the appropriate traversal multiplicity; convergence of intersection counts requires more than saying that pictures converge. The full planar kinematic formula, also quoted, states that motions for which compact convex bodies $K_1,gK_2$ intersect have measure
$2\pi(A_1+A_2)+L_1L_2$. Santaló treats this and its higher-dimensional versions. Exercise~\ref{ex:v9-ch11-moving-circles} verifies both totals for circles and disks directly.

For a solid in $\R^3$, replace a projected interval by a projected area. Let $\pi_u$ project orthogonally onto $u^\perp$, with $u$ uniform on $S^2$ under normalized area measure $\sigma$. A useful elementary fact, Archimedes' hat-box calculation, is that $u\cdot n$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ for any fixed unit vector $n$. Rotate $n$ to the north pole and parametrize
\[
u=(\sqrt{1-z^2}\cos\phi,\sqrt{1-z^2}\sin\phi,z).
\]
The two coordinate tangent vectors are orthogonal with lengths $\sqrt{1-z^2}$ and $1/\sqrt{1-z^2}$, so $dA=dz\,d\phi$ away from the null poles. Consequently $\mathbb E|u\cdot n|=1/2$.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy's surface area formula]\label{thm:cauchyspace}
For a convex polytope $K\subset\R^3$ with nonempty interior and surface area $A$,
\[
\int_{S^2}\operatorname{area}(\pi_u(K))\,d\sigma(u)=A/4.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A face $F$ of area $A_F$ and outer normal $n_F$ has projected area $A_F|n_F\cdot u|$, by the determinant of orthogonal projection between the two planes. Exclude the finitely many great circles where a face is parallel to $u$; they have $\sigma$-measure zero. Also exclude points in the shadow lying on projections of edges or on its polygonal boundary, a planar null set.

Every remaining interior shadow point $y$ has a preimage in $\operatorname{int}K$. To prove this without a separation theorem, choose $x_0\in\operatorname{int}K$ and $y_0=\pi_u(x_0)$. For sufficiently small $\lambda>0$, the point $q=(y-\lambda y_0)/(1-\lambda)$ stays in the shadow because it is close to its interior point $y$. Choose $z\in K$ projecting to $q$. Then $\lambda x_0+(1-\lambda)z$ is interior by the ball argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:convexline}, and projects to $y$.

The line $y+\R u$ therefore meets the boundary in exactly two points, both in face interiors after the edge exclusion. Thus the projected faces cover the shadow twice almost everywhere:
\[
\sum_F A_F|n_F\cdot u|=2\operatorname{area}(\pi_u(K)).
\]
Average in $u$. Each absolute dot product has mean $1/2$, so the mean shadow area is $\tfrac12\sum_F A_F\cdot\tfrac12=A/4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
A symmetry condition has turned crossing counts into length and projected area into surface area. Hadwiger's valuation theorem, quoted here, describes the wider setting. On compact convex sets in $\R^n$, every continuous motion-invariant valuation is a linear combination of the $n+1$ intrinsic volumes. A valuation satisfies $\mu(K\cup M)+\mu(K\cap M)=\mu(K)+\mu(M)$ whenever the union is convex, with $\mu(\varnothing)=0$; continuity is for convergence of compact convex sets in Hausdorff distance. In the plane the three quantities are area, perimeter and Euler characteristic. In three dimensions there are four, including volume, surface area and mean width. The theorem requires these hypotheses, rather than asserting that symmetry alone determines every conceivable measurement. Related invariant integrals on hyperbolic geodesics and the Radon transform lead towards tomography, where line integrals are used to reconstruct an image.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

For a disk of radius $R$, the family of hitting lines has measure $2\pi R$. If a curve of length $L$ lies inside it, Crofton says that conditioning on those lines gives mean crossing count $L/(\pi R)$. This is a practical way to read the normalization: doubling the window radius halves the expected count, even though the curve has not changed. Increasing the number of crossings by retracing the curve also increases its measured length.

The uniqueness proof explains the sampling rule, and the preimage lemma explains why a count becomes an integral of speed. From there convexity makes almost every boundary crossing count exactly two; it is this extra geometric information that turns perimeter ratios into hitting probabilities. The moving-segment parallelogram and the twice-covered shadow repeat the same method with different incidence spaces.

This closes the chapters. The appendices offer transformations, conics, curves, lattices and convex geometry in more concrete pictures, and can be read alongside the constructions that use them. For the next volume, \emph{Abstraction: Categories, Algebra and Homology}, the useful habit is to ask what a map preserves. Here that question selected measures and turned geometry into an integral. Chapter~8 also leads towards Volume~12, where random matrices and the zeros of the zeta function meet again.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:bertrandlaws}
For the unit disk, derive the density of the distance $|p|$ under uniform independent endpoints and under a uniform midpoint. Show why neither produces the invariant uniform-offset law. Under the invariant law, find the chord-length distribution and its mean, including the range of possible lengths.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:chord}
Let $K$ be a compact convex planar body of area $A$ and perimeter $L$, with the boundary regularity used above. Put $s(\ell)=\operatorname{length}(K\cap\ell)$. Prove $\int s(\ell)d\ell=\pi A$ and deduce that the invariant mean chord length among hitting lines is $\pi A/L$. Evaluate it for a radius-$r$ disk and a unit square.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:twolines}
Choose two independent lines from the normalized invariant law among lines hitting such a convex body $K$. Prove that their intersection lies in $K$ with probability $2\pi A/L^2$. Compute the disk and square cases, and prove the bound $1/2$, including its equality case, using the earlier proof in this chapter.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:laplacegrid}
A straight needle of length $0\le l\le d$ is thrown on a square grid of spacing $d>0$. Take its angle uniform and its two positional offsets independent and uniform modulo $d$. Prove that it crosses at least one grid joint with probability $(4ld-l^2)/(\pi d^2)$, Laplace's grid formula.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:shadows}
(a) Find the mean shadow area of a unit cube. Show that its largest shadow has area $\sqrt3$ and, along a body diagonal, is a regular hexagon.
(b) Compute the shadow area of a radius-$r$ sphere in every direction and compare it with one quarter of its surface area. Explain why this direct check does not require applying a polytope theorem to a sphere.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:spherecrofton}
For a $C^1$ curve $c:[0,1]\to S^2$ of length $L$, count intersections with $u^\perp\cap S^2$ by parameter multiplicity. Prove
\[
\int_{S^2}n(c\cap u^\perp)\,d\sigma(u)=L/\pi.
\]
Give a change-of-variables proof valid for $C^1$ curves, including points with zero velocity; a thin-band heuristic alone is not sufficient. Check the result on a great circle traversed once.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch11-window-law}
For a compact convex planar body $K$ with the boundary regularity used above, define $h_\pm(\theta)=\min/\max_{x\in K}x\cdot u_\theta$.
(a) Find the conditional angle density and offset law for an invariant line conditioned to hit $K$.
(b) For an axis-aligned rectangle with side lengths $a,b>0$, write the angle density explicitly and compare its values at $0$ and $\pi/2$.
(c) Characterize when the conditional angle is uniform. Must the window be a disk? Use the constant-width example already described in this volume.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch11-moving-circles}
Let $C_r,C_s$ be circles of radii $r,s>0$ and let $D_r,D_s$ be the disks they bound.
(a) Directly compute $\int n(C_r\cap gC_s)dg$ from the set of possible centre translations, treating tangencies and coincident circles correctly.
(b) Compute the motion measure for $D_r\cap gD_s\ne\varnothing$ and compare it with the quoted kinematic formula.
(c) If the translated centre is instead uniform in a disk of radius $R\ge r+s$, and the rotation is independently uniform, find the crossing probability and mean count. Explain the normalization missing from an infinite Haar integral.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch11-affine-crofton}
Let $T:\R^2\to\R^2$ be invertible, $D=|\det T|$ and $q(\theta)=|T^{-\mathsf T}u_\theta|$.
(a) Show that $T\ell(\theta,p)$ has unit normal $T^{-\mathsf T}u_\theta/q$ and offset $p/q$. Prove that the line-coordinate Jacobian has absolute value $1/(Dq^3)$.
(b) Deduce the affine form of Crofton,
\[
\operatorname{length}(Tc)=\frac12\int_{\mathcal L}
\frac{n(c\cap\ell(\theta,p))}{Dq(\theta)^3}\,dp\,d\theta.
\]
(c) Check rotations and uniform positive dilations. For $T=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)$ with $a,b>0$, obtain an integral for the perimeter of the resulting ellipse by applying the formula to the unit circle.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch11-spherical-cap}
Let $K_a=\{x\in S^2:x\cdot n\ge\cos a\}$, where $n$ is a fixed unit vector and $0<a<\pi$. A great circle is chosen by uniform pole $u$ (the two opposite poles describe the same circle).
(a) Show that the probability of meeting the latitude boundary $\partial K_a$ is $\sin a$, and find its expected intersection count.
(b) Find the probability of meeting the whole cap $K_a$, distinguishing $a\le\pi/2$ from $a>\pi/2$.
(c) Check spherical Crofton using the latitude length and describe the limits as $a\to0$ and $a\to\pi$. Why is half the boundary crossing count not the cap-hitting probability for a large cap?
\end{exercise}

\section{Hints}

\begin{hint}{ex:bertrandlaws}
Fix one endpoint by rotational symmetry and use $|p|=|\cos(\varphi/2)|$ for the other endpoint's uniform angle. A uniform disk point has radial distribution $r^2$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:chord}
For a fixed normal direction, integrating chord lengths over offsets is exactly Fubini's area formula in rotated coordinates.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:twolines}
Condition on the first line. Its chord has length $s$, and hitting that segment has line measure $2s$, directly from Crofton. Normalize by $L$ and average again.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:laplacegrid}
Use inclusion--exclusion. At angle $\theta$, the two hit probabilities are $l|\sin\theta|/d$ and $l|\cos\theta|/d$, independent conditional on the angle.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:shadows}
For the cube, the projected-face identity reduces the shadow area to $|u_1|+|u_2|+|u_3|$. Use Cauchy--Schwarz for the maximum.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:spherecrofton}
On a short parameter interval choose a $C^1$ orthonormal frame $e_1,e_2$ perpendicular to $c$, and parametrize the incidence poles by $F(t,\phi)=e_1(t)\cos\phi+e_2(t)\sin\phi$. Its absolute spherical Jacobian is $|F\cdot c'|$. Use equal-dimensional Sard and countably many inverse charts to justify counting multiplicities.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-ch11-window-law}
Integrate the constant joint density $1/L$ over the offset interval $[h_-,h_+]$. Projection of a rectangle adds the projections of its two edge segments.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-ch11-moving-circles}
Two circles cross twice exactly when their centre distance is strictly between $|r-s|$ and $r+s$. The region of translations is an annulus. Rotations leave a centred circle unchanged but still supply a factor $2\pi$ in Haar measure.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-ch11-affine-crofton}
For $v(\theta)=T^{-\mathsf T}u_\theta$, the derivative of its argument is $\det(v,v')/|v|^2$. The offset scale contributes one more factor $1/q$ to the full Jacobian.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-ch11-spherical-cap}
On $u^\perp\cap S^2$, the largest and smallest values of $x\cdot n$ are $\pm\sqrt{1-(u\cdot n)^2}$. Use the uniform law of $u\cdot n$ on $[-1,1]$.
\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:bertrandlaws}
\idea Compare the radial-offset distributions produced by the three sampling rules.
\writeup Under uniform independent endpoints, rotation lets the first have angle zero and the other have uniform relative angle $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$. The chord's distance is $P=|\cos(\varphi/2)|$. Its two inverse branches give density $2/(\pi\sqrt{1-P^2})$ for $0<P<1$. Under a uniform midpoint, the event $P\le r$ is a concentric disk of area fraction $r^2$, giving density $2P$. Neither density is the constant $1$ of the invariant law, so neither has a constant line-coordinate density after conditioning on the disk.

For the invariant law, $P$ is uniform and the length $S=2\sqrt{1-P^2}$ has survival function
\[
\Pr(S>s)=\sqrt{1-s^2/4}\quad(0\le s\le2).
\]
It is $1$ for $s<0$ and $0$ for $s\ge2$; there are no endpoint atoms. Its mean is
$\int_0^1 2\sqrt{1-P^2}\,dP=\pi/2$, the area of a half unit disk. The possible lengths range from $0$ to $2$, with the endpoint values occurring only in null tangent or central families.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:chord}
\idea Slicing in any fixed direction reconstructs the same area.
\writeup For fixed $\theta$, use the orthonormal coordinates along $u_\theta$ and its perpendicular. Fubini gives $\int_{\R}s(\ell(\theta,p))dp=A$. Integrate over $0\le\theta<\pi$ to get $\int s\,d\ell=\pi A$. The hitting family has total measure $L$, so its normalized mean chord is $\pi A/L$. A radius-$r$ disk gives $\pi(\pi r^2)/(2\pi r)=\pi r/2$; a unit square gives $\pi/4$. The mean can be shorter than a side because lines near corners give short chords.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twolines}
\idea The second line must hit the segment cut out by the first.
\writeup Outside the null family of parallel or coincident pairs, the intersection lies in $K$ exactly when the second line meets the first line's chord. A segment of length $s$ has hitting-line measure $2s$ by Crofton. Hence the measure of successful ordered pairs is
$\int_{\mathcal L}2s(\ell_1)d\ell_1=2\pi A$, using the preceding exercise. All pairs of hitting lines have product measure $L^2$, giving probability $2\pi A/L^2$.

For a disk it is $1/2$; for a unit square it is $2\pi/16=\pi/8\approx0.3927$. The local isoperimetric proof above gives $4\pi A\le L^2$, with equality exactly for disks, so the desired probability is at most $1/2$, with precisely that equality case. No result from the later convex appendix is needed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:laplacegrid}
\idea Horizontal and vertical hit events are conditionally independent, so subtract their intersection once.
\writeup At angle $\theta$, a uniform offset modulo $d$ puts an interval of height $h\le d$ across a ruled line with probability $h/d$. The two extents are $l|\sin\theta|$ and $l|\cos\theta|$, and the two offsets are independent. Each marginal hit probability averages to $2l/(\pi d)$. The probability of both is
\[
\frac{l^2}{2\pi d^2}\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin\theta\cos\theta|\,d\theta
=\frac{l^2}{\pi d^2}.
\]
Inclusion--exclusion gives $(4ld-l^2)/(\pi d^2)$. At $l=0$ it is zero; at $l=d$ it is $3/\pi\approx0.9549$. Directions and placements causing tangencies, a grid-vertex hit or a coincident segment form null events and do not alter the formula.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:shadows}
\idea The cube's six face contributions combine into three absolute coordinates.
\writeup (a) Its mean shadow is $6/4=3/2$. For a fixed unit $u$, the twice-covered-face identity gives area $|u_1|+|u_2|+|u_3|\le\sqrt3$, with equality exactly when all three absolute coordinates equal $1/\sqrt3$. Along any body diagonal, the two opposite vertices on that diagonal project to the centre and the other six project to a regular hexagon; in the orthonormal shadow basis $(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$, $(1,1,-2)/\sqrt6$, these six projected vertices have coordinates $(0,\pm2/\sqrt6)$ and $(\pm1/\sqrt2,\pm1/\sqrt6)$. They lie equally spaced on a circle of radius $\sqrt{2/3}$, giving a regular hexagon of area $(3\sqrt3/2)(2/3)=\sqrt3$.
(b) Every sphere projection is a radius-$r$ disk of area $\pi r^2$. Its surface area is $4\pi r^2$, so it satisfies the same quarter-area identity directly. This is a consistency check. Extension of Cauchy's theorem to arbitrary convex bodies requires its own approximation theorem; the direct sphere calculation does not prove that general extension.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:spherecrofton}
\idea Parametrize all incident poles by a two-dimensional map and integrate its Jacobian with multiplicity.
\writeup Subdivide $[0,1]$ into finitely many intervals on which a fixed vector $a$ is never parallel to $c(t)$. Such a finite subdivision exists by continuity and compactness. Normalize $a-(a\cdot c)c$ to obtain a $C^1$ unit vector $e_1(t)\perp c(t)$, and put $e_2=c\times e_1$. On each interval the incidence map
\[
F(t,\phi)=e_1(t)\cos\phi+e_2(t)\sin\phi
\]
parametrizes exactly the unit poles perpendicular to $c(t)$. A preimage of a pole is therefore an intersection parameter, with a unique $\phi$ modulo $2\pi$. Values at subdivision endpoints and the angular seam are images of $C^1$ curves and have spherical area zero, so splitting into rectangles does not affect the integral.

Differentiate $F\cdot c=0$ and use $F_\phi=c\times F$. Since $F_t\cdot F=0$, the vector triple-product identity gives
\[
F\cdot(F_t\times F_\phi)=-F_t\cdot c=F\cdot c'.
\]
Thus the absolute area Jacobian is $|F\cdot c'|$, also when $c'=0$.

Here is the required multiplicity justification. For a $C^1$ map between two-dimensional coordinate patches, equal-dimensional Sard (Theorem~\ref{thm:sard}) makes the critical image null. Cover the regular set by countably many inverse-function neighborhoods and partition it into disjoint Borel pieces subordinate to that cover. Each piece lies in a diffeomorphism chart; change of variables on it and Tonelli show that the integral of the absolute Jacobian equals the integral of its preimage count. The critical domain has zero Jacobian and its image is null, so it contributes nothing. The count is Borel: having at least $k$ distinct preimages is a countable union of compact-image conditions obtained by exhausting the parameter rectangle by compact sets and requiring pairwise parameter separation at least $1/m$. Sphere charts and the finitely many seams preserve this argument. This proves the needed area formula for the actual $C^1$ incidence map, without assuming a smoother tangent field.

For fixed $t$, $c'(t)$ lies in the span of $e_1,e_2$. Its dot product with $F$ integrates in absolute value to $4|c'(t)|$ as $\phi$ runs through $2\pi$, including zero speed. Hence
\[
\int_{S^2}n(c\cap u^\perp)\,dA(u)
=\int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}|F\cdot c'|\,d\phi\,dt=4L.
\]
Divide by $4\pi$ to normalize $\sigma$. For a great circle $L=2\pi$, and almost every other great circle crosses it twice, giving mean $2=L/\pi$. Coincident great circles form a null pair of poles.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch11-window-law}
\idea Conditioning restricts the offset interval differently at different angles.
\writeup (a) The joint density on hitting parameters is $1/L$ for $h_-(\theta)\le p\le h_+(\theta)$. Integrating out $p$ gives angle density $w_K(\theta)/L$. Conditional on an angle, $p$ is uniform on that projection interval. Every width is positive because the body has interior.
(b) A rectangle with horizontal side $a$ and vertical side $b$ has width $a|\cos\theta|+b|\sin\theta|$ and perimeter $2(a+b)$. Thus the density is this width divided by $2(a+b)$; at $0$ it is $a/(2(a+b))$, at $\pi/2$ it is $b/(2(a+b))$. Even for a square, intermediate directions have a different density.
(c) Uniformity means $w_K(\theta)/L=1/\pi$ almost everywhere. Width is continuous, so this is equivalent to constant width everywhere. A disk is one example, but a noncircular constant-width body also has this law. For a construction using only earlier tools, take a Reuleaux triangle as the intersection of radius-$w$ disks centred at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $w$: each support direction pairs a vertex with its opposite circular arc, a distance $w$ apart. Its three arc--vertex pairs cover every direction. Appendix~J develops this example further. Thus uniform conditional angle does not characterize disks. The correct sampling rule for a general window weights angles by width before choosing the offset.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch11-moving-circles}
\idea Intersection of two circle boundaries corresponds to an annulus of centre positions.
\writeup (a) For $|r-s|<|v|<r+s$ there are two crossings, and outside this annulus there are none. The boundary translation circles are tangency events of area zero. If $r=s$, coincident centres give coincident circles and infinitely many intersections, but this single translation also has measure zero. The translation integral is twice the annulus area,
\[
2\pi((r+s)^2-(r-s)^2)=8\pi rs.
\]
Rotation contributes a further $2\pi$, giving $16\pi^2rs=4(2\pi r)(2\pi s)$, consistent with Poincaré's extension.
(b) Disk intersection requires $|v|\le r+s$, so the motion measure is $2\pi\cdot\pi(r+s)^2=2\pi^2(r+s)^2$. The quoted expression gives $2\pi(\pi r^2+\pi s^2)+(2\pi r)(2\pi s)$, exactly the same result.
(c) With $v$ uniform in a radius-$R$ disk containing the entire annulus, the crossing probability is $4rs/R^2$ and the mean count is $8rs/R^2$. Uniform rotation changes neither. Equivalently divide the Haar count integral by the finite motion-window measure $2\pi^2R^2$. Without this division there is no probability interpretation for the infinite motion measure.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch11-affine-crofton}
\idea Transform the normals and offsets together; an angular Jacobian is as necessary as the offset scale.
\writeup (a) If $y=Tx$, then $u_\theta\cdot x=(T^{-\mathsf T}u_\theta)\cdot y=p$, giving the stated unit normal and $p'=p/q$. Put $v=T^{-\mathsf T}u_\theta$. For a local angle branch $\theta'$, differentiating its unit direction gives
\[
\frac{d\theta'}{d\theta}=\frac{\det(v,v')}{|v|^2}
=\frac1{\det(T)q^2}.
\]
The full map $(\theta,p)\mapsto(\theta',p')$ has triangular Jacobian, because $\theta'$ is independent of $p$, while $\partial p'/\partial p=1/q$. Its absolute determinant is $1/(Dq^3)$. The unoriented coordinate seams change both normal and offset signs and do not affect this absolute measure identity.
(b) The invertible map $T$ preserves the intersection parameter count: $n(Tc\cap T\ell)=n(c\cap\ell)$. Apply ordinary Crofton to $Tc$ and change line coordinates using (a), obtaining the formula.
(c) For a rotation, $D=q=1$ and the formula is unchanged. For $T=\lambda I$, $\lambda>0$, $D=\lambda^2$ and $q=1/\lambda$, giving weight $\lambda$, as length scaling requires. For $T=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)$, $q^2=\cos^2\theta/a^2+\sin^2\theta/b^2$. The unit circle has two crossings for $|p|<1$, so its transformed perimeter is
\[
\frac2{ab}\int_0^\pi
\left(\frac{\cos^2\theta}{a^2}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{b^2}\right)^{-3/2}d\theta.
\]
This is an exact integral for the ellipse, equal to its direct parametric length integral. At $a=b=\lambda$ it reduces to $2\pi\lambda$, providing a normalization check.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch11-spherical-cap}
\idea Separate crossing the latitude boundary from reaching any point of the cap.
\writeup (a) The projection of $n$ into $u^\perp$ has length $\sqrt{1-(u\cdot n)^2}$. On the corresponding great circle, $x\cdot n$ ranges over the negative and positive of this value. It takes the value $\cos a$ exactly when
$|\cos a|\le\sqrt{1-(u\cdot n)^2}$, equivalently $|u\cdot n|\le\sin a$. The dot product is uniform on $[-1,1]$, so this band has probability $\sin a$. Strict inequality gives two intersections; equality gives null tangencies, except for coincident equators at $a=\pi/2$, also a null pole pair. The mean count is $2\sin a$.
(b) Hitting the cap only requires the maximum $\sqrt{1-(u\cdot n)^2}$ to be at least $\cos a$. For $a\le\pi/2$ this has the same probability $\sin a$. For $a>\pi/2$, the right side is negative, so every great circle meets the cap and the probability is $1$.
(c) The latitude circle has length $2\pi\sin a$, giving Crofton mean $L/\pi=2\sin a$. As $a\to0$, both the cap-hitting probability and boundary mean tend to zero. As $a\to\pi$, the cap-hitting probability stays $1$ while the boundary mean tends to zero. Many great circles are then entirely inside the cap, producing a hit with no boundary crossing. This is why the planar convex-body rule of exactly two boundary crossings cannot be transplanted to large spherical caps.
\end{solution}
