\chapter{The Riemann Mapping Theorem}\label{ch:riemannmap}

\begin{center}\itshape
Can a square be mapped onto a disc without changing a single angle?\\
The disc, a square, the inside of a snowflake, the slit plane:\\
to a holomorphic bijection they are all the same.
\end{center}

The map $z\mapsto(z-i)/(z+i)$ takes the upper half-plane onto the unit disc.
It bends a straight boundary into a circle, yet preserves angles inside.
Could a holomorphic map do the same for the inside of a square? What about a
region whose boundary has no straight pieces at all?

Riemann's mapping theorem gives an answer without asking for a formula.
We choose a point $z_0$ in the region and look at injective holomorphic maps
into the disc that send $z_0$ to $0$. Among them, we seek the map with the
largest $|f'(z_0)|$. The derivative measures the local change of scale.
A map that leaves a point of the disc uncovered can be made to stretch more.
So a maximiser must cover the whole disc.

There are two things to justify before that argument can work. There must be
at least one map to start with, and a sequence of better maps must have a
limit that is still injective. Square roots solve the first problem;
compactness of bounded holomorphic functions solves the second.
The hypothesis we use is that every zero-free holomorphic function on the
region has a holomorphic square root. Volume~8, Corollary~14.3, supplies this
hypothesis for homologically simply connected regions.

In his 1851 thesis, Riemann argued by minimising an energy. His use of the
Dirichlet principle left an existence question unanswered: Weierstrass showed
in 1870 that an infimum need not be attained in a problem of this kind.
Osgood gave a complete proof of the mapping theorem in 1900. The proof here
uses Koebe's square-root construction and an extremal argument of Fej\'er
and Frigyes Riesz, published by Tibor Rad\'o in 1923.

\section{The half-plane, written down}

Write $D=\{w\in\C:|w|<1\}$ and $\mathbb H=\{z:\operatorname{Im}z>0\}$.
For $z\in\mathbb H$, the point $i$ is nearer than $-i$. Taking the ratio
of these two distances suggests the required map.

\begin{lemma}[the Cayley map]\label{lem:cayley}
The function $\varphi(z)=(z-i)/(z+i)$ maps $\mathbb H$ holomorphically and
bijectively onto $D$. Its inverse is the holomorphic function
$w\mapsto i(1+w)/(1-w)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The denominator $z+i$ does not vanish in $\mathbb H$. Moreover,
\[
 |z+i|^2-|z-i|^2=4\operatorname{Im}z>0,
\]
so $|\varphi(z)|<1$. Solving $w=(z-i)/(z+i)$ for $z$ gives
$z=i(1+w)/(1-w)$. For $|w|<1$, its denominator is nonzero and
\[
 \operatorname{Im}\left(i\frac{1+w}{1-w}\right)
 =\frac{1-|w|^2}{|1-w|^2}>0.
\]
Thus the proposed inverse takes $D$ into $\mathbb H$. Substitution in both
orders gives the identity. Both expressions are rational functions with no
pole in their stated domains, so both are holomorphic.
\end{proof}

A fractional linear map takes circles and lines to circles and lines.
A square therefore needs a different construction. One example is
\[
 f(z)=\int_0^z(1+w^4)^{-1/2}\,\dd w,
\]
where the square root is the branch equal to $1$ at $0$.
The integrand is holomorphic in $D$, so the integral is independent of the
path there. The Schwarz--Christoffel formula says that its image is a square.
This description of the image is a quoted example; it is not a step in our
proof of the mapping theorem. Figure~\ref{fig:squaremap} shows the image of
a grid of circles and radii.

For a general region, a formula may be hard to find. We can instead take
limits of maps. The next theorem says that local bounds on holomorphic
functions give enough control to extract such a limit.

\begin{theorem}[Montel, 1907]\label{thm:montel}
Let $\Omega\subset\C$ be open. Suppose that $(f_n)$ is a sequence of
holomorphic functions on $\Omega$ and that, for each compact $K\subset\Omega$,
there is a constant $M_K$ with $|f_n(z)|\le M_K$ for every $n$ and $z\in K$.
Then some subsequence converges uniformly on every compact subset of
$\Omega$. Its limit is holomorphic on $\Omega$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $\Omega$ is empty, there is nothing to check. Otherwise, use the compact sets
\[
 K_j=\{z\in\Omega:|z|\le j,\ d(z,\C\setminus\Omega)\ge1/j\},
 \qquad j=1,2,\ldots,
\]
with $d(z,\emptyset)=\infty$. They increase to $\Omega$. Every compact
subset of $\Omega$ lies in some $K_j$: it is bounded and stays a positive
distance from the complement.

Fix $j$. If $z\in K_j$, the closed disc of radius $1/(2j)$ about $z$ lies
in $K_{2j}$. Let $M_j$ bound all $|f_n|$ on $K_{2j}$.
For $z,z'\in K_j$ with $|z-z'|<1/(4j)$, every point $\zeta$ of the
segment $[z,z']$ has its closed disc of radius $1/(4j)$ inside $K_{2j}$.
Cauchy's estimate (Volume~5, Corollary~9.12) gives
$|f_n'(\zeta)|\le4jM_j$. Integrating along that segment gives
\[
 |f_n(z)-f_n(z')|\le4jM_j|z-z'|.
\]
This bound is independent of $n$. The functions are therefore uniformly
bounded and equicontinuous on $K_j$. Arzel\`a--Ascoli (Volume~7,
Theorem~4.15) gives a subsequence converging uniformly there. Empty $K_j$
place no restriction on the subsequence.

First extract for $K_1$, then extract from that subsequence for $K_2$, and
continue. The diagonal subsequence converges uniformly on each $K_j$,
hence on every compact subset of $\Omega$. A locally uniform limit of
holomorphic functions is holomorphic (Volume~5, Theorem~9.15).
\end{proof}

A limit of injective functions need not be injective: $f_n(z)=z/n$ tends
to a constant. For holomorphic functions on a connected region, that is the
only way injectivity can be lost.

\begin{lemma}[injective holomorphic maps]\label{lem:injective}
If $f$ is holomorphic and injective on an open set $\Omega$, then
$f'(z)\ne0$ at every $z\in\Omega$, the image $f(\Omega)$ is open, and the
inverse $f^{-1}:f(\Omega)\to\Omega$ is holomorphic.
If $\Omega$ is connected and injective holomorphic functions $f_n$ on
$\Omega$ converge uniformly on compact sets to $f$, then $f$ is either
injective or constant.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $z_0\in\Omega$ and put $w_0=f(z_0)$. The zero of $f-w_0$ at $z_0$
has some finite order $k\ge1$, since injectivity prevents $f$ from being
constant near $z_0$. Choose a small closed disc $\overline D(z_0,r)$ inside
$\Omega$ on which $f-w_0$ has no other zero and $f'$ has no zero except
possibly $z_0$. This is possible because zeros of a nonzero holomorphic
function are isolated (Volume~5, Theorem~9.18).
Set
\[
 \mu=\min_{|z-z_0|=r}|f(z)-w_0|>0.
\]
Whenever $|w-w_0|<\mu$, Rouch\'e's theorem (Volume~5, Theorem~9.38)
says that $f-w$ has $k$ zeros in the disc, counted with multiplicity.
For $w\ne w_0$, none is $z_0$ and each is simple, by our choice of disc.
Injectivity allows only one such zero. Thus $k=1$ and $f'(z_0)\ne0$.

The same argument shows that $D(w_0,\mu)\subset f(D(z_0,r))$, so the
image is open. We can take $r$ arbitrarily small; injectivity then shows
that $f^{-1}(w)\to z_0$ as $w\to w_0$. Writing $z=f^{-1}(w)$ now gives
\[
 \frac{f^{-1}(w)-f^{-1}(w_0)}{w-w_0}
 =\frac{z-z_0}{f(z)-f(z_0)}\longrightarrow\frac1{f'(z_0)}.
\]
The inverse is holomorphic.

For the limit assertion, suppose $f$ is not constant and
$f(z_1)=f(z_2)=w$ at two distinct points. Connectedness and the identity
theorem let us choose disjoint small closed discs about $z_1,z_2$ whose
boundary circles contain no zero of $f-w$. On those circles, $|f-w|$
has a positive minimum. For large $n$, uniform convergence makes
$|f_n-f|<|f-w|$ on both circles. Rouch\'e's theorem puts a zero of
$f_n-w$ in each disc, contrary to injectivity. Thus $f$ is injective.
\end{proof}

We also need a bound on stretching inside the disc. A map that fixes its
centre cannot stretch more than a rotation does.

\begin{lemma}[Schwarz; disc automorphisms]\label{lem:schwarz}
Let $D$ be the open unit disc.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $f:D\to D$ is holomorphic and $f(0)=0$, then
$|f(z)|\le|z|$ for every $z\in D$ and $|f'(0)|\le1$.
If $|f'(0)|=1$, or if $|f(z)|=|z|$ at any nonzero $z\in D$, then
$f(z)=cz$ for a constant $c$ with $|c|=1$.
\item[(b)] For each $a\in D$, the function
$\varphi_a(z)=(z-a)/(1-\overline a z)$ is a holomorphic bijection of
$D$ onto itself. It sends $a$ to $0$ and has inverse $\varphi_{-a}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) Dividing by the known zero gives a holomorphic function
$g(z)=f(z)/z$, extended at $0$ by $g(0)=f'(0)$.
On $|z|=r<1$, we have $|g(z)|\le1/r$. The maximum principle
(Volume~5, Theorem~9.19) gives the same bound inside that circle.
For each fixed $z\in D$, let $r$ increase to $1$. We obtain $|g(z)|\le1$.
If equality holds anywhere, the maximum principle makes $g$ constant,
with modulus $1$. This proves both inequalities and both equality cases.

(b) The denominator cannot vanish in $D$, and
\[
 |1-\overline a z|^2-|z-a|^2=(1-|a|^2)(1-|z|^2)>0.
\]
Thus $\varphi_a(D)\subset D$. Direct substitution gives
\[
 \varphi_{-a}(\varphi_a(z))
 =\frac{(z-a)+a(1-\overline a z)}
 {(1-\overline a z)+\overline a(z-a)}=z.
\]
Replacing $a$ by $-a$ proves the other composition identity.
Both functions are holomorphic on $D$, so they are the required inverses.
\end{proof}

Moving a point to $0$ lets us use Schwarz's lemma at any chosen point.
For example, conjugating by $\varphi_a$ turns a fixed point at $a$ into
one at $0$. Exercise~\ref{ex:schwarzfixed} uses this to show that a
holomorphic self-map with two distinct fixed points inside $D$ is the identity.

\begin{theorem}[Riemann mapping theorem]\label{thm:riemannmap}
Let $\Omega\subset\C$ be a connected open set different from $\C$, and
suppose that every zero-free holomorphic function on $\Omega$ has a
holomorphic square root. This hypothesis holds if $\Omega$ is
homologically simply connected, by Volume~8, Corollary~14.3.
For each $z_0\in\Omega$, there is exactly one holomorphic bijection
$F:\Omega\to D$ such that $F(z_0)=0$ and $F'(z_0)$ is a positive real
number. Its inverse is holomorphic.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal F$ consist of the injective holomorphic maps
$f:\Omega\to D$ with $f(z_0)=0$.

\emph{Find a first map.}
Choose $a\in\C\setminus\Omega$. The function $z-a$ has no zero in
$\Omega$, so there is a holomorphic $q$ with $q(z)^2=z-a$.
Equal values of $q$ have equal squares and hence equal arguments; thus $q$
is injective. Also $q(\Omega)$ and $-q(\Omega)$ are disjoint: an equality
$q(z)=-q(w)$ would give $z=w$ and then $q(z)=0$, forcing $z=a$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:injective}, $q(\Omega)$ is open. Choose a disc
$D(c,r)\subset q(\Omega)$. The reflected disc $D(-c,r)$ is disjoint
from $q(\Omega)$, so $|q(z)+c|\ge r$ on $\Omega$. Consequently
\[
 f_0(z)=\frac r{q(z)+c}
\]
is holomorphic, injective and bounded by $1$. Its image is open, so in
fact $|f_0(z)|<1$ everywhere. Composing with
$\varphi_{f_0(z_0)}$ gives an element of $\mathcal F$.
Figure~\ref{fig:v9-root-separation} shows why the reflected disc is missing.

\emph{Attain the largest derivative.}
Choose $\delta>0$ with $\overline D(z_0,\delta)\subset\Omega$.
Cauchy's estimate gives $|f'(z_0)|\le1/\delta$ for all $f\in\mathcal F$.
Thus
\[
 s=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f'(z_0)|
\]
is finite. It is positive because $\mathcal F$ is nonempty and its maps
have nonzero derivative. Choose $f_n\in\mathcal F$ with
$|f_n'(z_0)|\to s$. Montel's theorem gives a locally uniformly convergent
subsequence, with holomorphic limit $F$. Derivatives also converge locally
uniformly (Volume~5, Theorem~9.15), so $|F'(z_0)|=s>0$.
The limit is therefore nonconstant, and Lemma~\ref{lem:injective} makes
it injective. We have $F(z_0)=0$ and $|F|\le1$; openness of its image
rules out values on $|w|=1$. Hence $F\in\mathcal F$ and it attains $s$.

\emph{Rule out a missing point.}
Suppose $w\in D\setminus F(\Omega)$. Since
$\varphi_w\circ F$ is zero-free, it has a holomorphic square root $g$.
The equation $g^2=\varphi_w\circ F$ shows both that $g$ is injective
and that $|g|<1$. Put $b=g(z_0)$ and $h=\varphi_b\circ g$.
Then $h\in\mathcal F$. Undoing these operations gives
\[
 F=\Psi\circ h,\qquad
 \Psi(\zeta)=\varphi_{-w}\bigl(\varphi_{-b}(\zeta)^2\bigr).
\]
The function $\Psi$ maps $D$ into $D$ and fixes $0$.
It is not injective: $\varphi_{-b}$ maps onto $D$, squaring identifies
opposite nonzero points, and $\varphi_{-w}$ does not undo that
identification. Thus $\Psi$ is not a rotation. The strict case of
Schwarz's lemma gives $|\Psi'(0)|<1$. By the chain rule,
\[
 |F'(z_0)|=|\Psi'(0)|\,|h'(z_0)|<|h'(z_0)|,
\]
contradicting the choice of $F$. No point is missing, so $F$ is onto.
Figure~\ref{fig:v9-extremal-map} keeps track of these compositions.

\emph{Fix the direction and prove uniqueness.}
Multiply $F$ by the unit complex number
$\overline{F'(z_0)}/|F'(z_0)|$. Its derivative at $z_0$ is now positive
real. Lemma~\ref{lem:injective} supplies the holomorphic inverse.
If $G$ has the same normalisation, then $\psi=F\circ G^{-1}$ is an
automorphism of $D$ fixing $0$. Applying Schwarz's lemma to $\psi$ and
$\psi^{-1}$ gives $|\psi'(0)|=1$, so $\psi(z)=cz$ with $|c|=1$.
But $c=F'(z_0)/G'(z_0)>0$, hence $c=1$ and $F=G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:jordanconformal}
The interior of every Jordan curve admits a holomorphic bijection onto $D$
whose inverse is holomorphic.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The Jordan curve theorem (Volume~7, Theorem~6.10) gives a bounded connected
open interior. Volume~8, Proposition~14.4, shows that this interior is
homologically simply connected. It therefore satisfies the square-root
hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:riemannmap}, which supplies the map.
\end{proof}

\end{think}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A square and a disc have different boundaries, but their interiors admit a
holomorphic bijection. The theorem makes no claim here about extending that
map to the boundary. Inside, its derivative never vanishes, so small angles
are preserved. Specifying the image of $z_0$ removes the freedom to move the
centre; asking for a positive derivative removes the freedom to rotate.

The proof also explains why boundedness mattered. Cauchy's estimates changed
bounds on values into bounds on derivatives. Those bounds gave a convergent
subsequence. A nonzero limiting derivative kept its limit from collapsing to
a constant. Finally, a square root turned any missing point into a strict
improvement, which maximality forbade. The whole plane is excluded for a
reason: Liouville's theorem makes every holomorphic map $\C\to D$ constant.
Exercise~\ref{ex:v9-punctured-disc} gives a different obstruction, a hole.

Chapter~11 asks how to measure curves by counting crossings with lines.
There, too, symmetry determines a normalisation. The question is which
measure on lines stays unchanged when the plane is translated or rotated.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:riemannmaps}
\emph{Maps from three regions.} Give a holomorphic bijection onto $D$ from
(a) $\mathbb H$; (b) the strip $|\operatorname{Im}z|<\pi/2$;
(c) the slit plane $\C\setminus(-\infty,0]$.
In each case, explain why every point of $D$ is reached exactly once.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:quadrant}
\emph{Opening a right angle.} Find a holomorphic bijection from
$Q=\{z:\operatorname{Re}z>0,\ \operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ onto $D$.
Check that its formula has no pole in $Q$ and describe the inverse.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:schwarzfixed}
\emph{Two fixed points.} Suppose $f:D\to D$ is holomorphic.
(a) Show that $f$ is the identity if it fixes $0$ and a nonzero point $a$.
(b) Deduce the same conclusion if $f$ fixes any two distinct points of $D$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-schwarz-pick}
\emph{Stretching away from the centre.} Let $f:D\to D$ be holomorphic.
Prove, for $a,z\in D$, that
\[
 \left|\frac{f(z)-f(a)}{1-\overline{f(a)}f(z)}\right|
 \le\left|\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}\right|,
 \qquad
 \frac{|f'(a)|}{1-|f(a)|^2}\le\frac1{1-|a|^2}.
\]
Show that equality in the first inequality for some $z\ne a$, or in the
second at any $a$, holds exactly when $f$ is an automorphism of $D$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-conformal-radius}
\emph{How an inclusion changes scale.} For a region $U$ satisfying
Theorem~\ref{thm:riemannmap} and $a\in U$, define its conformal radius at $a$
by $r_U(a)=1/F_U'(a)$, where $F_U:U\to D$ is the normalised map at $a$.
Suppose $U\subset V$ and both regions satisfy that theorem. Prove
$r_U(a)\le r_V(a)$, with equality if and only if $U=V$.
Compute $r_{D(c,R)}(a)$ when $|a-c|<R$, and deduce bounds for $r_U(a)$
from $D(a,r)\subset U\subset D(a,R)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-punctured-disc}
\emph{A hole that no bijection removes.} Put $D^*=D\setminus\{0\}$.
Show that no holomorphic bijection $f:D^*\to D$ exists.
Use the removable singularity theorem and Schwarz's lemma; do not assume
that a hypothetical inverse extends to the boundary.
Also prove directly that $z$ has no holomorphic square root on $D^*$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-derivative-interpolation}
\emph{A prescribed value and derivative.} Fix $a,b\in D$ and $\lambda\in\C$.
Determine exactly when a holomorphic map $f:D\to D$ can satisfy
$f(a)=b$ and $f'(a)=\lambda$. Construct such a map whenever it exists.
Which choices force the map to be unique?
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:riemannmaps}
\idea Turn each region into a half-plane before trying to make a disc.
\writeup (a) Lemma~\ref{lem:cayley} gives $(z-i)/(z+i)$, with inverse
$i(1+w)/(1-w)$. (b) On the strip, $e^{x+iy}$ has modulus $e^x>0$ and
argument $y\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Modulus and argument show that $e^z$
is a bijection onto the right half-plane, with holomorphic inverse given
by the logarithm whose argument lies in that interval. Multiplication by
$i$ gives $\mathbb H$, so the composite is
\[
 \frac{ie^z-i}{ie^z+i}=\frac{e^z-1}{e^z+1}=\tanh(z/2).
\]
Every component map has a holomorphic inverse, proving bijectivity.
(c) Write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r>0$ and $-\pi<\theta<\pi$.
The principal root $\sqrt r e^{i\theta/2}$ maps the slit plane bijectively
onto the right half-plane; its inverse is squaring there.
Thus $(\sqrt z-1)/(\sqrt z+1)$ is the desired map. Its inverse is
$w\mapsto((1+w)/(1-w))^2$, which takes $D$ into the slit plane.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:quadrant}
\idea Squaring doubles an argument between $0$ and $\pi/2$ into one between
$0$ and $\pi$.
\writeup The map $z=re^{i\theta}\mapsto r^2e^{2i\theta}$ takes $Q$
bijectively onto $\mathbb H$. Its inverse is the holomorphic square root
on $\mathbb H$ with argument in $(0,\pi/2)$.
Composing gives $f(z)=(z^2-i)/(z^2+i)$. Since $z^2\in\mathbb H$,
$z^2$ cannot equal $-i$, so there is no pole in $Q$.
The inverse is $w\mapsto\sqrt{i(1+w)/(1-w)}$, with the indicated root.
Each inverse step stays in its required domain, so $f$ is onto and injective.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:schwarzfixed}
\idea A second fixed point forces equality in Schwarz's bound.
\writeup (a) Since $f(0)=0$ and $|f(a)|=|a|$ at $a\ne0$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:schwarz}(a) gives $f(z)=cz$, $|c|=1$.
The equality $ca=a$ forces $c=1$.
(b) Let $g=\varphi_a\circ f\circ\varphi_{-a}$.
It maps $D$ into $D$ and fixes $0$ and $\varphi_a(b)$.
The latter is nonzero because $b\ne a$. Part (a) gives $g=\mathrm{id}$.
Composing with the inverse automorphisms yields $f=\mathrm{id}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-schwarz-pick}
\idea Move the two chosen values to the origin, where Schwarz's lemma
already supplies the estimate and its equality cases.
\writeup Set $g=\varphi_{f(a)}\circ f\circ\varphi_{-a}$.
Then $g:D\to D$ and $g(0)=0$. Apply $|g(\zeta)|\le|\zeta|$
at $\zeta=\varphi_a(z)$ to obtain the first inequality.
Differentiating the formula for $\varphi_c$ gives
\[
 \varphi_c'(c)=\frac1{1-|c|^2},\qquad
 \varphi_{-c}'(0)=1-|c|^2.
\]
Thus $g'(0)=f'(a)(1-|a|^2)/(1-|f(a)|^2)$, and
$|g'(0)|\le1$ gives the second inequality.
Either specified equality makes $g$ a rotation by Schwarz's lemma.
Therefore $f=\varphi_{-f(a)}\circ g\circ\varphi_a$ is an automorphism.
Conversely, an automorphism makes $g$ an automorphism fixing $0$;
Schwarz's lemma applied also to $g^{-1}$ makes $g$ a rotation.
Both inequalities are then equalities everywhere they are stated.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-conformal-radius}
\idea Compare the two normalised maps on their common disc coordinate.
\writeup The holomorphic map $g=F_V\circ F_U^{-1}:D\to D$ fixes $0$.
Its derivative is
$g'(0)=F_V'(a)/F_U'(a)=r_U(a)/r_V(a)>0$.
Schwarz's lemma gives $r_U(a)\le r_V(a)$.
If equality holds, $g$ is a rotation with positive derivative, hence the
identity. It follows that $F_V(U)=D=F_V(V)$; injectivity of $F_V$ gives
$U=V$. The converse follows from uniqueness of the normalised map.

For $U=D(c,R)$, put $u=(a-c)/R$. The map
$F(z)=\varphi_u((z-c)/R)$ sends $a$ to $0$ and has derivative
$1/(R(1-|u|^2))>0$ there. Hence
\[
 r_{D(c,R)}(a)=R(1-|u|^2)=\frac{R^2-|a-c|^2}{R}.
\]
In particular the inclusions centred at $a$ give
$r\le r_U(a)\le R$. Equality at either end forces $U$ to be the
corresponding round disc, by the equality case just proved.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-punctured-disc}
\idea Boundedness fills the missing point analytically. A bijection would
then have nowhere to send that point.
\writeup Suppose $f:D^*\to D$ were a holomorphic bijection.
The removable singularity theorem (Volume~5) extends it to a holomorphic
$\widetilde f:D\to\C$ with $|\widetilde f|\le1$.
The extension is nonconstant, so the maximum principle gives
$\widetilde f(D)\subset D$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:injective}, the inverse
$f^{-1}:D\to D^*$ is holomorphic. Therefore
$h=f^{-1}\circ\widetilde f$ is a holomorphic map $D\to D$ fixing every
point of $D^*$. It is the identity by Exercise~\ref{ex:schwarzfixed}.
But $h(0)$ belongs to $D^*$ and so cannot be $0$, a contradiction.

If $q^2=z$ on $D^*$, then $q$ has no zero and $2q'/q=1/z$.
Integrating around $|z|=1/2$ and dividing by $2\pi i$ would give
$2\,\operatorname{wind}(q\circ\gamma,0)=1$.
The winding number is an integer (Volume~5), so this is impossible.
Thus the square-root hypothesis fails on $D^*$ itself.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-derivative-interpolation}
\idea Normalising the two values turns the derivative requirement into
Schwarz's bound at $0$.
\writeup If such an $f$ exists, then
$g=\varphi_b\circ f\circ\varphi_{-a}$ fixes $0$ and has derivative
$\mu=\lambda(1-|a|^2)/(1-|b|^2)$. Necessarily $|\mu|\le1$.
Conversely, if $|\mu|\le1$, the map $g(\zeta)=\mu\zeta$ takes $D$
into $D$. The function
\[
 f=\varphi_{-b}\circ g\circ\varphi_a
\]
has $f(a)=b$ and, by the chain rule, $f'(a)=\lambda$.
Thus existence is equivalent to
$|\lambda|\le(1-|b|^2)/(1-|a|^2)$.

If $|\mu|=1$, the equality case of Schwarz's lemma forces
$g(\zeta)=\mu\zeta$, so the displayed $f$ is unique.
If $|\mu|<1$, choose any $c$ with $0<|c|<1-|\mu|$.
Both $g_0(\zeta)=\mu\zeta$ and
$g_c(\zeta)=\mu\zeta+c\zeta^2$ map $D$ into $D$:
$|g_c(\zeta)|\le(|\mu|+|c|)|\zeta|<1$.
They have the same value and derivative at $0$ and are distinct.
Conjugating back gives distinct admissible $f$. These cases include
$\lambda=0$, so there is no further uniqueness case.
\end{solution}
