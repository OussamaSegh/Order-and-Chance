\chapter{Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem}

\begin{center}\itshape
Integrating a derivative over a region can turn into an integral over its boundary. In one dimension this is the fundamental theorem of calculus. Differential forms give the same rule in every dimension, with signs that remember the orientation of the region and its boundary.
\end{center}

On 2 July 1850, William Thomson wrote to George Gabriel Stokes with a result in a postscript: a field's circulation around a surface boundary equals its curl flux through that surface. In 1854 Stokes set it as a Smith's Prize examination question at Cambridge. Maxwell, then twenty-two, was among the candidates and shared the prize with Routh. The result now bears Stokes' name. Katz's historical account and the surviving letter and examination paper are listed in the sources.

The modern statement uses a different language. Cartan's 1899 work developed the exterior calculus: products satisfy $\dd x\wedge\dd y=-\dd y\wedge\dd x$, and the exterior derivative $\dd$ combines the gradient, curl and divergence. Stokes' theorem becomes $\int_M\dd\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega$. The algebra fixes the signs; a local calculation and partitions of unity prove the formula.

Forms also detect an obstruction to being a derivative. On a punctured plane, a form can have zero exterior derivative and still integrate to $2\pi$ around the missing point. De Rham's 1931 work put this connection with topology into a general theorem. We shall prove the punctured-plane case here. Before that, Stokes will rule out a smooth retraction of a ball onto its sphere and give Brouwer's fixed point theorem in every dimension.

\section{Alternating forms}

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional real vector space. A \emph{$k$-form} is a multilinear map $V^k\to\R$ that changes sign when two arguments are exchanged. Equivalently it vanishes whenever two arguments coincide. Write $\Lambda^kV^*$ for the vector space of these maps. A $1$-form is a linear functional; a $0$-form is a scalar. The determinant is the model for a top-degree form: it measures signed volume, changing sign when the orientation of the input vectors reverses.

Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ and its dual $e^1,\ldots,e^n$. For an index sequence $I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$, define
\[
e^I(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\det(e^{i_a}(v_b))_{a,b=1}^k.
\]
A repeated index makes it zero, and permuting indices multiplies it by the permutation's sign. At $k=0$ the determinant and empty index product are both $1$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:altbasis}
The $e^I$ for increasing index sequences form a basis of $\Lambda^kV^*$, and
\[
\alpha=\sum_{I\text{ increasing}}\alpha(e_{i_1},\ldots,e_{i_k})e^I.
\]
Its dimension is $\binom nk$; for $k>n$ the space is zero.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Evaluating $e^I$ on an increasing basis tuple $e_J$ gives $1$ for $I=J$ and $0$ otherwise. A multilinear alternating map is determined by these tuples, since all others either repeat a basis vector or differ by a permutation. The displayed expression therefore agrees with $\alpha$ on every tuple and hence everywhere. The same evaluations prove linear independence. For $k>n$ every tuple repeats an index, and for $k=0$ the sole basis element is the scalar $1$, so both edge cases fit.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{exterior product} is the bilinear map $\wedge:\Lambda^kV^*\times\Lambda^lV^*\to\Lambda^{k+l}V^*$ determined by $e^I\wedge e^J=e^{IJ}$, where $IJ$ concatenates the index sequences.
\end{definition}

The sign rule extends this prescription from increasing basis sequences to all sequences. Associativity follows because both bracketings give $e^{IJK}$. Moving $l$ indices past $k$ others takes $kl$ swaps, giving \emph{graded commutativity}:
$\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{kl}\beta\wedge\alpha$. In particular a $1$-form $\lambda$ satisfies $\lambda\wedge\lambda=0$. For a small example, $(2e^1+e^2)\wedge e^2=2e^1\wedge e^2$; the repeated-direction term disappears. Degree-zero exterior multiplication is ordinary scalar multiplication.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:wedgedet}
For linear functionals $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$ and vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$,
\[
(\lambda_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\lambda_k)(v_1,\ldots,v_k)
=\det(\lambda_i(v_j)).
\]
The exterior product is independent of the basis used to construct it. For a linear map $A:W\to V$, its pullback $A^*\alpha(w_1,\ldots,w_k)=\alpha(Aw_1,\ldots,Aw_k)$ satisfies
$A^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=A^*\alpha\wedge A^*\beta$. If $W=V$, then $A^*\omega=(\det A)\omega$ for every $\omega\in\Lambda^nV^*$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\lambda_i=\sum_a\lambda_{i,a}e^a$. Expanding the wedge by multilinearity gives
\[
\sum_{a_1,\ldots,a_k}\left(\prod_i\lambda_{i,a_i}\right)
\det(e^{a_i}(v_j)).
\]
Expanding the claimed determinant by multilinearity in its rows gives exactly this sum. The expression uses only the functionals and vectors. Since every form is a sum of products of functionals, and products of such factors are their concatenated wedges, this proves basis independence for the entire product. Apply the same determinant formula after replacing each $v_j$ by $Aw_j$ to obtain pullback multiplicativity, then extend by bilinearity. Finally evaluate the pullback of $e^1\wedge\cdots\wedge e^n$ on the full basis: it gives $\det A$. The top-form space is one-dimensional, proving the last identity, including dimension zero with empty determinant $1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Differential forms and the exterior derivative}

\begin{definition}
A \emph{differential $k$-form} on $M$ assigns an alternating form on $T_pM$ at each point, with smooth coordinate coefficients:
\[
\omega=\sum_{I\text{ increasing}}a_I\dd x^I,\qquad
\dd x^I=\dd x^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^{i_k}.
\]
The space of these forms is $\Omega^k(M)$. For a smooth map $F:M\to N$, define its \emph{pullback} by
$(F^*\omega)_p(v_1,\ldots,v_k)=\omega_{F(p)}(dF_pv_1,\ldots,dF_pv_k)$.
\end{definition}

A $0$-form is a smooth function. We also set $\Omega^k(M)=0$ for $k<0$, which will make the degree-zero homotopy formula unambiguous. The differential $\dd f$ of a function acts on $v$ as $df(v)$ and equals $\sum_i\partial_i f\,\dd x^i$ in coordinates. This explains why the coordinate covectors are called $\dd x^i$.

Pullback respects wedges by Proposition~\ref{prop:wedgedet}, respects functions by $F^*f=f\circ F$, and respects function differentials by the chain rule. In a chart it is substitution:
\[
F^*(a\,\dd y^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd y^{j_k})
=(a\circ F)\dd F^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd F^{j_k}.
\]
For instance the map $F(s,t)=(2s+t,t)$ gives $F^*(\dd x\wedge\dd y)=(2\dd s+\dd t)\wedge\dd t=2\dd s\wedge\dd t$, its area factor.

\begin{theorem}[exterior derivative]\label{thm:extd}
There is a unique family of linear maps $\dd:\Omega^k(M)\to\Omega^{k+1}(M)$ agreeing with function differentials, satisfying $\dd^2=0$, and obeying
\[
\dd(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\dd\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^k\alpha\wedge\dd\beta
\quad(\alpha\in\Omega^k(M)).
\]
In a chart it is $\dd(\sum_Ia_I\dd x^I)=\sum_I\dd a_I\wedge\dd x^I$, and for every smooth $F$, $F^*\dd\omega=\dd F^*\omega$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On an open subset of $\R^n$, use the displayed coordinate formula as a definition. It is linear and has the required action on functions. For monomials $\alpha=a\dd x^I$ and $\beta=b\dd x^J$, differentiating $ab$ gives two terms. Moving $\dd b$ past the $k$ factors in $\dd x^I$ gives the sign $(-1)^k$, proving the product rule. Linearity handles arbitrary sums.

For a function, $\dd^2f=\sum_{i,j}\partial_j\partial_if\,\dd x^j\wedge\dd x^i=0$: equal mixed partials multiply opposite wedges. Constant-coordinate wedges have derivative zero by the defining formula. Thus $\dd^2(a\dd x^I)=\dd^2a\wedge\dd x^I-\dd a\wedge\dd(\dd x^I)=0$. Conversely, any operator with the claimed rules has $\dd(\dd x^i)=0$ and hence kills their wedges, forcing the same coordinate formula.

For a coordinate monomial, differentiate its pullback expression. The product rule, $\dd^2F^j=0$, and the ordinary chain rule give
\[
\dd F^*\omega=\dd(a\circ F)\wedge\dd F^{j_1}\wedge\cdots
=F^*(\dd a\wedge\dd y^{j_1}\wedge\cdots)=F^*\dd\omega.
\]
Therefore coordinate transitions commute with the local construction, letting it fit into a globally defined operator with all the stated properties.

For global uniqueness, these rules also force locality. If $\omega$ vanishes near $p$, choose a bump $\chi$ supported in that neighborhood and equal to $1$ near $p$. Since $\chi\omega=0$, the product rule gives $\dd\omega(p)=0$. Local coordinate functions and forms may then be multiplied by bumps and extended globally without changing the value near $p$. The Euclidean uniqueness argument applies there, proving global uniqueness as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[grad, curl, div]\label{ex:gradcurldiv}
In $\R^3$, a vector field $F=(P,Q,R)$ gives two different forms,
\[
\omega_F=P\dd x+Q\dd y+R\dd z,\qquad
\eta_F=P\dd y\wedge\dd z+Q\dd z\wedge\dd x+R\dd x\wedge\dd y.
\]
Together with functions and top forms $\rho\dd x\wedge\dd y\wedge\dd z$, these identify the successive exterior derivatives as
$\dd f=\omega_{\nabla f}$, $\dd\omega_F=\eta_{\nabla\times F}$ and $\dd\eta_F=(\nabla\cdot F)\dd x\wedge\dd y\wedge\dd z$. Thus $\dd^2=0$ contains both curl-of-gradient and divergence-of-curl identities. Exercise~\ref{ex:gradcurl} checks each coefficient and its integral theorem.
\end{example}

\section{Orientation and integration}

\begin{definition}
For positive dimension, two charts are \emph{orientation-compatible} if their transitions have positive Jacobian determinant. An \emph{orientation} is a maximal atlas of pairwise compatible charts, and a manifold is \emph{orientable} when it admits one.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:volumeform}
For dimension $n\ge1$, a manifold is orientable exactly when it has a nowhere-vanishing $n$-form. Such a form determines the positive charts by having positive coefficient in $\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A nonvanishing coefficient has constant sign on a connected chart domain. Reflect one coordinate when necessary to make it positive. On an overlap, the determinant pullback formula says that two positive coefficients differ by a positive Jacobian factor, so these charts are compatible. Conversely, use an oriented chart cover and a subordinate partition of unity $\rho_j$. The locally finite sum of the forms $\rho_j\dd x_j^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x_j^n$, extended by zero off their chart supports, is smooth. In every positive chart all summands are nonnegative multiples of its coordinate volume form and at least one is positive. The sum therefore vanishes nowhere.
\end{proof}

The sphere's form below gives an orientation. Nonorientable examples include the M\"obius band and $\R P^2$. For the band, use its strip description $(x,y)\sim(x+1,-y)$. A nonvanishing top form on the band would lift to $a(x,y)\dd x\wedge\dd y$ with $a(x+1,-y)=-a(x,y)$. Along $y=0$, the continuous nonzero coefficient would change sign between $x$ and $x+1$, impossible. Exercise~\ref{ex:mobius} treats projective spaces using their antipodal charts.

For integration in dimension zero, use the signed-point convention: assign each point a sign $\varepsilon_p\in\{1,-1\}$. Integrating a compactly supported $0$-form $f$ means the finite sum $\sum_p\varepsilon_pf(p)$. Compact subsets of a discrete zero-manifold are finite. These signs are needed for endpoints of oriented intervals, rather than being obtained by reflecting an absent coordinate.

\begin{definition}
For an oriented manifold and a compactly supported top-degree form $\omega$, if its support lies in one positive chart, write the chart expression as $a\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n$ and integrate $a$ against Lebesgue measure there. In general use a subordinate partition of unity $\rho_j$ and set $\int_M\omega=\sum_j\int_M\rho_j\omega$. Only finitely many terms meet the support. In dimension zero use the signed-point convention above.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:intwelldef}
The integral is well defined and linear. For a diffeomorphism $F:N\to M$ preserving orientation, $\int_NF^*\omega=\int_M\omega$; for one reversing orientation the sign changes.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On an overlap, if the form has coefficient $b$ in $y$ coordinates, its coefficient in $x$ coordinates is $(b\circ T)\det DT$ for $T=y\circ x^{-1}$. Positive orientation makes the determinant positive, so change of variables from Volume~7, Theorem~9.8 identifies the two one-chart integrals. Given two partitions $\rho_j,\sigma_i$, insert $\sum_i\sigma_i=1$ in each chart integral:
\[
\sum_j\int\rho_j\omega=\sum_{j,i}\int\sigma_i\rho_j\omega
=\sum_i\int\sigma_i\omega.
\]
The sums are finite on the compact support and each product lies in both relevant chart domains, so the overlap calculation applies. This proves independence and linearity. Pullback by $F$ is the same coordinate change with a positive or negative Jacobian; the absolute Jacobian in measure change leaves exactly its orientation sign. For zero-manifolds this is the corresponding bijection of signed finite sums.
\end{proof}

A $k$-form is integrated on an oriented $k$-dimensional submanifold by pulling it back to that submanifold. For a parametrized curve the rule is
$\int_\gamma\omega=\int_a^b\omega_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma'(t))dt$.
For $\omega=\dd f$ this is $f(\gamma(b))-f(\gamma(a))$, the ordinary fundamental theorem of calculus.

\section{Stokes' theorem}

\begin{definition}
For $n\ge1$, a \emph{manifold with boundary} has charts in relatively open subsets of a closed half-space, $H_-^n=\{x_1\le0\}$ or $H_+^n=\{x_1\ge0\}$. Smoothness means local extension to an open subset of $\R^n$. The \emph{boundary} consists of points with $x_1=0$ in such a chart. Allowing either half-space gives the same smooth structures, since reflection interchanges them; it also lets positive interval coordinates describe both endpoints. A zero-dimensional manifold has empty boundary.
\end{definition}

An interior point cannot become a boundary point under a chart transition. Otherwise the inverse function theorem would map a small open Euclidean neighborhood onto an open set containing a boundary point but lying in a half-space, impossible. Applying this to the inverse gives the converse, so the boundary is intrinsic. Restricting boundary charts gives a smooth $(n-1)$-manifold without boundary. The closed ball is a manifold with boundary its sphere; a planar region with disjoint smooth boundary curves is another example.

Partitions of unity also work up to the boundary. At a point in a half-space chart, restrict an ambient Euclidean bump supported in a small ball whose intersection with the half-space lies in the chart. The locally finite refinement and normalization argument of Chapter~1 then gives smooth relative chart bumps and a subordinate partition, including boundary points. This is the version used for integration and Stokes below.

\begin{proposition}[boundary orientation]\label{prop:bdryorient}
For an oriented $n$-manifold with boundary, $n\ge2$, normalize positive boundary charts to $H_-^n$. Their restricted coordinates $(x^2,\ldots,x^n)$ are orientation-compatible and give the induced boundary orientation: an outward transverse vector followed by a positive boundary basis is a positive ambient basis.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A positive chart into $H_+^n$ can be changed to $H_-^n$ by negating both $x_1,x_2$, preserving orientation. For transitions $T$ between normalized charts, $T_1(0,x')=0$ implies $\partial_jT_1=0$ for $j\ge2$. Since interior negative values remain negative, $\partial_1T_1\ge0$ on the boundary. The invertible derivative and its triangular block form give
$\det DT=\partial_1T_1\det D'T'>0$. The first factor cannot vanish, so both are positive. Thus the restricted charts agree in orientation. In the normalized half-space the outward direction is $e_1$, making $(e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_n)$ positive; this is precisely the stated rule.
\end{proof}

For a planar region, the outer boundary runs counterclockwise and each hole boundary clockwise, with the region to the left. In dimension one, a positive coordinate into $H_-^1$ gives an ending point of sign $+$, while one into $H_+^1$ gives a starting point of sign $-$. These are the signed zero-dimensional orientations used in the fundamental theorem of calculus. No metric is needed: ``outward'' means pointing out of the half-space, not choosing a particular unit normal.

\begin{theorem}[Stokes]\label{thm:stokes}
Let $M$ be an oriented $n$-manifold with boundary, $n\ge0$, with induced boundary orientation. For every compactly supported $(n-1)$-form $\omega$,
\[
\int_M\dd\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n=0$, the form has degree $-1$ and is zero by convention, and the boundary is empty, so both integrals vanish. Now suppose $n\ge1$. A boundary-compatible partition of unity writes $\omega$ as a finite sum of forms supported in single charts. Both sides are linear in these forms, so it suffices to treat one. For $n\ge2$ use a positive chart normalized to $H_-^n$, pull back the form and extend it by zero outside its compact relative support in that half-space. Write
\[
\omega=\sum_{j=1}^n a_j\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\dd x^j}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n.
\]
The hat omits that factor. Its derivative has coefficient $\sum_j(-1)^{j-1}\partial_ja_j$ in the standard volume form. Fubini and the fundamental theorem of calculus make every $j\ge2$ integral zero: it integrates a compactly supported derivative over the full $x_j$-axis. The remaining term gives
\[
\int_{H_-^n}\partial_1a_1\,dx
=\int_{\R^{n-1}}a_1(0,x')\,dx'.
\]
On the boundary, $\dd x^1$ restricts to zero, so only $a_1\dd x^2\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n$ remains. Its positively oriented integral is exactly the right side above. A chart entirely in the interior has no boundary contribution and gives zero by the same computation.

For $n=1$, the form is a function $a$. A positive $H_-^1$ chart gives $\int_{-\infty}^0a'=a(0)$; a positive $H_+^1$ chart gives $\int_0^\infty a'=-a(0)$. These are exactly the signed endpoint integrals. Interior charts give zero. The same partition argument completes this case, including empty boundaries and zero forms.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The multidimensional cancellation happens before integration. Every derivative in a tangential coordinate integrates to zero. Only the derivative in the outward coordinate survives, leaving the restriction of the original form on the boundary. The exterior algebra supplies the signs, and the partition makes the local computation apply to the whole manifold.
\end{beauty}

\begin{example}[the volume form of the sphere]\label{ex:sigma}
For $n\ge1$, put
\[
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^n(-1)^{i-1}x_i\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\dd x^i}\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n
\]
on $\R^n$. Differentiation gives $\dd\sigma=n\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n$. Hence Stokes on the unit ball gives $\int_{S^{n-1}}\sigma=n\lambda(B^n)>0$. At a sphere point,
$\sigma_x(v_2,\ldots,v_n)=\det(x,v_2,\ldots,v_n)$, so its restriction measures area with the outward sphere orientation and vanishes nowhere. When $n=1$, $S^0=\{-1,1\}$ has boundary signs $-,+$ and $\sigma(x)=x$, giving integral $(-1)(-1)+1\cdot1=2$. For $n=2$ the circle integral of $x\dd y-y\dd x$ is $2\pi$; for $n=3$ the sphere area is $4\pi$.
\end{example}

\subsection*{Brouwer's fixed point theorem in every dimension}

Volume~7, Theorem~6.7 proved the disk case with winding numbers. The sphere's nonzero area integral now gives the higher-dimensional obstruction.

\begin{theorem}[no smooth retraction]\label{thm:noretraction}
For $n\ge1$, no smooth map $r:\bar B^n\to S^{n-1}$ restricts to the identity on $S^{n-1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Restrict $\sigma$ from the preceding example to the sphere. If $r$ existed, the boundary pullback would equal $\sigma$, while Stokes and pullback compatibility would give
\[
0<\int_{S^{n-1}}\sigma
=\int_{S^{n-1}}r^*\sigma
=\int_{\bar B^n}\dd(r^*\sigma)
=\int_{\bar B^n}r^*(\dd\sigma)=0.
\]
The final derivative is taken \emph{on the sphere}, where it has degree $n$ on an $(n-1)$-manifold and is therefore zero. It is not the nonzero ambient derivative on $\R^n$. The contradiction proves the claim, also for $n=1$ with the signed-point convention.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Brouwer]\label{thm:brouwern}
Every continuous map $f:\bar B^n\to\bar B^n$, $n\ge0$, has a fixed point.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n=0$ the ball is a single point, necessarily fixed. For $n\ge1$, suppose instead that no point is fixed. Compactness gives $m=\min_{\bar B^n}|f(x)-x|>0$. Stone--Weierstrass, Volume~7, Theorem~4.19, provides a polynomial vector map $P$ uniformly within $m/4$ of $f$; approximate its finitely many coordinates accurately enough for this vector bound. Let $g=P/(1+m/4)$. Then $|g|<1$ on the ball and
\[
|g-f|\le|P-f|+\frac{m/4}{1+m/4}|P|<m/2.
\]
Thus the smooth map $g$ still has no fixed point.

Continue the ray from $g(x)$ through $x$ to the sphere. With $u=x-g(x)\ne0$, the exit point is $r(x)=x+t(x)u$, where
\[
t(x)=\frac{-\langle x,u\rangle+\sqrt{\langle x,u\rangle^2+|u|^2(1-|x|^2)}}{|u|^2}.
\]
It is the nonnegative root of the equation $|x+tu|^2=1$. For interior $x$ the radicand is positive. On the boundary, $\langle x,u\rangle=1-\langle x,g(x)\rangle>0$, so it is positive there too and the displayed root is zero. Compactness and continuity make the denominator and radicand remain positive on an ambient neighborhood of the ball. Thus $r$ is smooth up to the boundary, maps to the sphere, and equals the identity on it. This contradicts no retraction.
\end{proof}

\section{De Rham cohomology}

A form is \emph{closed} if $\dd\omega=0$ and \emph{exact} if $\omega=\dd\eta$. Exact forms are closed because $\dd^2=0$. For a $1$-form in three-dimensional coordinates, closedness corresponds to zero curl; exactness corresponds to being a gradient. The difference can depend on the domain, as an integral around a hole will show.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{de Rham cohomology} in degree $k$ is
\[
H^k(M)=\frac{\{\text{closed }k\text{-forms}\}}{\{\text{exact }k\text{-forms}\}}.
\]
A smooth map $F:M\to N$ induces $F^*:H^k(N)\to H^k(M)$, since pullback commutes with $\dd$.
\end{definition}

Because negative-degree forms are zero, no nonzero $0$-form is exact. Closed $0$-forms are locally constant functions. Manifold components are open, so, writing $\pi_0(M)$ for the set of connected components, $H^0(M)$ is the product $\prod_{C\in\pi_0(M)}\R$, one freely assigned value on each connected component. When there are finitely many components, its dimension is their number. For infinitely many components it is a product of vector spaces, and the finite dimension formula must not be asserted. For the empty manifold it is the zero vector space.

\begin{theorem}[homotopy formula]\label{thm:homotopy}
For smooth $h:\R\times M\to N$, write $h_t=h(t,\cdot)$. There are linear maps $K:\Omega^k(N)\to\Omega^{k-1}(M)$ satisfying
\[
h_1^*\omega-h_0^*\omega=\dd K\omega+K\dd\omega.
\]
Smoothly homotopic maps therefore induce the same cohomology map.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On $\R\times M$, separate the terms containing $\dd t$:
$h^*\omega=\alpha_t+\dd t\wedge\beta_t$, uniquely, with forms on $M$ depending smoothly on $t$. Define $K\omega=\int_0^1\beta_tdt$, integrating coefficients. Restriction to $t=c$ kills $\dd t$, so $\alpha_c=h_c^*\omega$. In local coordinates,
\[
\dd h^*\omega=\dd_M\alpha_t+\dd t\wedge(\partial_t\alpha_t-\dd_M\beta_t).
\]
Pullback commutes with differentiation. Taking the $\dd t$ coefficient and integrating gives
$K\dd\omega=\alpha_1-\alpha_0-\dd K\omega$, the desired formula. Differentiation under this finite-time integral is valid locally for smooth coefficients. At $k=0$, $\beta_t$ and $K\omega$ are zero and the formula is simply the fundamental theorem in $t$ applied to a pulled-back function. For a closed form the difference is exact, proving the cohomology conclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Poincar\'e lemma]\label{cor:poincare}
Every closed $k$-form of positive degree on a star-shaped open set $U\subset\R^n$ is exact: $H^k(U)=0$ for $k\ge1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For nonempty $U$, translate its star centre to $0$. Choose the smooth function $\beta:\R\to[0,1]$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:bump}, with $\beta(0)=0$, $\beta(1)=1$, and use $h(t,x)=\beta(t)x$. Star-shapedness makes it take values in $U$ for all $t$. Its endpoint maps are the identity and a constant. The constant map's pullback kills positive-degree forms, so the homotopy formula gives $\omega=\dd K\omega$ for closed $\omega$. If $U$ is empty, all its forms are zero and the conclusion is immediate.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{The punctured plane and the winding number}

On $\R^2\setminus\{0\}$, put
\[
\dd\theta=\frac{x\dd y-y\dd x}{x^2+y^2}.
\]
This notation denotes a globally defined $1$-form, not the differential of a globally chosen real angle. Locally an angle can be chosen and has this derivative. Direct differentiation gives $\dd(\dd\theta)=0$, while its unit-circle integral is $2\pi$. An exact form integrates to zero around every closed curve, so this closed form is not exact.

\begin{theorem}[de Rham's theorem for the punctured plane]\label{thm:h1punctured}
A closed $1$-form $\omega$ on the punctured plane is exact precisely when $\int_{S^1}\omega=0$. Its first de Rham cohomology is one-dimensional, spanned by $[\dd\theta]$, and its isomorphism with $\R$ is
$[\omega]\mapsto(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{S^1}\omega$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Exactness implies zero circle integral by the fundamental theorem along the loop. For the converse, lift coordinates by $P(r,\phi)=(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi)$ on the convex domain $(0,\infty)\times\R$. The pulled-back form is closed, hence equals $\dd g$ by Poincar\'e. Its coefficients are periodic in $\phi$. Thus the two derivatives of $g(r,\phi+2\pi)-g(r,\phi)$ vanish, making the difference a constant $c$. At $r=1$, integration of $g_\phi$ shows $c=\int_{S^1}\omega$.

If $c=0$, the function $g$ is periodic and gives a well-defined function $f$ on the punctured plane with $g=f\circ P$. It is smooth on every angle chart because $P$ is locally a diffeomorphism. The identity $P^*\dd f=\dd g=P^*\omega$ implies $\dd f=\omega$, since local inverse charts recover the original forms. For arbitrary $c$, subtract $(c/(2\pi))\dd\theta$ to get zero period and hence an exact form. Since $\dd\theta$ itself has nonzero period, its class gives precisely the claimed one-dimensional quotient.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[the winding number as an integral]\label{cor:windingintegral}
For a closed piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma$ avoiding $a\in\R^2$, its winding number is
\[
n(\gamma,a)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\gamma\dd\theta_a,
\]
where $\dd\theta_a$ is the angle form translated to $a$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Translate $a$ to zero. The argument-lifting lemma from Volume~7, Lemma~6.4 supplies a continuous real argument along the parameter interval. On each short piece this agrees, up to a constant multiple of $2\pi$, with a smooth local angle composed with $\gamma$. Integrating $\dd\theta$ gives that piece's argument increment. Sum the increments to obtain the full change, which by Volume~7, Definition~6.5 is $2\pi n(\gamma,0)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This proves the de Rham identification only for the punctured plane. The general de Rham theorem is quoted: it identifies form cohomology with the dual of real singular homology, which Chapter~6 will introduce. It requires a separate theorem, not just the single-hole calculation here.
\end{remark}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The angle form gives a useful test to keep: it has zero derivative everywhere on its domain, yet its loop integral is not zero. The obstruction is not visible in one coordinate neighborhood; it appears when the neighborhoods go around the missing point. The Poincar\'e lemma explains the local potential, and the period detects why those potentials cannot fit into one global function.

Stokes provides a second use of the same algebra. The sphere's nonzero integral prevents a retraction, and a fixed-point-free map of the ball would construct exactly that forbidden retraction. Following the ray formula shows how a statement about a continuous map reaches a smooth integral contradiction.

Forms have done this without a notion of length. The next chapter adds a metric. Its moving-frame connection produces a $1$-form whose derivative measures curvature, and Stokes will turn that derivative into an index count on a surface.

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:gradcurl}
Verify all three derivative identifications in Example~\ref{ex:gradcurldiv}, including each curl sign. Deduce curl of a gradient and divergence of a curl are zero. Then recover from Stokes:

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:shoelace}

(b) Derive the shoelace formula $\tfrac12\sum_i(x_iy_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)$ for a simple polygon listed counterclockwise, with cyclic indices. Explain its signed-triangle interpretation and justify the passage through polygonal corners rather than applying the smooth boundary theorem there without comment.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:windingcompute}
(a) Differentiate the angle form and verify it is closed.
(b) Find its normalized integral for $\gamma_k(t)=(\cos(2\pi kt),\sin(2\pi kt))$, $0\le t\le1$, for every $k\in\Z$, and for $\gamma(t)=(\cos2t+\tfrac12\cos t,\sin2t+\tfrac12\sin t)$, $0\le t\le2\pi$.
(c) Prove invariance under a smooth homotopy of closed curves avoiding the origin.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:toruscohomology}
On $T^2=\R^2/\Z^2$, use coordinates $\theta_1,\theta_2$ modulo $1$.
(a) Explain why $\dd\theta_1,\dd\theta_2$ are globally defined closed forms and neither is exact.
(b) Prove their classes are linearly independent by integrating on the coordinate circles.
(c) Prove $\dd\theta_1\wedge\dd\theta_2$ is closed and not exact. The exact dimensions $H^1=\R^2,H^2=\R$ are established constructively in Exercise~\ref{ex:v9-ch03-torus-periods}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:mobius}
(a) For the antipodal map $A$ on $S^2$, prove $A^*\sigma=-\sigma$ for the sphere area form.
(b) If a nonvanishing $2$-form existed on $\R P^2$, its pullback would be $f\sigma$ for a nonzero smooth function on $S^2$. Show this would force $f(-x)=-f(x)$ and derive a contradiction.
(c) Prove that $\R P^n$ is orientable for odd $n$, explaining how the invariant sphere form descends using the local quotient charts, not merely asserting descent.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:spherevolume}
For $n\ge1$, let $\omega_n$ be the unit ball volume and $s_{n-1}$ the unit sphere area.
(a) Prove $s_{n-1}=n\omega_n$.
(b) Use the Gaussian integral $\int_{\R^n}e^{-|x|^2}dx=\pi^{n/2}$ and polar coordinates to obtain $s_{n-1}=2\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2)$.
(c) Deduce $\omega_n=\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1)$ and its limit zero. Compare with the volume fraction in $[-1,1]^n$, and identify the dimension where the unit ball volume is largest.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch03-compact-potential}
(a) Show that every compactly supported closed $1$-form on the punctured plane has an ordinary smooth primitive.
(b) Choose a smooth radial function $\chi$ equal to $1$ for $0<r\le1$ and $0$ for $r\ge2$. Show that $\dd\chi$ is compactly supported there but has no compactly supported primitive.
(c) Explain why cutting off the angle form by a radial factor need not preserve closedness: compute $\dd(f(r)\dd\theta)$ and determine when it vanishes.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch03-torus-periods}
(a) Prove $H^1(T^2)=\R^2$ by lifting a closed form to $\R^2$, taking a primitive there and examining its two translation increments.
(b) For a smooth periodic $f$, explicitly construct a periodic primitive of $(f-m)\dd x\wedge\dd y$, where $m=\int_{[0,1]^2}f$. Deduce $H^2(T^2)=\R$.
(c) For an integer matrix $M$, find the induced pullback of coordinate $1$-form classes and of the area class under $F_M(x)=Mx$ modulo $\Z^2$. Compute the example $M=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, including its number of preimages of each point, and compare with a singular matrix.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch03-source-flux}
For $n\ge2$ and $x\ne0$, put $\alpha=|x|^{-n}\sigma$, where $\sigma$ is the ambient form of Example~\ref{ex:sigma}.
(a) Prove $\dd\alpha=0$ away from zero and compute its integral over every outward-oriented sphere centred at zero.
(b) If $D$ is a compact smooth region in $\R^n$ with $0\notin\partial D$, compute $\int_{\partial D}\alpha$ according as zero is inside or outside $D$.
(c) Identify the planar angle form and the three-dimensional inverse-square flux field represented by this calculation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch03-radial-potentials}
On an open set star-shaped about zero, prove that a closed $k$-form, $k\ge1$, has the explicit primitive
\[
\eta_x(v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1})=
\int_0^1 t^{k-1}\omega_{tx}(x,v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1})dt.
\]
Apply this to a curl-free field $F$ in $\R^3$ to obtain a scalar potential, and to a divergence-free field to obtain a vector potential
$A(x)=\int_0^1tF(tx)\times x\,dt$. Check the latter for $F=(0,0,1)$ and compute its circulation around a radius-$R$ horizontal circle.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:gradcurl}
\idea Exterior differentiation gives the familiar coefficient formulas, and one Stokes statement integrates each of them.
\writeup We have $\dd f=f_x\dd x+f_y\dd y+f_z\dd z=\omega_{\nabla f}$. Expanding the three terms of $\omega_F$ gives
\[
\dd\omega_F=(R_y-Q_z)\dd y\wedge\dd z
+(P_z-R_x)\dd z\wedge\dd x+(Q_x-P_y)\dd x\wedge\dd y,
\]
exactly $\eta_{\nabla\times F}$. In $\dd\eta_F$, only each missing-coordinate derivative survives, yielding $(P_x+Q_y+R_z)\dd x\wedge\dd y\wedge\dd z$. Applying $\dd^2=0$ now gives both vector identities.
(a) With $\omega=P\dd x+Q\dd y$ on a plane region, its derivative is $(Q_x-P_y)\dd x\wedge\dd y$; Stokes gives Green with outer counterclockwise and inner clockwise boundaries.
(b) Use $\omega=\eta_F$ on the three-dimensional region. On its outward-oriented boundary, the form is $(F\cdot\nu)\dd S$, so Stokes gives divergence as outward flux.
(c) Restrict $\omega_F$ to the oriented surface and apply Stokes with its induced boundary orientation. Its derivative is the curl flux form computed above. This is the classical circulation--curl identity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:shoelace}
\idea Choose a primitive of the area form whose integral along a segment is a determinant.
\writeup (a) Direct differentiation gives $\dd((x\dd y-y\dd x)/2)=\dd x\wedge\dd y$. Stokes then gives the area integral with the induced boundary signs.
(b) For $\gamma(t)=p+t(q-p)$, the pullback of $x\dd y-y\dd x$ is the constant $p_1q_2-p_2q_1$, so summing the edge integrals gives the stated formula. To justify applying (a) at corners, round the finitely many vertices inside disjoint disks of radius $\delta$, using scaled smooth arcs joining the two incident edges. The region changes only inside these disks, so its area changes by $O(\delta^2)$. The replaced boundary pieces have total length $O(\delta)$ and the $1$-form coefficients stay bounded on a fixed compact neighborhood, so the boundary integral changes by $O(\delta)$. Apply (a) to the rounded regions and let $\delta\to0$.

The contribution $\det(p,q)/2$ is the signed triangle area of $0pq$. Its sign records whether the oriented edge turns counterclockwise or clockwise about zero. Their sum is the polygon area; overlapping positive and negative contributions cancel as the figure illustrates. The smoothing argument establishes this identity even when the origin is outside or the polygon is nonconvex.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:windingcompute}
\idea Factor the curve into its visible rotation and a factor that cannot wind around zero.
\writeup (a) Put $\rho=x^2+y^2$. Differentiating gives
\[
\dd(\dd\theta)=\left(\partial_x\frac x\rho+\partial_y\frac y\rho\right)\dd x\wedge\dd y
=\frac{(\rho-2x^2)+(\rho-2y^2)}{\rho^2}\dd x\wedge\dd y=0.
\]
(b) The first curve pulls the angle form back to $2\pi k\,dt$, so its normalized integral is $k$, including zero and negative traversals. For the second curve, $\gamma=e^{2it}(1+\tfrac12e^{-it})$. The factor in parentheses stays in the disk of radius $1/2$ centred at $1$ and has an argument returning to its starting value in the right half-plane. The first factor contributes $4\pi$, so the normalized integral is $2$.
(c) Apply the homotopy formula to the closed angle form. Its two pullbacks differ by an exact form on the parameter circle, whose integral is zero. Thus the periods, hence winding numbers, agree. This also matches winding homotopy invariance from Volume~7.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:toruscohomology}
\idea Test a form on closed loops, and a top form on the closed surface.
\writeup (a) Translations of lifted coordinates by integers change no differentials, so the two forms descend and are locally differentials, hence closed. On $c_1(t)=(t,0)$ modulo integers, $0\le t\le1$, the first has integral $1$; on $c_2(t)=(0,t)$ the second has integral $1$. An exact form has zero integral on each closed curve, so neither is exact.
(b) Integrating $a\dd\theta_1+b\dd\theta_2$ over the two circles gives respectively $a,b$. If the form were exact both would vanish, proving independence.
(c) The top form is closed by degree. Its integral over the fundamental square is $1$. If it were $\dd\eta$, Stokes on the boundaryless torus would make that integral zero, a contradiction. These tests give lower bounds on dimensions; the later exercise proves they exhaust the classes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:mobius}
\idea A quotient form must agree under the two local choices of sphere representative.
\writeup (a) In ambient dimension three, each term of $\sigma$ has one coordinate and two differential factors. Antipodal pullback negates all three, giving $-\sigma$.
(b) The quotient map $\pi:S^2\to\R P^2$ is locally a diffeomorphism by the charts of Exercise~\ref{ex:rp2}. A nonvanishing pulled-back form must be $f\sigma$, with $f$ smooth and never zero. Since $\pi\circ A=\pi$, invariance gives $(f\circ A)(-\sigma)=f\sigma$ and hence $f(-x)=-f(x)$. On connected $S^2$, a continuous nonzero function has constant sign, contradicting this relation.
(c) In ambient dimension $n+1$ the sign is $(-1)^{n+1}$. For odd $n$, the sphere form is invariant. In each projective quotient chart define a form using one local inverse to $\pi$; the other inverse is its antipode and gives the same result. The chart definitions therefore fit into a smooth nonvanishing top form on $\R P^n$, proving orientability. This includes $\R P^1$, a circle. The identification $\R P^3$ with $SO(3)$ will be developed in Chapter~7 and is not needed here.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:spherevolume}
\idea Separate the Gaussian into its radial integral and its unknown angular area.
\writeup (a) The sphere example and Stokes give $s_{n-1}=n\omega_n$.
(b) For $x=r\xi$, $r>0$, $\xi\in S^{n-1}$, the determinant of the radial map gives volume factor $r^{n-1}dr$ times the sphere area form: the radial vector is $\xi$ and each of the $n-1$ tangent vectors is multiplied by $r$. For $n=1$ the two rays give the same formula with the positive counting area of $S^0$ equal to $2$. Therefore
\[
\pi^{n/2}=s_{n-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{n-1}dr
=\frac{s_{n-1}}2\Gamma(n/2).
\]
This proves the sphere formula.
(c) Divide by $n$ and use $\Gamma(n/2+1)=(n/2)\Gamma(n/2)$ to get $\omega_n=\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1)$. Stirling's formula from the earlier analysis volumes proves its limit zero. The first five volumes are $2,\pi,4\pi/3,\pi^2/2,8\pi^2/15$. The recurrence $\omega_{n+2}=2\pi\omega_n/(n+2)$ makes the odd sequence largest at $n=5$ and the even sequence largest at $n=6$; comparing $8\pi^2/15$ with $\pi^3/6$ gives the overall maximum at $5$ (the comparison uses $\pi<16/5$). For a direct bound from the earlier alternating arctangent series, $\pi/4<\sum_{j=0}^{18}(-1)^j/(2j+1)<4/5$; the final inequality is a finite rational calculation. The maximum is about $5.2638$.

The cube has volume $2^n$, so the ball's fraction is $\omega_n/2^n$, about $0.00249$ for $n=10$. Most of the high-dimensional cube lies outside this fixed-radius central ball. This statement concerns distance from the centre; it does not say that a typical point lies arbitrarily close to a vertex.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch03-compact-potential}
\idea A large circle detects the ordinary cohomology class, while compact support asks for a primitive to vanish at both ends of the punctured plane.
\writeup (a) Choose a radius $R>1$ whose circle is outside the compact support. Stokes on the annulus between the unit circle and this circle gives equal periods because the form is closed. The large-circle period is zero, so the punctured-plane theorem supplies a smooth primitive.
(b) The form $\dd\chi$ is supported in the compact annulus $1\le r\le2$ and is closed. Any other primitive is $\chi+c$, since their difference has zero derivative on the connected punctured plane. Compact support would require it to vanish for all sufficiently large $r$, forcing $c=0$, but then it equals $1$ near the missing origin. That support is not compact in the punctured plane: a sequence approaching zero has no limit there. Thus no compactly supported primitive exists.
(c) Since $\dd(\dd\theta)=0$, we have $\dd(f(r)\dd\theta)=f'(r)dr\wedge\dd\theta$. The two coordinate forms are independent on the punctured plane, so this vanishes exactly when $f$ is constant on $(0,\infty)$. A nonzero radial cutoff therefore destroys closedness. There is no contradiction with (a), which concerns forms that remain closed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch03-torus-periods}
\idea Lift to a space with primitives, then calculate exactly which primitive increments obstruct descent.
\writeup (a) Lift a closed $1$-form to $\R^2$. Poincar\'e gives a primitive $g$. Its derivatives are periodic, so both $g(x+1,y)-g(x,y)$ and $g(x,y+1)-g(x,y)$ are constants, say $c_1,c_2$. They are the two coordinate-circle periods. The function $g-c_1x-c_2y$ is periodic in both variables and descends smoothly to the torus. Thus the original form differs from $c_1\dd\theta_1+c_2\dd\theta_2$ by an exact form. The earlier independence calculation makes these coordinates unique, proving $H^1=\R^2$.
(b) Put $a(y)=\int_0^1f(x,y)dx-m$, whose mean over one $y$-period is zero. Define
\[
A(y)=-\int_0^y a(v)dv,\qquad
B(x,y)=\int_0^x(f(t,y)-m-a(y))dt.
\]
Both are smooth and periodic: $A$ has zero increment in $y$, while the integrand for $B$ has zero average in $x$ and is periodic in $y$. The torus form $\eta=A(y)\dd x+B(x,y)\dd y$ satisfies
$\dd\eta=(B_x-A_y)\dd x\wedge\dd y=(f-m)\dd x\wedge\dd y$.
Every $2$-form is closed by degree and therefore has class $m[\dd x\wedge\dd y]$. Its integral is $m$, so it is exact exactly for $m=0$. This proves $H^2=\R$.
(c) Integer entries ensure $M(x+z)-Mx\in\Z^2$, so $F_M$ is well defined. If $M=(m_{ij})$, then $F_M^*\dd\theta_i=\sum_jm_{ij}\dd\theta_j$. It sends a coefficient column $c$ to $M^{\mathsf T}c$ on $H^1$, and multiplies the area class by $\det M$. For the stated matrix, the forms become $2\dd\theta_1+\dd\theta_2$ and $\dd\theta_2$, and area doubles. A target $(u,v)$ has preimages with $y=v$ and $x=(u-v+j)/2$, $j=0,1$, modulo integers, precisely two. For $M=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$, the second $1$-form class and the area class pull back to zero, consistently with its image being a circle. The pullback identities do not require an invertible map.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch03-source-flux}
\idea The singularity is the only place where the flux cannot be removed by Stokes.
\writeup (a) Write $r=|x|$ and $dV=\dd x^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\dd x^n$. Expanding each wedge gives
$(\sum_i x_i\dd x^i)\wedge\sigma=r^2dV$. Hence
\[
\dd\alpha=-nr^{-n-2}\left(\sum_i x_i\dd x^i\right)\wedge\sigma
+r^{-n}n\,dV=0.
\]
On a radius-$R$ sphere the pullback of $\sigma$ under scaling is $R^n\sigma$ on the unit sphere; its coefficient contributes one power and its $n-1$ differential factors contribute the rest. Multiplication by $r^{-n}$ cancels the factor. The outward sphere integral is therefore always $s_{n-1}$.
(b) If zero is outside the compact region, $\alpha$ is smooth on a neighborhood of it, and Stokes gives boundary integral zero. If zero is inside, remove a small closed ball around it contained in the interior. Stokes on the remaining region gives
$0=\int_{\partial D}\alpha-\int_{S^{n-1}_\varepsilon}\alpha$, with minus sign on the inner boundary. Thus the answer is $s_{n-1}$. The assumption $0\notin\partial D$ excludes an undefined singular boundary integral.
(c) For $n=2$, $\alpha=(x\dd y-y\dd x)/(x^2+y^2)=\dd\theta$ and flux is $2\pi$ around zero. For $n=3$, it is the flux form of $F(x)=x/|x|^3$, a radial inverse-square field, and the enclosing flux is $4\pi$. A physical normalization can rescale this constant; the geometric calculation specifies the one used here.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch03-radial-potentials}
\idea The homotopy operator integrates the form along the radial motion that contracts the domain.
\writeup For the contraction $h(t,x)=tx$ on $0\le t\le1$, its time derivative is $x$ and its spatial derivative sends $v$ to $tv$. The coefficient of $\dd t$ in $h^*\omega$ is therefore the integrand $t^{k-1}\omega_{tx}(x,v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1})$. To fit the all-real-time homotopy convention, use $h(t,x)=\beta(t)x$ as in Poincar\'e's lemma. The integrand becomes $\beta'\beta^{k-1}\omega_{\beta x}(x,v_1,\ldots)$; the fundamental theorem applied to an antiderivative in $\beta$ gives exactly the same integral from $0$ to $1$. Thus this is $K\omega$, is smooth including at $x=0$, and satisfies $\dd\eta=\omega$ for a closed positive-degree form by the homotopy formula.

For a curl-free field, $\omega_F$ is a closed $1$-form and its primitive is
$f(x)=\int_0^1F(tx)\cdot x\,dt$, with $\nabla f=F$.
For a divergence-free field, the flux form $\eta_F$ is closed. Its contraction on $x,v$ is $F(tx)\cdot(x\times v)=(F(tx)\times x)\cdot v$. Consequently its primitive is the $1$-form of
$A(x)=\int_0^1tF(tx)\times x\,dt$, and the derivative identification gives $\nabla\times A=F$.
For $F=(0,0,1)$ this yields $A=(-y/2,x/2,0)$. Direct differentiation gives curl $(0,0,1)$. On $x=R\cos t,y=R\sin t$, $0\le t\le2\pi$, the circulation integrand is $R^2/2$, giving $\pi R^2$, also the field's upward flux through the radius-$R$ disk.
\end{solution}

\emph{Differential Forms in Algebraic Topology} (Springer, 1982), takes de Rham cohomology much further. The no-retraction proof of Brouwer's theorem by
integration goes back to the same circle of ideas as Milnor's article cited in Chapter~2; the ray construction of $r$ is standard.
