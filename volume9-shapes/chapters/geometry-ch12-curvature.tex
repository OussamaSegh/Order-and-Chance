\chapter{Curvature of Curves and Polyhedra}\label{geometry:ch:curvature}

\begin{center}\itshape
If a curved surface is developed upon any other surface whatever, the measure of curvature at each point remains unchanged.
\upshape(paraphrase of Gauss's \emph{Theorema Egregium}, 1827)
\end{center}

A tangent tells us which way a curve is heading. Curvature tells us how quickly that heading changes. Walk around a circle and the tangent turns steadily; walk around an ellipse and it turns faster at the narrow ends. On a surface there are two bending directions, and their product measures something that survives bending without stretching.

Gauss published his \emph{Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas} in 1827 after years of surveying Hanover. His \emph{Theorema Egregium}, or remarkable theorem, says that Gaussian curvature can be determined from measurements made within the surface. A plane and a cylinder both have zero Gaussian curvature and fit together locally without stretching. A sphere has positive curvature and cannot be flattened in the same way.

Chapter~4 proved the intrinsic result and smooth Gauss--Bonnet. Here we approach curvature through tangent angles, supporting lines and the corners of polyhedra. The curve and convex-polyhedron calculations need calculus and Euclidean geometry; the planar graph step also uses the polygonal separation theorem from Volume~7, Theorem~6.1. We identify that prerequisite where it enters. The smooth surface discussion explains the Gauss map directly, without assuming Chapter~4's curvature formulas. General smooth Gauss--Bonnet is recalled with its hypotheses, not proved a second time.

\begin{think}
Roll paper into a cylinder and compare the lengths of lines drawn on it before and after rolling. Why does trying the same thing around a ball require a crease, a tear or some stretching?
\end{think}

\section{The curvature of a plane curve}

Let $\gamma:[0,L]\to\R^2$ be $C^2$ and parametrized by arc length, so $T=\gamma'$ has length $1$. Differentiating $T\cdot T=1$ shows that $T'$ is perpendicular to $T$. Let $J(x,y)=(-y,x)$, rotation through $+\pi/2$, and put $N=JT$. There is therefore a scalar
\[
\kappa=\det(T,T')=x'y''-y'x'',\qquad T'=\kappa JT.
\]
This is \emph{signed curvature}; reversing orientation changes its sign.

To turn this into an angle without assuming the desired formula, choose $\theta_0$ with $T(0)=(\cos\theta_0,\sin\theta_0)$ and set $\theta(s)=\theta_0+\int_0^s\kappa(u)\,du$. Differentiate the vector obtained by rotating $T(s)$ through $-\theta(s)$. Its derivative is zero, because $T'=\kappa JT$ and $\theta'=\kappa$. Its initial value is $(1,0)$, proving
\[
T=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad\kappa=\theta',\qquad\gamma''=\kappa N.
\]
Thus curvature really is the tangent's turning rate per unit length. A counterclockwise radius-$r$ circle has curvature $1/r$; a line has curvature zero.

For a general regular parametrization $t\mapsto(x(t),y(t))$, meaning that its velocity never vanishes, the formula is
\[
\kappa=\frac{x'y''-y'x''}{(x'^2+y'^2)^{3/2}}.
\]
Exercise~\ref{geometry:ex:graphcurv} derives it without dividing by $x'$, which might vanish. At a point with $\kappa\ne0$, the \emph{osculating circle} has radius $1/|\kappa|$ and centre $\gamma+N/\kappa$. It has the same point, tangent and second derivative when both curves are parametrized with the matching unit speed: it matches the curve to second order. At zero curvature there is no finite such circle.

\begin{theorem}[curvature determines the curve]\label{geometry:thm:fundcurve}
Every continuous function $\kappa$ on an arc-length interval $[0,L]$ is the curvature of a unit-speed $C^2$ curve. Any two such curves differ by a rotation followed by a translation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose an initial point $p$ and angle $\theta_0$. Define
\[
\theta(s)=\theta_0+\int_0^s\kappa(u)\,du,
\qquad \gamma(s)=p+\int_0^s(\cos\theta(u),\sin\theta(u))\,du.
\]
Continuity of $\kappa$ makes $\theta$ continuously differentiable and $\gamma$ twice continuously differentiable. Its velocity has length $1$ and turning rate $\kappa$. Conversely, the angle construction above shows that any curve with this curvature has exactly these formulas for its initial data. Changing the initial angle rotates its velocity vectors, and changing the initial point translates the resulting curve.
\end{proof}

A \emph{closed smooth curve} here is periodic with matching derivatives at the join: for a $C^k$ curve, $\gamma^{(j)}(0)=\gamma^{(j)}(L)$ for $0\le j\le k$. For a regular such curve the tangent returns to itself, giving $\int_0^L\kappa\,ds=2\pi m$ for an integer $m$, its turning number. This is signed total curvature, distinct from $\int|\kappa|\,ds$. Hopf's turning theorem, quoted here, gives $m=\pm1$ for a simple closed regular curve. Self-crossings allow other values; Exercise~\ref{geometry:ex:totalcurv} gives $m=0$ for a figure eight. Having integer total turning alone does not guarantee closure of a prescribed-curvature curve.

\section{The four vertex theorem}

A \emph{vertex} is a point where curvature has a local maximum or minimum. We use non-strict extrema: a constant-curvature arc contains infinitely many of both kinds. The ellipse has two maxima at the ends of its long axis and two minima at the ends of its short axis.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{geometry-ch12-vertices}
\caption{The ellipse $x=1.6\cos t$, $y=\sin t$ with its osculating circles at the four vertices (radius $0.625$ at the ends of the long axis, $2.56$ at the ends of
the short one), and its curvature as a function of $t$: two maxima and two minima.}
\label{geometry:fig:vertices}
\end{figure}

An \emph{oval} is a simple closed $C^3$ unit-speed curve bounding a convex region and meeting each line in at most two points. Its derivatives are periodic as specified above. The line condition excludes straight boundary segments. We need not assume that curvature is everywhere nonzero.

\begin{theorem}[four vertex theorem]\label{geometry:thm:fourvertex}
An oval that is not a circle has at least two local maxima and two local minima of curvature.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First derive an identity. Periodicity and integration by parts give
\[
\int_0^L\kappa'\,ds=0,\qquad
\int_0^L\kappa'x\,ds=-\int_0^L\kappa\cos\theta\,ds
=-[\sin\theta]_0^L=0.
\]
Likewise $\int\kappa'y\,ds=\int(\cos\theta)'\,ds=0$. Consequently
\[
\int_0^L\kappa'(s)\ell(\gamma(s))\,ds=0
\]
for every affine function $\ell(x,y)=ax+by+c$.

If $\kappa$ is constant on a nonempty open arc, any two distinct interior points of that arc are both local maxima and local minima, proving the stated non-strict version. Suppose there is no such arc. Between any two distinct local maxima there is a local minimum on each of the two joining arcs. Indeed, near the endpoint having the smaller value there are points of strictly smaller value on either side; otherwise one side would be constant on an interval. The minimum on each closed joining arc is therefore below both endpoint values and is attained in its interior. Interchanging maximum and minimum gives the converse observation. Thus if the conclusion fails, there must be exactly one local maximum $P$ and one local minimum $Q$. This argument also covers infinitely many extrema, without an unjustified finite alternation assumption.

The curvature is nonconstant: constant nonzero curvature would give a circle by the preceding theorem, and constant zero curvature would give a line, which cannot close with positive length. On one arc from $P$ to $Q$, $\kappa$ must be nonincreasing, and on the other it must be nondecreasing. To justify this, a rise between two points on an arc from the global maximum to the global minimum would force an additional interior minimum before that rise; taking a minimum on the corresponding closed subarc proves it.

The line through $P,Q$ meets the oval only at those points. The two open arcs lie on opposite sides, because the oval bounds a convex region and the chord crosses its interior. Choose the sign of an affine function $\ell$ vanishing on that line so that $\kappa'\ell(\gamma)\ge0$ on both arcs. Since $\kappa$ changes between its distinct extreme values, its derivative is nonzero at some interior point. There $\ell\ne0$, so the continuous product is positive on a small interval. Its integral is strictly positive, contradicting the identity above. The supposed failure is impossible.
\end{proof}

Mukhopadhyaya proved the oval theorem in 1909; Kneser's 1912 extension covers all simple closed curves under the appropriate regularity assumptions. The affine-function proof above is attributed to Herglotz. Its mechanism is worth following: closure forces three integrals to vanish, while only two vertices would supply a line making their combination strictly positive. The obstruction is an integral, not a visual rule about how an egg ought to look.

\section{The curvature of a surface}

At a point $p$ of a smooth surface in $\R^3$, translate and rotate coordinates so that $p=0$ and the tangent plane is $z=0$. Locally the surface is a graph $z=f(x,y)$ with $f(0)=0$ and $\nabla f(0)=0$. Its first nontrivial Taylor term is $\tfrac12(x,y)H(x,y)^{\mathsf T}$, where $H$ is the symmetric Hessian at $0$.

\begin{definition}
In these orthonormal tangent coordinates, the eigenvalues $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$ of $H$ are the \emph{principal curvatures}, with the sign convention determined by the chosen normal. Their product $K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\det H$ is the \emph{Gaussian curvature}.
\end{definition}

Rotating the tangent coordinates conjugates $H$ by an orthogonal matrix, leaving its eigenvalues unchanged. Reversing the normal negates both eigenvalues and leaves $K$ unchanged. The spectral theorem identifies two perpendicular principal directions: in each corresponding normal plane, the surface section has the indicated signed curve curvature at $p$.

At the south pole of a radius-$r$ sphere the graph $z=r-\sqrt{r^2-x^2-y^2}$ has quadratic term $(x^2+y^2)/(2r)$, so $K=1/r^2$. A cylinder has one curvature $1/r$ and one zero, giving $K=0$. For $z=xy$, the Hessian at $0$ is $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $K=-1$. A positive determinant means the quadratic term is definite and the surface lies locally on one side of its tangent plane. A negative determinant gives opposite signs and a saddle crossing that plane. When $K=0$, higher terms matter: a surface can be nonplanar despite zero curvature at one point.

On an oriented surface patch, choose a continuous unit normal. The \emph{Gauss map} sends the patch to $S^2$ by this choice of $N$; no global normal is assumed for an arbitrary surface. Convex boundaries below have a global outward normal. For a graph with upward normal,
\[
N=\frac{(-f_x,-f_y,1)}{\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}}.
\]
It measures how quickly the tangent plane changes as we move on the surface.

\begin{proposition}\label{geometry:prop:gaussmap}
With the compatible orientations on the tangent planes, the Jacobian determinant of the Gauss map at $p$ is $K(p)$. For patches $U_r$ given by tangent-coordinate disks of radius $r$ centred at $p$,
\[
\operatorname{area}N(U_r)=|K(p)|\operatorname{area}(U_r)+o(\operatorname{area}(U_r))
\quad(r\to0).
\]
When $K(p)\ne0$, the map is locally one-to-one and the same local area ratio holds for arbitrary shrinking measurable patches in a sufficiently small neighborhood.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the graph coordinates at $0$, $N(0)=(0,0,1)$ and both tangent planes are horizontal. Differentiating the normal gives $dN_0=-H$: the derivative of the denominator vanishes because $\nabla f(0)=0$. Hence $\det dN_0=\det H=K(p)$.

If this determinant is nonzero, the inverse function theorem makes $N$ a diffeomorphism on a small neighborhood. Change of variables gives $\operatorname{area}N(U)=\int_U|K|\,dA$ there. Continuity of $K$ supplies the asserted ratio as $U$ shrinks to $p$.

If $K(p)=0$, use smooth coordinates on the sphere at $N(p)$. On a radius-$r$ coordinate disk the map equals its rank-at-most-one linear differential plus a uniform $o(r)$ remainder. Its image therefore lies in a rectangle of length $O(r)$ and width $o(r)$ if the rank is one; in rank zero both dimensions are $o(r)$. Coordinate area densities are bounded and tend to their values at the centres. Thus the image area is $o(r^2)$, whereas $\operatorname{area}(U_r)=\pi r^2+o(r^2)$, proving the zero-curvature case without an invalid one-to-one change of variables.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Gauss's remarkable theorem says that these externally defined curvatures have an intrinsic product: lengths within the surface determine $K$. Chapter~4 proves this, and do Carmo gives a classical treatment. Consequently a local distance-preserving bending keeps $K$ fixed. A plane can roll into a cylinder, but cannot become a sphere of radius $r$, whose curvature is $1/r^2$. Exercise~\ref{geometry:ex:nomap} gives a small-distance obstruction directly. For an unstretched sheet, $K=0$ means that if one principal bending curvature is nonzero, the other is zero. This explains the familiar stiffening when a flexible slice is bent across its width, within the idealized unstretched-surface model.
\end{remark}

\section{Gauss--Bonnet}

\begin{theorem}[Gauss--Bonnet for convex surfaces]\label{geometry:thm:gbconvex}
Let $S$ be the smooth boundary of a compact convex body in $\R^3$, with $K>0$ at every point. Then
\[
\iint_S K\,dA=4\pi.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose the outward normal. For every unit vector $u$, the linear function $x\mapsto u\cdot x$ has a maximum on the compact body, at a boundary point $p$. Its support plane is the tangent plane, with outward normal $u$, so $N(p)=u$. Conversely the tangent plane to a smooth convex boundary supports the body, so points with normal $u$ maximize that linear function.

There cannot be two different maximizing points. Their joining segment would lie in the supporting plane and in the boundary. At an interior point of that segment, the graph Hessian is semidefinite because the body stays on one side of the tangent plane. Its quadratic term vanishes in the segment direction, so the Hessian has a zero eigenvalue. This would give $K=0$, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence the Gauss map is bijective.

Its differential has positive determinant everywhere, so the inverse function theorem gives local smooth inverses, which agree and give a global inverse. Apply change of variables to this diffeomorphism:
$\int_S K\,dA=\operatorname{area}(S^2)=4\pi$.
\end{proof}

The total stays $4\pi$ even when the surface is not round. Changing its shape changes both the curvature and the area element; their integral remains fixed. The general smooth theorem proved in Chapter~4 is $\int_S K\,dA=2\pi\chi(S)$ for a compact smooth surface \emph{without boundary}. Here $\chi$ is the Euler characteristic: $2$ for a sphere, $0$ for a torus, and $2-2g$ for a closed orientable genus-$g$ surface. With boundary there is an additional boundary-turning term, so that hypothesis cannot be dropped. On a geodesic triangle its local form reads $\int_TK\,dA=\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi$, recovering the spherical and hyperbolic area formulas already proved in Appendix~I and Chapter~9.

\section{Polyhedra and Descartes' theorem}

A face of a convex polyhedron is flat, so there is no smooth curvature within a face. At a vertex $v$, add the angles of the incident faces and compare the sum with a full turn. The \emph{angular defect} is
\[
\delta_v=2\pi-\sum_{\text{faces at }v}\text{face angle}.
\]
For an actual vertex of a three-dimensional convex polyhedron this is positive. A point added along an edge or within a face has zero defect and need not be counted as a new geometric vertex. At a cube corner, three right angles give defect $\pi/2$.

$8\cdot\frac\pi2=4\pi$.}
\label{geometry:fig:defect}
\end{figure}

\label{fig:v9-app-k-turning-offset}
\end{figure}

\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Euler, 1758]\label{geometry:thm:euler}
For a three-dimensional convex polyhedron with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ faces, $V-E+F=2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Obtain a straight-edged planar drawing by projecting from a point just outside the relative interior of one face onto that face's plane. Choose the point sufficiently close that all vertices not on the face project strictly inside it. This is possible because there are finitely many vertices, each at positive depth behind the face plane; as the projection centre approaches the chosen interior point, all their projections approach that point. Rays through the face enter the convex body there and leave it at exactly one point on the remaining boundary. Thus the projected remaining faces partition the face polygon without crossings. The original chosen face corresponds to the unbounded region. This is the Schlegel drawing, with the same $V,E,F$.

We prove the planar formula for this connected polygonal graph. A connected graph without cycles is a tree and has $E=V-1$: remove a leaf inductively, or grow a spanning tree one new vertex and edge at a time. Its complement is connected, so $F=1$. One can see this inductively as well: attaching a leaf arc does not separate a region because its free end can be passed around inside a small disk. Thus $V-E+F=2$ in this case.

Otherwise remove an edge on a simple cycle. The graph stays connected, since the rest of the cycle joins its endpoints. The polygonal separation theorem of Volume~7, Theorem~6.1 says that this cycle has two sides in different complementary regions, so the two regions adjacent to the removed edge are distinct. Removing the edge joins exactly those regions, lowering both $E$ and $F$ by $1$ and preserving $V-E+F$. Repeat until a tree remains; the formula follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Descartes]\label{geometry:thm:descartesdefect}
The angular defects of a three-dimensional convex polyhedron sum to $4\pi$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A convex face with $n_f$ edges has angle sum $(n_f-2)\pi$, by drawing diagonals from one vertex. Summing face angles counts every edge twice, so the total is $(2E-2F)\pi$. Subtract this from the $2\pi$ assigned to each vertex:
\[
\sum_v\delta_v=2\pi V-(2E-2F)\pi=2\pi(V-E+F)=4\pi.
\]
The last equality is Euler's formula.
\end{proof}

Descartes recorded the defect theorem in \emph{De solidorum elementis}, around 1630. Leibniz copied the manuscript in 1676; that copy preserved a result recovered in the nineteenth century. Federico's study gives its history. For a polyhedron, the defect is the discrete curvature carried at a vertex. It is the counterpart of the smooth curvature integral, though proving convergence for polyhedral approximations requires a further argument and is not part of the calculation above.

A cube makes the total visible as eight gaps of $\pi/2$. A smooth convex surface measures the same total by the area swept out by its normals. These two calculations are useful places to finish a first reading: they explain why local bending can change while the total stays fixed. The exercises revisit that connection with cones, tori and surfaces whose noncompactness changes the answer. For the next volume, \emph{Abstraction: Categories, Algebra and Homology}, keep the habit used here: identify what a construction preserves before trying to recognize every object it can produce.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:graphcurv}
For every regular $C^2$ plane parametrization, prove
$\kappa=(x'y''-y'x'')/(x'^2+y'^2)^{3/2}$. Your argument must work at a vertical tangent. Deduce $\kappa=f''/(1+f'^2)^{3/2}$ for the graph $y=f(x)$ oriented with increasing $x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:ellipsevertex}
For $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$ with $a>b>0$, find the curvature and prove that there are exactly four vertices. Identify each as a maximum or minimum and give its curvature value.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:constcurv}
Show that a regular curve of constant nonzero signed curvature lies on a circle of radius $1/|\kappa|$. Explain the zero-curvature alternative and the effect of orientation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:totalcurv}
Compute the signed total curvature of a counterclockwise circle traversed once and of the regular figure eight $(\sin2t,\sin t)$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. Verify regularity and matching tangent vectors before interpreting turning numbers.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:clothoid}
Construct the unit-speed curve with $\kappa(s)=s$, starting at the origin in the positive horizontal direction. Prove that it approaches a finite point as $s\to\infty$. Identify that point by evaluating the Fresnel integrals; the exact evaluation may use the Gaussian integral and complex analysis from Volume~5, while convergence should follow directly by integration by parts. Explain the road-design interpretation.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:surfacesK}
Find the Gaussian curvature at the origin for $z=x^2+3y^2$, $z=x^2-y^2$, $z=x^2$ and the monkey saddle $z=x^3-3xy^2$. Which examples show that zero Gaussian curvature at one point does not imply local planarity?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:torusK}
For the torus $((R+r\cos v)\cos u,(R+r\cos v)\sin u,r\sin v)$, with $0<r<R$ and $u,v$ modulo $2\pi$, use
\[
K=\frac{\cos v}{r(R+r\cos v)},\qquad dA=r(R+r\cos v)\,du\,dv
\]
to compute its total Gaussian curvature. Locate the regions where $K$ is positive, negative and zero.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:platonic}
Verify the total defect $4\pi$ for each of the five Platonic solids by computing its defect per vertex and number of vertices.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:fiveplatonic}
A three-dimensional convex polyhedron has $p$ edges on every face and $q$ faces at every vertex. Use Euler's formula to prove $1/p+1/q>1/2$ and list the possible integer pairs $p,q\ge3$. Deduce the usual bound of five Platonic types. Distinguish the restriction on pairs from constructing the regular solid of each type.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{geometry:ex:football}
A three-dimensional convex polyhedron has only pentagons and hexagons as faces, and three faces meet at each vertex. Prove that there are exactly twelve pentagons. Do not assume the faces are regular or that all angular defects are equal.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{geometry:ex:nomap}
On the unit sphere choose a small equilateral geodesic triangle $ABC$ of side $a>0$, with $A$ the North Pole, and let $M$ be the midpoint of the short arc $BC$. Use the spherical cosine law to compute $AM$ and compare it with $\sqrt3\,a/2$. Show that preserving the five distances $AB,AC,BC,BM,CM$ in the plane forces this latter value for $AM$. Prove that the two values differ for all sufficiently small $a>0$, and conclude that no spherical neighborhood admits a map to the plane preserving every intrinsic distance.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-app-k-offset-curvature}
Let $\gamma$ be an oriented unit-speed $C^3$ plane curve with left normal $N$, and put $\gamma_a=\gamma+aN$ for a constant $a$.
(a) On an interval where $1-a\kappa>0$, prove $ds_a=(1-a\kappa)ds$ and $\kappa_a=\kappa/(1-a\kappa)$.
(b) Describe the failure when $1-a\kappa=0$, using a counterclockwise circle as an example.
(c) On a positively oriented strictly convex closed curve with $\kappa>0$, if $a<0$, show that its outward parallel length is $L-2\pi a$. Explain the use of the turning theorem in this last step.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-app-k-turning-not-closure}
On $[0,2\pi]$ prescribe $\kappa_\eta(s)=1+\eta\cos s$, where $0<|\eta|<1$. Construct its curve with initial point $0$ and initial unit tangent $1$, identifying $\R^2$ with $\mathbb C$.
(a) Show that its tangent returns to its initial value and its total curvature is $2\pi$.
(b) Prove that its displacement is $-\pi\eta+O(\eta^2)$ as $\eta\to0$.
(c) Conclude that for every sufficiently small nonzero $\eta$ the curve is not closed, despite its positive periodic curvature and matching end tangents.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-app-k-pyramid-limit}
Fix $0<\beta<\pi/2$. Build a regular $n$-gonal pyramid, $n\ge3$, whose apex-to-base-vertex edges have length $1$, base circumradius is $\sin\beta$ and height is $\cos\beta$.
(a) Compute its apex face angle and apex defect.
(b) Find the limiting apex defect as $n\to\infty$ and compare it with the missing angle in the development of a cone of slant length $1$.
(c) Compute the total defect at the base vertices, its limit, and the limit of the defect at an individual base vertex. Why does vanishing of each individual base defect not make their total vanish?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-app-k-noncompact-gauss}
For the entire graph $z=(ax^2+by^2)/2$, where $a,b\in\R$, use the upward Gauss map.
(a) Derive $K=ab/(1+a^2x^2+b^2y^2)^2$ from its area Jacobian.
(b) For $ab\ne0$, compute $\int K\,dA$ over the entire graph and identify the Gauss-map image.
(c) Treat $ab=0$ separately and explain why the answers do not contradict the compact convex Gauss--Bonnet theorem.
\end{exercise}

\section{Hints}

\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:graphcurv}
Normalize the velocity to a unit vector $T$. The derivative of its angle is $\det(T,dT/dt)$; divide by the speed to change from $t$ to arc length.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:ellipsevertex}
The numerator is $ab$. Study $b^2+(a^2-b^2)\sin^2t$ in the denominator.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:constcurv}
Differentiate $\gamma+N/\kappa$ when $\kappa$ is a nonzero constant, or use curvature uniqueness.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:totalcurv}
The figure eight's velocity is $(2\cos2t,\cos t)$ and never vanishes. Compare the two parameter intervals by $t\mapsto t+\pi$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:clothoid}
In complex notation the curve is $\int_0^s e^{iu^2/2}\,du$. On a tail use $d(e^{iu^2/2})=iu e^{iu^2/2}du$. For the value, first add a positive Gaussian damping parameter and then remove it, controlling tails uniformly.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:surfacesK}
All four graphs have horizontal tangent plane at the origin, so take the determinant of the Hessian there.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:torusK}
Multiplication by the area density cancels the denominator. Keep $R>r$ when deciding signs.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:platonic}
At a vertex carrying $q$ regular $p$-gons the defect is $2\pi-q(p-2)\pi/p$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:fiveplatonic}
Count incidences to obtain $pF=2E=qV$ and substitute into Euler's formula.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:football}
If there are $f_5$ pentagons and $f_6$ hexagons, then $2E=5f_5+6f_6$, $3V=2E$ and $F=f_5+f_6$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{geometry:ex:nomap}
Symmetry makes $AM$ perpendicular to $BC$, giving $\cos a=\cos(AM)\cos(a/2)$. Compare its quotient with the Taylor expansion of $\cos(\sqrt3\,a/2)$ to fourth order.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-app-k-offset-curvature}
Differentiate $N=JT$ to get $N'=-\kappa T$. On a regular offset with positive factor, its unit tangent is still $T$.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-app-k-turning-not-closure}
The tangent angle is $s+\eta\sin s$. Expand $e^{i\eta\sin s}$ with a uniform remainder on the finite interval before integrating.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-app-k-pyramid-limit}
A base edge has length $2\sin\beta\sin(\pi/n)$. Its two incident slant edges form an isosceles triangle. Descartes supplies the remaining total defect.
\end{hint}
\begin{hint}{ex:v9-app-k-noncompact-gauss}
Set $D=1+a^2x^2+b^2y^2$. Compute $N\cdot(N_x\times N_y)$ and divide by the surface area density $\sqrt D$. For $ab\ne0$, substitute $u=ax,v=by$ and integrate in polar coordinates.
\end{hint}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:graphcurv}
\idea A determinant measures turning without choosing a slope chart.
\writeup Put $v=\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}>0$ and $T=(x',y')/v$. Differentiating and taking a determinant cancels the term parallel to the velocity, giving
$\det(T,dT/dt)=(x'y''-y'x'')/v^2$. A local tangent angle, whose existence was proved above, has derivative equal to this determinant. Since $ds/dt=v$, divide by $v$ to get the curvature formula. Nothing required $x'\ne0$. For the graph with increasing $x=t$, substitute $x'=1,x''=0,y'=f',y''=f''$ to obtain $f''/(1+f'^2)^{3/2}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:ellipsevertex}
\idea The curvature numerator stays constant, so extrema come from the speed alone.
\writeup Differentiating the parametrization gives $x'y''-y'x''=ab$ and squared speed $g(t)=a^2\sin^2t+b^2\cos^2t>0$. Hence $\kappa=ab/g(t)^{3/2}$. Now $g'=(a^2-b^2)\sin2t$ vanishes only at $0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$. At $0,\pi$ it is minimal with value $b^2$, so curvature is maximal with value $a/b^2$. At $\pi/2,3\pi/2$ it is maximal with value $a^2$, so curvature is minimal with value $b/a^2$. These are four distinct vertices and there are no others. The assumption $a>b$ excludes the circle, whose curvature is constant everywhere.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:constcurv}
\idea A candidate centre stays fixed if and only if the nonzero curvature is constant.
\writeup Reparametrize by arc length. From $T'=\kappa N$ and $N'=-\kappa T$, a constant $\kappa\ne0$ gives
$(\gamma+N/\kappa)'=T-T=0$. This fixed centre is at distance $1/|\kappa|$ from every point of the curve. Positive signed curvature traverses the circle counterclockwise, negative curvature clockwise; a long parameter interval may trace it more than once. For $\kappa=0$, $T'=0$, so the curve is a straight line segment or line portion.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:totalcurv}
\idea Reflection reverses signed turning, making the figure eight's two halves cancel.
\writeup A counterclockwise circle has curvature $1/r$ and length $2\pi r$, hence total $2\pi$. For the figure eight, simultaneous vanishing of $2\cos2t$ and $\cos t$ is impossible: when $\cos t=0$, $\cos2t=-1$. The curve is regular and all derivatives match at $0,2\pi$. Also $\gamma(t+\pi)=(x(t),-y(t))$. Reflection reverses the determinant in the signed-curvature numerator and preserves speed. Therefore the integrals of $\kappa\,ds$ on $[0,\pi]$ and $[\pi,2\pi]$ cancel, giving $0$. The corresponding turning numbers are $1$ and $0$, respectively.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:clothoid}
\idea Increasing tangent angular speed creates a convergent oscillatory integral, even though the curve has infinite length.
\writeup The curvature theorem gives $\theta(s)=s^2/2$ and
\[
\gamma(s)=\left(\int_0^s\cos(u^2/2)\,du,\ \int_0^s\sin(u^2/2)\,du\right).
\]
For $T>R>0$, integration by parts in complex notation gives
\[
\int_R^T e^{iu^2/2}\,du
=\left[\frac{e^{iu^2/2}}{iu}\right]_R^T
+\frac1i\int_R^T\frac{e^{iu^2/2}}{u^2}\,du.
\]
The tail is bounded by $1/T+1/R+\int_R^T u^{-2}du\le2/R$. Thus both coordinate integrals converge by the Cauchy criterion. This proves existence of a limit using calculus alone.

To evaluate it, put $a=\varepsilon-i$, $\varepsilon>0$. The Gaussian identity
\[
\int_0^\infty e^{-a u^2/2}\,du=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,a^{-1/2}
\quad(\operatorname{Re}a>0)
\]
follows from the real Gaussian integral by the holomorphic identity theorem of Volume~5; on each compact subset of this half-plane Gaussian domination justifies holomorphy, and the square root is the branch positive on the positive real axis. Integration by parts gives the uniform tail bound $2/(|a|R)\le2/R$ for $0<\varepsilon\le1$. On $[0,R]$ dominated convergence removes the damping, and the uniform tails let $R\to\infty$ afterwards. The limit of the right side is $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,e^{i\pi/4}$, so
\[
\lim_{s\to\infty}\gamma(s)=(\sqrt\pi/2,\sqrt\pi/2).
\]
The clothoid gives a road transition from zero curvature to a circular bend. At constant travel speed its curvature, hence required lateral acceleration, changes linearly with time; this is the geometric reason for using it rather than an abrupt change from a line to a circle.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:surfacesK}
\idea At a horizontal tangent point, the Hessian already gives both principal curvatures.
\writeup The four Hessians at $0$ are $\operatorname{diag}(2,6)$, $\operatorname{diag}(2,-2)$, $\operatorname{diag}(2,0)$ and the zero matrix. Their determinants are respectively $12,-4,0,0$. The third graph is a nonplanar parabolic cylinder with one zero principal curvature. The monkey saddle has vanishing quadratic term but nonzero cubic terms and crosses its tangent plane. Both show why $K=0$ at a point cannot imply a planar neighborhood.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:torusK}
\idea The positive and negative parts have equal integral after the area factor cancels.
\writeup Since $R+r\cos v\ge R-r>0$,
\[
\int K\,dA=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos v\,dv\,du=0.
\]
The sign is exactly that of $\cos v$. Thus $K>0$ on the outer half $-\pi/2<v<\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$, $K<0$ on the inner half $\pi/2<v<3\pi/2$, and $K=0$ on the two circles $v=\pi/2,3\pi/2$. The result agrees with $2\pi\chi$ for a torus, whose Euler characteristic is zero.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:platonic}
\idea Use the regular face angle at one vertex, then multiply by the vertex count.
\writeup The face angle of a regular $p$-gon is $(p-2)\pi/p$. For the tetrahedron $(p,q,V)=(3,3,4)$, the defect is $\pi$ and total is $4\pi$. For the cube $(4,3,8)$ it is $\pi/2$; for the octahedron $(3,4,6)$ it is $2\pi/3$; for the dodecahedron $(5,3,20)$ it is $\pi/5$; for the icosahedron $(3,5,12)$ it is $\pi/3$. Multiplying in each case gives $4\pi$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:fiveplatonic}
\idea The two incidence counts express Euler's formula using one edge count.
\writeup Each face contributes $p$ edge incidences and each edge is counted twice, so $pF=2E$. At vertices, $qV=2E$. Therefore
\[
2=V-E+F=E\left(\frac2q-1+\frac2p\right).
\]
Since $E>0$, the bracket is positive, giving $1/p+1/q>1/2$. If both $p,q\ge4$, the sum is at most $1/2$, so one is $3$. With $p=3$ the other is $3,4$ or $5$; with $q=3$ the possibilities are the transposed pairs. The full list is $(3,3),(3,4),(4,3),(3,5),(5,3)$, the tetrahedral, octahedral, cubical, icosahedral and dodecahedral types. These are the five possible Platonic types; realizing the familiar regular solids gives each one, while the incidence inequality alone establishes the restriction on the pairs.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:football}
\idea Euler's count depends on polygon degrees, not on their angles or shapes.
\writeup Put $F=f_5+f_6$. Counting gives $2E=5f_5+6f_6$ and $3V=2E$. Substituting in Euler,
\[
2=\frac{5f_5+6f_6}{3}-\frac{5f_5+6f_6}{2}+f_5+f_6
=\frac{f_5}{6}.
\]
Hence $f_5=12$, independently of $f_6$. The familiar truncated-icosahedral football has twenty hexagons as well as twelve pentagons, and the carbon framework of C$_{60}$ has that combinatorial arrangement. Equal defects were neither assumed nor needed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{geometry:ex:nomap}
\idea Five distances force a planar median, while spherical geometry gives a different value.
\writeup The spherical reflection exchanging $B,C$ fixes $A$ and the short-arc midpoint $M$, so $AM\perp BC$. The spherical cosine law of Appendix~I gives
\[
AM=\arccos\left(\frac{\cos a}{\cos(a/2)}\right).
\]
Such small equilateral spherical triangles exist: put $B,C$ at colatitude $a$ and choose their longitude separation $\phi$ with $\cos\phi=\cos a/(1+\cos a)$; the cosine law then gives $BC=a$.

If a map preserved $BC=a$ and $BM=CM=a/2$, equality in the Euclidean triangle inequality would put the image of $M$ at the segment midpoint of the images of $B,C$. The equal distances $AB=AC=a$ would then force the image distance $AM$ to be $\sqrt3\,a/2$. But
\[
\frac{\cos a}{\cos(a/2)}
=1-\frac38a^2-\frac1{128}a^4+O(a^6),\qquad
\cos(\sqrt3\,a/2)
=1-\frac38a^2+\frac3{128}a^4+O(a^6).
\]
Their difference is $-a^4/32+O(a^6)$, strictly negative for every sufficiently small positive $a$. Since cosine is strictly decreasing near these positive distances, the spherical median is strictly longer. An all-distance-preserving map on a neighborhood would preserve this sixth distance as well, a contradiction. Arbitrarily small such triangles fit inside any neighborhood, so the local obstruction follows. A decimal example would illustrate the discrepancy but could not prove it for all sufficiently small $a$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-app-k-offset-curvature}
\idea Differentiate the offset before changing its parameter to arc length.
\writeup (a) Since $N'=-\kappa T$, we have $\gamma_a'=(1-a\kappa)T$. With positive factor, its speed is $1-a\kappa$ and its unit tangent is $T$. Therefore $ds_a=(1-a\kappa)ds$ and
$\kappa_a=d\theta/ds_a=\kappa/(1-a\kappa)$.
(b) At a zero of that factor the displayed parametrization has zero velocity, so the regular curvature formula is unavailable. A counterclockwise radius-$R$ circle has inward left normal and $\gamma_a$ is a radius-$(R-a)$ circle for $a<R$. At $a=R$ it collapses to its centre, rather than becoming a regular curve of infinite curvature.
(c) For $a<0$ and $\kappa>0$ the factor stays positive. Integrating gives $L_a=L-a\int\kappa\,ds=L-2\pi a$. Here $\int\kappa\,ds=2\pi$ uses the quoted turning theorem for the positively oriented simple closed convex curve. It is a legitimate use of an explicitly quoted result, not a proof of that theorem. The formula agrees with the parallel-perimeter computation in Appendix~J.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-app-k-turning-not-closure}
\idea Returning the tangent to its starting value is one condition; cancelling the net displacement is another.
\writeup (a) The tangent angle is $\theta(s)=s+\eta\sin s$, so $\theta(2\pi)-\theta(0)=2\pi$ and the unit tangents at both ends are $1$. Curvature is positive because $1-|\eta|>0$.
(b) The curve is $\gamma(s)=\int_0^s e^{i(u+\eta\sin u)}du$. Uniform Taylor expansion on $[0,2\pi]$ gives
\[
\gamma(2\pi)=\int_0^{2\pi}e^{iu}(1+i\eta\sin u+O(\eta^2))du
=-\pi\eta+O(\eta^2).
\]
Indeed $ie^{iu}\sin u=(e^{2iu}-1)/2$, whose integral is $-\pi$. The bounded interval preserves the uniform remainder order.
(c) Divide the displacement by $\eta$: the limit as $\eta\to0$, $\eta\ne0$, is $-\pi$. Thus for all sufficiently small nonzero $\eta$ the displacement cannot vanish, even though the curvature is periodic and the end tangents agree. The prescribed-curvature theorem guarantees a curve, not closure of that curve.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-app-k-pyramid-limit}
\idea The apex angle follows from one isosceles face; Descartes determines the remaining total.
\writeup (a) A base edge subtends $2\pi/n$ at the base centre and has length $2\sin\beta\sin(\pi/n)$. A face has equal slant sides $1$, so its apex angle is
\[
\alpha_n=2\arcsin(\sin\beta\sin(\pi/n)),\qquad
\delta_n=2\pi-n\alpha_n.
\]
(b) Since $\sin t/t\to1$ and $\arcsin t/t\to1$ at zero, $n\alpha_n\to2\pi\sin\beta$. The limiting defect is $2\pi(1-\sin\beta)$. A cone with slant length $1$ and base radius $\sin\beta$ develops into a radius-$1$ sector with arc length $2\pi\sin\beta$, hence sector angle $2\pi\sin\beta$ and precisely this missing angle.
(c) Descartes gives base total $4\pi-\delta_n=2\pi+n\alpha_n$, tending to $2\pi(1+\sin\beta)$. Symmetry makes every base vertex defect equal to $(2\pi+n\alpha_n)/n$, which tends to zero. The number of base vertices tends to infinity, so their sum need not tend to zero with each summand; here the positive limiting curvature is distributed around the base rim.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-app-k-noncompact-gauss}
\idea The graph's normals cover a hemisphere rather than the whole sphere.
\writeup (a) The graph area density is $\sqrt D$ and $N=(-ax,-by,1)/\sqrt D$. Differentiation, or projecting the derivatives of $(-ax,-by,1)$ perpendicular to $N$, gives
\[
N\cdot(N_x\times N_y)=\frac{ab}{D^{3/2}}.
\]
Dividing the oriented spherical area density by the graph area density gives the Gauss-map Jacobian $K=ab/D^2$.
(b) If $ab\ne0$, the variables $u=ax,v=by$ range over $\R^2$ and $dx\,dy=du\,dv/|ab|$. Thus
\[
\int K\,dA
=\operatorname{sgn}(ab)\int_{\R^2}\frac{du\,dv}{(1+u^2+v^2)^{3/2}}
=2\pi\operatorname{sgn}(ab).
\]
The radial integral is $\int_0^\infty r(1+r^2)^{-3/2}dr=1$. The Gauss map is a bijection onto the open upper hemisphere: for a normal $(p,q,r)$ with $r>0$, its inverse has $x=-p/(ar)$ and $y=-q/(br)$. Its orientation is preserved for $ab>0$ and reversed for $ab<0$.
(c) If $ab=0$, $K$ is identically zero. The normal image lies in a great semicircle when exactly one coefficient is nonzero, or is a single point when both vanish, and its spherical area is zero. None of these entire graphs is compact. In the saddle case convexity and positive curvature also fail. For $a,b>0$ the graph is convex but noncompact, explaining why its total is $2\pi$ rather than $4\pi$.
\end{solution}
