\chapter{Vector Fields and Flows}

\begin{center}\itshape
Place an arrow at each point of a space and follow it. The arrows give a differential equation; its solutions move points in a flow. Two such motions need not commute. A short loop measures their discrepancy, and its leading term is the Lie bracket. On an even-dimensional sphere, topology forces at least one arrow to be zero.
\end{center}

Sophus Lie was working in Paris with Felix Klein when war broke out between France and Prussia in July 1870. Klein left; Lie later walked towards Italy and was arrested near Fontainebleau, suspected of being a German spy. Stubhaug's account describes the unfamiliar mathematical notes that helped arouse suspicion and Darboux's intervention to obtain his release after about a month. Lie continued working during the imprisonment.

His mathematical project was to understand symmetries of differential equations. Galois theory had used finite groups to study algebraic equations; Lie studied transformations depending continuously on parameters. A vector field gives an infinitesimal direction of motion, its flow produces actual transformations, and brackets record how the directions interact. Chapter~7 studies the resulting groups. Here we build the differential-equation tools that make those motions well defined.

Existence, uniqueness and dependence on initial data come first. They let us turn flow times into coordinates and prove Frobenius' theorem. At the end, Milnor's volume argument gives the hairy ball theorem: a hypothetical nowhere-zero field would make an odd power of $1+t^2$ behave like the square of a polynomial, which is impossible.

\section{Differential equations in \texorpdfstring{$\R^n$}{R\^{}n}}

Let $U\subset\R^n$ be open and $F:U\to\R^n$ be $C^1$. An \emph{integral curve}, or solution, is a $C^1$ map $x:J\to U$ on an interval with $x'=F(x)$. Its initial value specifies where the motion starts. A jointly $C^1$ time-dependent equation can be made autonomous by adding time as a coordinate with derivative $1$; later we shall separately treat a linear equation whose time coefficients are merely continuous.

On a closed ball contained in $U$, a bound $L$ on $\|DF\|$ and the mean-value inequality of Lemma~\ref{lem:mvi} give $|F(x)-F(y)|\le L|x-y|$. This \emph{Lipschitz bound} controls how far nearby trajectories can separate.

\begin{lemma}[Gr\"onwall]\label{lem:gronwall}
If $u:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous, $a,b\ge0$, and $u(t)\le a+b\int_0^t u(s)ds$, then $u(t)\le ae^{bt}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $w(t)=a+b\int_0^t u(s)ds$. Then $u\le w$ and $w'=bu\le bw$. Hence $(e^{-bt}w)'\le0$ and $w(t)\le w(0)e^{bt}=ae^{bt}$. This also covers $a=0$, $b=0$ and $T=0$ without division.
\end{proof}

We shall also need a compact-set version of this fact. A $C^1$ map is Lipschitz on every compact subset $K$ of its domain, even if $K$ is not convex. Otherwise choose pairs $x_j,y_j\in K$ with $|F(x_j)-F(y_j)|>j|x_j-y_j|$. Boundedness of $F$ on $K$ forces $|x_j-y_j|\to0$. Compactness gives a subsequence on which both points tend to the same point $z\in K$. They eventually lie in one small convex ball about $z$ where the derivative bound gives a fixed Lipschitz constant, a contradiction. This proof uses local derivative bounds, not a segment outside the domain.

\begin{theorem}[Picard--Lindel\"of]\label{thm:picard}
Let $x_0\in U$, $b>0$, and $\bar B(x_0,b)\subset U$. Let $M=\max_{\bar B}|F|$ and let $L\ge0$ be a Lipschitz constant there. Put
\[
\tau=\min\left(\frac bM,\frac1{2L}\right),
\]
interpreting either quotient with zero denominator as $\infty$. There is a solution on $[-\tau,\tau]$ taking values in that ball and starting at $x_0$; $\tau=\infty$ means all real times. Any two solutions on the same interval containing zero with the same initial value agree.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $M=0$, $F$ vanishes on the ball, so the constant curve $x=x_0$ is a solution for all times. This handles the possibly infinite $\tau$ directly. Otherwise $\tau$ is finite and positive. On the complete space $\mathcal C$ of continuous maps $[-\tau,\tau]\to\bar B(x_0,b)$, with uniform distance, define
\[
(Tx)(t)=x_0+\int_0^tF(x(s))ds.
\]
The displacement is at most $M|t|\le b$, so $T$ stays in $\mathcal C$. Also $\|Tx-Ty\|_\infty\le L\tau\|x-y\|_\infty\le\tfrac12\|x-y\|_\infty$. Banach's fixed point theorem gives $x=Tx$. The integral of its continuous integrand is differentiable, proving the equation and initial value.

For uniqueness, take any finite time segment in the common interval. The union of the two trajectory images is compact, and $F$ is Lipschitz on this compact set by the compact-set fact proved above. Thus $u(t)=|x(t)-y(t)|$ satisfies Gr\"onwall with $a=0$ and is zero. Reverse time for a negative segment. Every time in the interval lies in one of these finite segments.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
For $x'=\sqrt{|x|}$, the constant solution can wait at zero until any time $c\ge0$, then follow $(t-c)^2/4$. Which hypothesis above rules out this behavior for a $C^1$ field? Exercise~\ref{ex:sqrtuniqueness} compares it with a different non-Lipschitz equation that still has unique solutions.
\end{think}

Solutions through an initial point agree on their overlaps, so their union gives a \emph{maximal solution} $\varphi(t,x_0)$ on an interval $J(x_0)$ containing zero. This interval is open: at every finite endpoint that is reached inside $U$, local existence extends the curve. The \emph{flow domain} is $\mathcal D=\{(t,x):x\in U,\ t\in J(x)\}$, and the map $\varphi$ on it is the flow.

\begin{proposition}[escape lemma]\label{prop:escape}
If the upper endpoint $\beta$ of $J(x_0)$ is finite, the solution eventually stays outside every compact $K\subset U$. In particular, it cannot stay in one compact subset for all $0\le t<\beta$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a nonempty compact $K$, choose $b>0$ so that its closed $b$-neighborhood $K_b$ is compact and contained in $U$. Bounds for $|F|$ and a Lipschitz constant on $K_b$ give one positive local existence time for every start in $K$, since each ball $\bar B(y,b)$ lies in $K_b$. If the quantitative time is infinite, choose any positive finite one.

If the trajectory visited $K$ arbitrarily close to $\beta$, choose such a visit at $t_1\in[0,\beta)$ with $\beta-t_1$ less than half this common time. The local solution starting there agrees with the original on their overlap by uniqueness and extends it beyond $\beta$, impossible. Thus visits cease before $\beta$. The empty compact set needs no argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[continuous dependence]\label{thm:flowcont}
The domain $\mathcal D$ is open and the flow is continuous on it. If $[0,T]\subset J(x_0)$, there are $\delta>0$ and $L\ge0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $[0,T]\subset J(x)$ and
\[
|\varphi(t,x)-\varphi(t,x_0)|\le e^{Lt}|x-x_0|\quad(0\le t\le T).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The reference trajectory $K=\varphi([0,T],x_0)$ is compact. Take $b>0$ with $K_b\subset U$ and a Lipschitz constant $L$ on this compact tube. Put $\delta=(b/2)e^{-LT}$. While a trajectory starting within $\delta$ stays in $K_b$, the integral equation and Gr\"onwall give
$|\varphi(t,x)-\varphi(t,x_0)|\le e^{Lt}|x-x_0|<b/2$.

Let $t^*$ be the supremum of times up to $T$ through which it exists and remains in $K_b$. Local existence makes this set nonempty. The estimate keeps the trajectory in the smaller compact tube $K_{b/2}$ until $t^*$. The escape lemma prevents $t^*$ from being a finite maximal endpoint; continuity then puts its value in the smaller tube. If $t^*<T$, that tube's margin inside $K_b$ would allow a further short time in $K_b$, contradicting the supremum. Thus $t^*=T$ and the estimate holds through the endpoint. For $T=0$ the conclusion is immediate. The same argument for $-F$ handles negative times.

For $(t_0,x_0)\in\mathcal D$, choose a slightly longer compact time interval inside $J(x_0)$ containing both zero and $t_0$. The estimates on its positive and negative parts give a product neighborhood of $(t_0,x_0)$ in $\mathcal D$, proving openness. Finally split
$|\varphi(t,x)-\varphi(t_0,x_0)|$ into its change in initial point at fixed $t$ and its change along the reference trajectory. The estimate controls the first uniformly in nearby $t$, and continuity of that trajectory controls the second, proving joint continuity.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Smooth dependence on the initial point}

A small perturbation of the initial point evolves, to first order, by a linear equation along the reference trajectory. We must construct this linear equation even though its time coefficients need only be continuous.

\begin{theorem}[smooth dependence]\label{thm:flowsmooth}
If $F$ is $C^k$, $1\le k\le\infty$, its flow is $C^k$ on $\mathcal D$. The derivative in initial data, $Y(t)=D_x\varphi(t,x)$, is the unique solution of
\[
Y'=DF(\varphi(t,x))Y,\qquad Y(0)=I.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a compact reference time interval $[0,T]$ and a tube as in continuous dependence; nearby trajectories stay in it. For each nearby start let $A_x(t)=DF(\varphi(t,x))$, a continuous matrix function bounded by some $M$.

First solve $Y=I+\int_0^t A_x(s)Y(s)ds$ directly. On $C([0,h],M_n(\R))$ this integral operator is a contraction for $Mh<1$; if $M=0$ it is constant. The space is complete, and no differentiability of $A_x$ in time is needed. Finitely many such steps construct the unique solution on $[0,T]$. Gr\"onwall gives $\|Y(t)\|\le e^{Mt}$. Negative time is treated in the same way. This constructs the candidate derivative with continuous time coefficients; no time derivative of $A_x$ is assumed.

For differentiability, put $u(t)=\varphi(t,x_0+h)-\varphi(t,x_0)$. Uniform continuity of $DF$ on the compact tube gives a modulus $\omega(r)\to0$ and
\[
|F(a+u)-F(a)-DF(a)u|\le\omega(|u|)|u|
\]
when the segment lies in the tube. Here $a$ is the reference trajectory point in $K$, and $|u|\le e^{LT}|h|<b/2$, so the entire segment does lie in its radius-$b$ ball. Let $r(t)=u(t)-Y_{x_0}(t)h$. It starts at zero and satisfies
\[
r'=A_{x_0}r+\rho(t),\qquad
|\rho(t)|\le\omega(e^{LT}|h|)e^{LT}|h|.
\]
Its integral equation and Gr\"onwall give
$\sup_{0\le t\le T}|r(t)|\le Te^{MT}e^{LT}\omega(e^{LT}|h|)|h|=o(|h|)$.
This is the required Fr\'echet derivative, uniformly on the time segment.

For its continuity, uniform convergence of nearby trajectories and uniform continuity of $DF$ give $\eta(x)=\sup_t\|A_x(t)-A_{x_0}(t)\|\to0$. The difference $Z=Y_x-Y_{x_0}$ satisfies
$Z'=A_xZ+(A_x-A_{x_0})Y_{x_0}$, $Z(0)=0$.
Gr\"onwall bounds its uniform norm by $T\eta(x)e^{MT}\max_t\|Y_{x_0}(t)\|$, tending to zero. Splitting $Y_x(t)-Y_{x_0}(t_0)$ into this uniform difference and $Y_{x_0}(t)-Y_{x_0}(t_0)$ proves joint continuity. The time derivative $\partial_t\varphi=F(\varphi)$ is continuous as well, so Proposition~\ref{prop:partials} gives $C^1$ dependence.

For higher regularity, induct on $k$. If $F$ is $C^k$, $k\ge2$, the autonomous field $G(x,Y)=(F(x),DF(x)Y)$ is $C^{k-1}$. Its flow is $(\varphi(t,x),Y_x(t)Y_0)$ by the linear uniqueness just proved; it exists on each compact time interval of the $x$-trajectory. The induction hypothesis makes this map $C^{k-1}$, hence $D_x\varphi$ is $C^{k-1}$ by setting $Y_0=I$. Also $\partial_t\varphi=F\circ\varphi$ is $C^{k-1}$. All first partials are therefore $C^{k-1}$, proving $C^k$. Applying every finite step gives the smooth case.
\end{proof}

The variational equation measures amplification of initial error. Exponential growth signals instability, but does not by itself establish chaotic dynamics: the elementary equation $x'=x$ already has derivative $e^t$. The upright pendulum $\theta''+\sin\theta=0$ likewise has linearized equation $\delta''=\delta$ near $\theta=\pi$ and exponentially growing solutions. Behavior near other trajectories can differ; a local amplification estimate is only part of the dynamics.

\section{Vector fields on manifolds and their flows}

\begin{definition}
A smooth \emph{vector field} assigns $X_p\in T_pM$ at every point, with smooth chart coefficients $X=\sum_iX^i\partial_{x^i}$. It acts on smooth functions by $(Xf)(p)=X_p(f)$. An \emph{integral curve} satisfies $\gamma'(t)=X_{\gamma(t)}$, with its velocity defined by the differential of the curve.
\end{definition}

In a chart this is the equation already studied, so solutions exist and are locally unique. Two curves agreeing at one time agree throughout their common interval: their agreement set is open by local uniqueness and closed because a Hausdorff manifold has closed diagonal. It is nonempty and the interval is connected. Each point therefore has a maximal curve with time interval $J(p)$.

Write its value as $\varphi_t(p)$. If $s\in J(p)$, shifting this curve by $s$ gives the maximal curve through $\varphi_s(p)$, so $J(\varphi_s(p))=J(p)-s$ and
$\varphi_t(\varphi_s(p))=\varphi_{t+s}(p)$ whenever $s,s+t\in J(p)$. These domain conditions are part of the flow identity. A field is \emph{complete} if $J(p)=\R$ for every point.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:complete}
Every smooth vector field on a compact manifold is complete, with smooth flow on $\R\times M$. Each $\varphi_t$ is a diffeomorphism inverse to $\varphi_{-t}$, and $t\mapsto\varphi_t$ is a homomorphism from $(\R,+)$ to the diffeomorphism group. The same conclusions hold on any manifold for a smooth vector field vanishing outside a compact set.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Local openness and smoothness of chart flows give, near each point $p$, a neighborhood $V_p$ and time $\varepsilon_p>0$ valid for every start in $V_p$. A finite collection covers compact $M$, giving a common positive time $\varepsilon$. For a compactly supported field use a finite cover of its support; all points outside the support have constant curves. If the manifold or support is empty, any positive common time works. In dimension zero all tangent fields are zero and all flows are the identity, also covered directly.

If a maximal interval had finite upper end $\beta$, choose $t_1\in[0,\beta)$ with $\beta-t_1<\varepsilon/2$. The uniform local solution from $\varphi_{t_1}(p)$ would extend it past $\beta$. The chosen time is inside the actual interval; no subtraction is assumed to stay there. Reverse time for a finite lower end. Thus all curves exist for all real times.

The flow is smooth for $|t|<\varepsilon$. Write any fixed $t_0$ as $N$ equal increments shorter than $\varepsilon$ and compose those smooth maps, proving $\varphi_{t_0}$ smooth. Near $t_0$ use $\varphi_t(p)=\varphi_{t-t_0}(\varphi_{t_0}(p))$ to get joint smoothness. Finally the flow identity gives both inverse and homomorphism assertions.
\end{proof}

On a noncompact manifold, local smoothness on the general flow domain follows by dividing a compact trajectory segment into finitely many arcs lying in charts. Each chart flow is smooth on a product neighborhood of its starting point and short time. Choose the arcs with small overlaps, then shrink the starting neighborhood so successive images remain in these chart neighborhoods. Their finite composition gives the flow near the chosen endpoint and allows a small extra time. This proves both domain openness and smoothness there. It is the local counterpart of the complete-flow argument.

\begin{example}[linear flows on the torus]\label{ex:torusflow}
On $T^2=\R^2/\Z^2$, the field $X=\partial_{\theta_1}+\alpha\partial_{\theta_2}$ has flow $\varphi_t(\theta)=\theta+t(1,\alpha)$ modulo integers. If $\alpha=p/q$ in lowest terms with $q>0$, the least positive period is $q$: a period must be an integer from its first coordinate, and the second then requires divisibility by $q$.

For irrational $\alpha$, integer times are dense on the vertical circle through the starting point by the irrational-rotation result of Volume~5, Corollary~6.14. To reach any other first coordinate, use times $k+s$ with one fixed $s\in[0,1)$; the second coordinates still contain the dense sequence $k\alpha$ modulo $1$. Thus the whole orbit is dense in $T^2$. Its parametrization is injective, since a repeated point would give an integer $t\ne0$ with $t\alpha$ integral, and it is an immersion because its velocity is nonzero. It is not an embedded submanifold: a submanifold is locally closed in a chart, whereas a proper dense one-dimensional subset could then be both closed and dense in a two-dimensional chart, forcing it to contain that chart. The flow gives a geometric example of the distinction from Chapter~1.
\end{example}

\section{The Lie bracket}

A vector field differentiates functions, so $X(Yf)$ makes sense. It usually involves second derivatives and is not itself a vector field. Subtract the opposite order and those second derivatives cancel.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bracket}
For smooth fields $X,Y$, $[X,Y]f=X(Yf)-Y(Xf)$ defines a smooth vector field, with chart coefficients
\[
[X,Y]^i=\sum_j(X^j\partial_jY^i-Y^j\partial_jX^i).
\]
Thus in Euclidean coordinates $[X,Y]=DY\cdot X-DX\cdot Y$. The bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric and satisfies
$[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The commutator is linear on functions. Expanding it on a product cancels the two cross terms and gives the Leibniz rule, so at each point it is a tangent derivation. In coordinates,
$X(Yf)=\sum_{i,j}X^j(\partial_jY^i)\partial_if+\sum_{i,j}X^jY^i\partial_j\partial_if$.
The second sum cancels with its counterpart for $Y(Xf)$ because mixed partials agree. The remaining coefficients are the displayed smooth functions. Bilinearity and antisymmetry follow from subtraction. For Jacobi, the three expanded terms are
\[
\begin{aligned}
&XYZ-XZY-YZX+ZYX,\\
&YZX-YXZ-ZXY+XZY,\\
&ZXY-ZYX-XYZ+YXZ.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each composition cancels with its negative, proving the identity as operators on every smooth function.
\end{proof}

The sign convention can be remembered from $X=\partial_x$ and $Y=x\partial_y$: $[X,Y]=\partial_y$. Move right before moving vertically and the upward motion is stronger. The loop below makes that comparison precise.

\begin{theorem}[the bracket as a small loop]\label{thm:bracketloop}
For fields $X,Y$ with local flows and a point $p$, define for sufficiently small $s$
$\psi(s)=\varphi^Y_{-s}\circ\varphi^X_{-s}\circ\varphi^Y_s\circ\varphi^X_s(p)$. Then every smooth function satisfies
\[
f(\psi(s))=f(p)+s^2[X,Y]_pf+O(s^3).
\]
In a chart, $\psi(s)=p+s^2[X,Y]_p+O(s^3)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define the smooth scalar function
$H(a,b,c,d)=f(\varphi^Y_d\circ\varphi^X_c\circ\varphi^Y_b\circ\varphi^X_a(p))$ near zero. Let the four fields in this point order be $V_1=X,V_2=Y,V_3=X,V_4=Y$. Its first partial derivative in the $i$th variable at zero is $V_if(p)$, and its repeated second partial is $V_i^2f(p)$. For $i<j$, differentiating the two-flow restriction gives mixed partial $V_i(V_jf)(p)$. Pullback acts on functions in the reverse order to point transformations; this is why the earlier point step is the outer derivation here.

Put $\varepsilon=(1,1,-1,-1)$ and apply Taylor along $(s,s,-s,-s)$. The linear coefficient is $X+Y-X-Y=0$. Twice the quadratic coefficient is
\[
Q=2X^2+2Y^2+2(XY-X^2-XY-YX-Y^2+XY)=2(XY-YX).
\]
This gives the scalar formula with its cubic Taylor remainder. Apply it to each coordinate function for the vector formula. Smallness ensures every intermediate point stays in a common local flow neighborhood; completeness is unnecessary.
\end{proof}

A diffeomorphism transports a vector field by its derivative: $(F_*Y)_{F(p)}=dF_p(Y_p)$. To compare $Y$ at different points of an $X$-trajectory, transport it \emph{back} to the same tangent space at $p$.

\begin{theorem}[Lie derivative]\label{thm:liederiv}
If $X$ is complete with flow $\varphi_t$, then for any smooth field $Y$,
\[
[X,Y]_p=\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_{t=0}
 d(\varphi_{-t})_{\varphi_t(p)}Y_{\varphi_t(p)}.
\]
At general time $t$, the derivative of this vector-valued curve in $T_pM$ is
$d(\varphi_{-t})_{\varphi_t(p)}[X,Y]_{\varphi_t(p)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In a chart near $p$, smooth dependence and the variational equation give
$D\varphi_{-t}(\varphi_t(p))=I-tDX(p)+O(t^2)$.
Also $Y(\varphi_t(p))=Y(p)+tDY(p)X(p)+O(t^2)$. Multiplication gives
$Y(p)+t(DY\cdot X-DX\cdot Y)(p)+O(t^2)$, whose derivative is the bracket coefficient.

For general time fix $q=\varphi_t(p)$. The flow identity expresses the vector at $t+h$ as $d\varphi_{-t}$ applied to $d\varphi_{-h}(Y_{\varphi_h(q)})$. The first derivative map is fixed and linear as $h$ varies. Differentiate at $h=0$ and apply the zero-time result at $q$, obtaining the stated transported bracket.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[commuting flows]\label{thm:commuting}
For complete smooth fields $X,Y$, their flows commute for all real times if and only if $[X,Y]=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If they commute, the small loop is exactly the identity. Divide its scalar expansion by $s^2$ and let $s\to0$ to obtain $[X,Y]_pf=0$ for every $p,f$.

Conversely, a zero bracket makes the transported field in the Lie-derivative theorem constant. Hence $d\varphi^X_t(Y_p)=Y_{\varphi^X_t(p)}$. For fixed $t$, the curve $s\mapsto\varphi^X_t(\varphi^Y_s(q))$ has velocity $Y$ at its current point and starts at $\varphi^X_t(q)$. Uniqueness of integral curves identifies it with $\varphi^Y_s(\varphi^X_t(q))$, proving commutation.
\end{proof}

The arguments also work locally: if the bracket vanishes on a neighborhood, the flows commute for sufficiently small times and nearby initial points while all the intermediate curves stay there. This local form will produce coordinates.

\section{Frobenius' theorem}

One nonzero field can become a coordinate direction. Several chosen fields need not all become coordinate directions, because coordinate derivatives commute. A family of tangent planes asks a more flexible question: can some smooth basis for those planes become coordinate directions?

\begin{definition}
A smooth \emph{distribution} of rank $k$, $0\le k\le n$, assigns a $k$-dimensional subspace $\Delta_p\subset T_pM$ that is locally spanned by $k$ smooth fields. It is \emph{involutive} if brackets of fields taking values in $\Delta$ still take values in $\Delta$. It is \emph{integrable} if every point has coordinates in which $\Delta$ is spanned by $\partial_{x^1},\ldots,\partial_{x^k}$.
\end{definition}

In such coordinates, fixing the remaining $n-k$ coordinates gives the \emph{integral manifolds}: their tangent spaces are the assigned planes. Rank is constant in this definition; a span whose dimension drops at special points is a different problem.

\begin{theorem}[Frobenius]\label{thm:frobenius}
A smooth constant-rank distribution is integrable if and only if it is involutive.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
In integral coordinates, fields in $\Delta$ have only their first $k$ components. The bracket formula cannot create a later component, so the distribution is involutive. Rank zero is already integrable in every chart and has only the zero field; this includes dimension zero.

For the converse with $k>0$, work near $p=0$ in a Euclidean chart. Choose a linear change so projection $\pi$ onto the first $k$ coordinates restricts to an isomorphism on $\Delta_0$. In a local spanning basis $Z_1,\ldots,Z_k$, their first-component matrix is invertible at zero and remains so nearby. Its smooth inverse constructs the unique fields $X_i\in\Delta$ with $\pi X_i=e_i$:
\[
X_i=\partial_i+\sum_{l>k}a_i^l\partial_l.
\]
Their first $k$ components are constants, so $[X_i,X_j]$ has zero projection. Involutivity puts it in $\Delta$, where projection is injective, so it is zero. This special choice of basis, rather than the originally supplied basis, commutes.

For small $t\in\R^k$ and $y\in\R^{n-k}$, define
\[
\Phi(t,y)=\varphi^{X_1}_{t_1}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi^{X_k}_{t_k}(0,y).
\]
Local commutation moves any chosen flow to the front, giving $\partial_{t_i}\Phi=X_i(\Phi)$. At zero the derivative matrix has first $k$ columns $X_i(0)$ and remaining columns $(0,e_j)$. Its block form has identity blocks on the diagonal and zero upper-right block, hence is invertible. The inverse function theorem makes $\Phi$ a local diffeomorphism. Its inverse coordinates carry $X_i$ to $\partial_{t_i}$, so their span is the required coordinate distribution. For $k=n$ the transverse variable is absent and the same argument applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The test uses brackets, but the proof constructs the integral manifolds. First normalize the fields by a coordinate projection; involutivity then forces their brackets to vanish. Following the resulting commuting flows makes the flow times into coordinates. This explains why an integrable distribution can start with a noncommuting basis: the basis may need changing, while its planes stay fixed.
\end{beauty}

\begin{example}[a non-integrable plane field]\label{ex:contact}
In $\R^3$, let $Y_1=\partial_y$ and $Y_2=\partial_x+y\partial_z$. Their span has rank two and consists of vectors $v$ satisfying $v_z-yv_x=0$. This covector is often written $\dd z-y\dd x$; Chapter~3 will develop the differential-form notation. Its bracket is $[Y_1,Y_2]=\partial_z$, which is outside the plane at every point. No local surface can be tangent to these planes: fields tangent to a submanifold have bracket tangent there, since they and their commutator kill its local defining functions. Here that would require the missing $\partial_z$ direction to be tangent as well.

The small-loop formula shows how a loop of allowed motions gains a displacement in this missing direction. In this three-dimensional example the two fields and their bracket span every tangent direction. Nonintegrability alone in an arbitrary larger space would not imply that full spanning property. Exercise~\ref{ex:parking} computes an idealized vehicle model in which the distinction can be seen directly.
\end{example}

\section{The hairy ball theorem}

An odd-dimensional unit sphere $S^{2m-1}\subset\C^m$, $m\ge1$, has the nowhere-zero tangent field $v(x)=ix$. Its flow is $x\mapsto e^{it}x$, whose velocity has unit length and is perpendicular to the radius. An even-dimensional sphere has no such field. Poincaré's surface work and Brouwer's higher-dimensional result precede the following proof of Milnor from 1978.

\begin{theorem}[hairy ball]\label{thm:hairyball}
For every even $n\ge0$, every continuous tangent vector field on $S^n$ vanishes somewhere.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $n=0$, both tangent spaces of $S^0$ are zero, so every tangent field is zero. Now let $n\ge2$ be even and set $N=n+1$, odd. Suppose a continuous nowhere-zero tangent field exists.

Compactness gives a positive minimum length $m$. Approximate it uniformly within $m/2$ on the sphere by a polynomial vector map, using Stone--Weierstrass from Volume~7, Theorem~4.19. Project that map onto $x^\perp$ at each unit $x$. Orthogonal projection does not increase the approximation error to a tangent vector, so the new smooth tangent field is still nonzero. Normalize it to length one and call it $v$.

Extend it to nonzero ambient points by $v(rx)=rv(x)$ for $|x|=1,r>0$. This is smooth, perpendicular to its argument, and has the same length as that argument. On the shell $A=\{1/2<|x|<3/2\}$ put $f_t(x)=x+tv(x)$. Pythagoras gives
$|f_t(x)|=\sqrt{1+t^2}|x|$.

For small $t$, this map is a diffeomorphism onto $\sqrt{1+t^2}A$. Indeed, $v$ is smooth on a neighborhood of the compact closure of the shell, hence Lipschitz there with some $L$. Thus $|f_t(x)-f_t(y)|\ge(1-|t|L)|x-y|$, proving injectivity for small $t$. The derivative $I+tDv$ is uniformly invertible there, with positive determinant, by continuity and compactness. On the unit sphere, $g_t=f_t/\sqrt{1+t^2}$ has invertible tangent differential. Its image is open by the inverse function theorem and closed by compactness. Since $S^n$ is connected for $n\ge2$, that image is all of $S^n$. Homogeneity now supplies every radius in the target shell. Local smooth inverses combine with injectivity to give the claimed diffeomorphism.

Change of variables gives
\[
|f_t(A)|=\int_A\det(I+tDv(x))dx.
\]
The right side is a polynomial in $t$, since the determinant has finitely many powers and bounded continuous coefficients on the shell. The left side is $(1+t^2)^{N/2}|A|$. Divide by $|A|>0$ to get a polynomial $R(t)$ agreeing with $(1+t^2)^{N/2}$ on a small interval. Squaring gives the polynomial identity $R(t)^2=(1+t^2)^N$. Over $\C$, its right side has root $i$ of odd multiplicity $N$, while a square has even root multiplicities. This contradiction rules out the field.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Following the hypothetical hair moves a shell to a uniformly enlarged shell. A Jacobian determinant measures that motion by a polynomial, while the enlargement factor measures it by $(1+t^2)^{N/2}$. The parity obstruction appears in these two volume calculations. No homology calculation is needed for this proof; Chapter~6 will give another approach.
\end{beauty}

\begin{remark}
An idealized continuous horizontal wind field on a spherical Earth must have a zero. The higher question of several independent fields is harder. We quote Adams' 1962 theorem: if $n=2^{4a+b}c$ with $c$ odd and $0\le b\le3$, the greatest number of independent tangent fields on $S^{n-1}$ is $2^b+8a-1$. Among positive-dimensional spheres, only $S^1,S^3,S^7$ admit a full tangent frame. The zero-dimensional sphere has its trivial empty frame. Complex numbers, quaternions and octonions explain the familiar constructions in those three dimensions; the maximality theorem is not proved here.
\end{remark}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The field $\partial_x$ and the field $x\partial_y$ make the bracket concrete: four motions return the horizontal coordinate but leave a vertical displacement $s^2$. Commuting flows remove this discrepancy. In Frobenius' construction the remaining flow times then serve as coordinates. The examples let you check the signs before using the abstract statement.

The existence and dependence proofs have a separate job. They ensure that nearby points can all be moved for a common short time, and that differentiating this motion is legitimate. Compactness or compact support repeats that short motion indefinitely; finite escape shows what can go wrong without such a condition. On a sphere, even the attempt to choose nonzero directions everywhere faces the parity obstruction of Milnor's volume calculation.

The next chapter turns from following vectors to integrating differential forms. Their pullback records how measurements change under maps, and Stokes' theorem compares a derivative inside a region with a measurement on its boundary.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:noncomplete}
(a) Find every maximal solution and the flow domain of $x'=x^2$ on $\R$.
(b) Use the coordinate $u=-1/x$ near infinity to extend the field to $\R P^1=\R\cup\{\infty\}$. Describe its all-time projective flow, including what happens at infinity and at time zero.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:sqrtuniqueness}
(a) For every $c\ge0$, verify that the function zero for $t\le c$ and $(t-c)^2/4$ for $t\ge c$ is a $C^1$ solution of $x'=\sqrt{|x|}$ through zero.
(b) Explain the failure of the Lipschitz hypothesis.
(c) Show that $x'=x\log|x|$, with value zero assigned at $x=0$, has unique solutions for every initial value despite also failing to be Lipschitz at zero. Treat negative as well as positive initial values and rule out a solution leaving zero after a waiting time.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:noncommute}
For $X=\partial_x$, $Y=x\partial_y$, compute their flows, bracket and exact four-step loop from Theorem~\ref{thm:bracketloop}. Show that their span has rank two off $x=0$ and rank one on it, so is not a constant-rank distribution on the whole plane. For a starting point on that line, distinguish the intermediate excursion from the final displacement.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:linearfields}
For a real matrix $A$, put $X_A(x)=Ax$.
(a) Define $e^{tA}$ by its power series and prove it gives the complete flow.
(b) Prove $[X_A,X_B]=X_{BA-AB}$.
(c) Deduce that $e^{tA}$ and $e^{sB}$ commute for all $s,t$ exactly when $AB=BA$.
(d) Expand $e^{-sB}e^{-sA}e^{sB}e^{sA}$ to second order, and evaluate it for $A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, $B=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:parking}
An ideal unicycle has position and heading $(x,y,\theta)\in\R^2\times S^1$. Its driving field is $F=\cos\theta\,\partial_x+\sin\theta\,\partial_y$, and its turning-in-place field is $T=\partial_\theta$.
(a) Compute $[T,F]$.
(b) Starting at $(0,0,0)$, turn by $s$, drive for $s$, turn back by $s$, then drive backward for $s$. Find the exact endpoint and its expansion through cubic order.
(c) Show that the plane distribution spanned by these two fields is not integrable, and every smooth function preserved by both flows is constant. Keep the conclusions within this idealized control model.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:hairyodd}
(a) Verify that $v(x)=ix$ is a unit tangent field on $S^{2m-1}\subset\C^m$, $m\ge1$.
(b) On the unit quaternions $S^3$, prove that $xi,xj,xk$ are orthonormal tangent fields.
(c) Find their flows and show that the field $ix$ commutes with all three. Compute $[xi,xj]$ directly to see that the three right-multiplication fields do not commute with one another.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:straighten}
Prove directly that a smooth field with $X_p\ne0$ is $\partial_{x^1}$ in some chart near $p$, using a transverse slice and its flow. Give such coordinates for $X=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y$ near $(1,0)$, specifying a local angle branch.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch02-nonlinear-decay}
For $x'=-x^3$:
(a) find the maximal flow domain, including the equilibrium at zero;
(b) compute its derivative in the initial value and check the variational equation;
(c) find its forward decay for nonzero starts and explain why this attraction does not give a uniform exponential contraction near zero. Identify the finite-time escape in the opposite time direction.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch02-volume-jacobian}
For a smooth Euclidean field $F$, write $\operatorname{div}F=\sum_i\partial_iF^i$ and prove on its flow domain
\[
\det D_x\varphi(t,x)=\exp\left(\int_0^t\operatorname{div}F(\varphi(s,x))ds\right).
\]
Deduce that local flow maps preserve Lebesgue volume exactly when $\operatorname{div}F=0$. Apply this to the planar field $(H_y,-H_x)$ for smooth $H$: prove area preservation and conservation of $H$, then compute its flow for $H=(x^2+y^2)/2$. No differential-form machinery is needed.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch02-simultaneous-coordinates}
On an open rectangle take $X=\partial_x$ and $Y=a(x,y)\partial_x+b(x,y)\partial_y$, with smooth $a,b$ and $b\ne0$.
(a) Determine when they commute.
(b) In that case construct local coordinates $u,v$ making $X=\partial_u$ and $Y=\partial_v$ explicitly by one-variable integrals.
(c) Compare $Y=e^x\partial_y$. Its span with $X$ is the full tangent plane, an integrable distribution. Why can these particular two fields nevertheless not both become coordinate fields?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v9-ch02-torus-averages}
For the torus flow $\theta\mapsto\theta+t(1,\alpha)$:
(a) show that for irrational $\alpha$, every continuous invariant function is constant;
(b) for irrational $\alpha$, prove for every continuous $f$ that its time averages converge uniformly in the initial point to $\int_{T^2}f$, using finite Fourier sums and uniform approximation;
(c) for $\alpha=p/q$ in lowest terms, describe every smooth invariant function using a determinant-one integer change of coordinates, and give an example whose orbit average differs from the torus mean. This should use earlier Fourier and compact-space approximation tools, not a later ergodic theorem.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:noncomplete}
\idea Passing infinity changes the coordinate chart, not the smoothness of the projective motion.
\writeup (a) At a nonzero value, $(1/x)'=-1$, giving $\varphi(t,x_0)=x_0/(1-tx_0)$. For $x_0>0$ its maximal interval is $(-\infty,1/x_0)$; for $x_0<0$ it is $(1/x_0,\infty)$. The zero solution is constant for all times, so the joint domain is $tx_0<1$. The nonzero solutions escape in norm at the indicated finite endpoints and cannot extend in $\R$.
(b) In the chart $u=-1/x$, the chain rule gives $u'=x'/x^2=1$, smooth and nonzero at infinity. In homogeneous coordinates the global flow is
$[x:y]\mapsto[x:y-tx]$, invertible with inverse parameter $-t$ and smooth in charts for all $t$. On the affine chart it is the displayed fraction. At $t\ne0$ it sends $1/t$ to infinity and infinity to $-1/t$, and has only the fixed point zero. At $t=0$ it is the identity everywhere. A coordinate blowup on the line thus becomes an ordinary passage through another projective chart.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sqrtuniqueness}
\idea Finite arrival at an equilibrium permits waiting; the logarithmic equation never reaches zero in finite time.
\writeup (a) For $t>c$, the derivative of $(t-c)^2/4$ is $(t-c)/2$, its nonnegative square root. For $t<c$ both sides are zero, and at $c$ both one-sided derivatives are zero. Thus the curve is $C^1$ and solves the equation, giving infinitely many solutions through zero.
(b) A local Lipschitz constant would bound $\sqrt{|x|}/|x|$, but this tends to infinity. The $C^1$ hypothesis of the existence-and-uniqueness theorem is also absent there.
(c) On an interval with $x\ne0$, put $u=\log|x|$. Then $u'=x'/x=u$, so for either sign of $x_0\ne0$,
\[
x(t)=\operatorname{sgn}(x_0)\exp\bigl(e^{t-t_0}\log|x_0|\bigr).
\]
This is defined for every real $t$, keeps its sign, and cannot approach zero at a finite time; its limit there is a nonzero finite number. Any nonzero component of a putative solution through zero would have a finite endpoint where it joined zero, contradicting this formula and continuity. Thus the solution through zero is identically zero, and all nonzero initial values have the unique formula above. The equilibria $\pm1$ are included. Although $F(x)/x=\log|x|$ is unbounded near zero, uniqueness holds. Osgood's modulus criterion gives a broader sufficient uniqueness condition; the explicit calculation here proves the required instance without assuming that theorem.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:noncommute}
\idea The two vertical increments use different horizontal positions.
\writeup The flows are $\varphi^X_t(x,y)=(x+t,y)$ and $\varphi^Y_s(x,y)=(x,y+sx)$. Their bracket is $\partial_y$ because $DY\cdot X=(0,1)$ and $DX=0$. The four points in the loop are
\[
(x+s,y),\quad(x+s,y+s(x+s)),\quad(x,y+s(x+s)),\quad(x,y+s^2).
\]
Thus the displacement is exactly $s^2\partial_y$, not just a second-order approximation. At $x=0$, $Y=0$ and only $X$ spans the assigned space; off that line the span is the whole plane. Its variable rank prevents the constant-rank Frobenius theorem from applying globally. Starting on the line, the intermediate horizontal step leaves it, but the final endpoint lies on it and has moved \emph{along} it in the vertical direction. The final vertical displacement is not in the rank-one horizontal span at the starting point.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:linearfields}
\idea Differentiate the exponential series and keep track of the point-composition order in the commutator.
\writeup (a) Define $e^{tA}=\sum_{j\ge0}t^jA^j/j!$. Matrix norms bound this series and its differentiated series uniformly on bounded time intervals by scalar exponential series. Thus its derivative is $Ae^{tA}$ and its value at zero is $I$. Uniqueness gives the flow $e^{tA}x$ for every $t$. Differentiating $e^{-tA}e^{tA}$ shows its derivative is zero and initial value $I$, proving the inverse as well.
(b) The bracket formula gives $B(Ax)-A(Bx)=(BA-AB)x$.
(c) These fields are complete by (a), so the commuting-flow theorem gives equivalence with $AB=BA$.
(d) In the four factors write $M_1=-B,M_2=-A,M_3=B,M_4=A$. Multiplication through order two gives
\[
I+s\sum_iM_i+s^2\left(\frac12\sum_iM_i^2+\sum_{i<j}M_iM_j\right)+O(s^3).
\]
The linear term is zero, and the quadratic coefficient is
$(A^2+B^2)+(BA-B^2-BA-AB-A^2+BA)=BA-AB$.
For the stated matrices $AB=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $BA=-AB$, giving
$I-2s^2\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)+O(s^3)$.
This is the small-loop field sign $BA-AB$, opposite to the usual matrix commutator. Chapter~7 explains the corresponding convention for invariant fields on a matrix group; no such group theory was needed for this calculation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:parking}
\idea Turning before driving creates a sideways component that reversing at the old heading does not cancel.
\writeup (a) Differentiating the drive field in the heading direction gives $[T,F]=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)$, a unit sideways direction to the left.
(b) The four endpoints are $(0,0,s)$, $(s\cos s,s\sin s,s)$, $(s\cos s,s\sin s,0)$ and
\[
(s\cos s-s,s\sin s,0)=(-s^3/2,s^2,0)+O(s^4).
\]
The bracket theorem with $X=T,Y=F$ predicts exactly the quadratic vertical term at heading zero; the cubic error is in the horizontal coordinate.
(c) The fields $F,T,[T,F]$ are independent everywhere, so the rank-two distribution fails involutivity. If a smooth $g$ is invariant under the two flows, then $Fg=Tg=0$ and $[T,F]g=T(Fg)-F(Tg)=0$. These three independent directions force $dg=0$. The configuration space $\R^2\times S^1$ is connected, hence $g$ is constant globally. The calculation describes a turning-in-place unicycle. A real car's steering variable and collision clearance require an additional model, rather than following solely from this bracket computation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:hairyodd}
\idea Unit multiplication preserves lengths and makes the imaginary directions perpendicular to the radius.
\writeup (a) We have $|ix|=|x|=1$ and real inner product $\operatorname{Re}\sum_j\bar x_j(ix_j)=\operatorname{Re}(i|x|^2)=0$. Thus the field is unit and tangent.
(b) Quaternion norm multiplicativity gives $|xa|=|x||a|$; polarization then gives $\langle xa,xb\rangle=|x|^2\langle a,b\rangle$. Applying this to $1,i,j,k$ at unit $x$ shows $x,xi,xj,xk$ are orthonormal, with the last three tangent.
(c) Differentiating $xe^{tq}$ for $q=i,j,k$ gives its own value multiplied by $q$ on the right, so it is the required flow. The field $ix$ has flow $e^{ti}x$. Associativity makes these left and right multiplication flows commute, hence their field brackets vanish. For two fields $X_p(x)=xp$ and $X_q(x)=xq$, the coordinate derivative formula instead gives
$[X_p,X_q](x)=(xp)q-(xq)p=x(pq-qp)$.
Thus $[xi,xj]=2xk$, so these three fields do not commute with one another.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:straighten}
\idea Use time along the flow and a transverse starting point as local coordinates.
\writeup Choose a chart with $p=0,X_p=e_1$. The map $\Phi(t,y)=\varphi^X_t(0,y)$ has first derivative column $X_p$ and remaining columns the transverse coordinate vectors, so $D\Phi(0)=I$. It is a local diffeomorphism by the inverse function theorem. Since $\partial_t\Phi=X(\Phi)$, the inverse chart makes $X=\partial_t$.
For rotation near $(1,0)$, flow from the transverse radial segment $(1+y,0)$:
$\Phi(t,y)=((1+y)\cos t,(1+y)\sin t)$. Its inverse is $t=\arg(x+iy)$ on a small branch about zero angle and transverse coordinate $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}-1$. In these polar coordinates the rotation field is $\partial_t$. The temporary coordinate name $y$ in the transverse parameter is separate from the original Cartesian coordinate.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch02-nonlinear-decay}
\idea Decay toward zero and sensitivity to a nearby initial value measure different things.
\writeup (a) A nonzero trajectory cannot cross the equilibrium at zero by uniqueness. Differentiating $x^{-2}$ gives $2$, so
\[
\varphi(t,x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+2tx^2}},\qquad
\mathcal D=\{(t,x):1+2tx^2>0\}.
\]
For $x\ne0$ its maximal interval is $(-1/(2x^2),\infty)$; at zero it is all $\R$. The formula is smooth on the displayed open domain and also includes zero.
(b) Differentiation gives $Y=(1+2tx^2)^{-3/2}$, including $Y(t,0)=1$. Its time derivative is $-3x^2(1+2tx^2)^{-5/2}=-3\varphi(t,x)^2Y$, with initial value $1$, as the variational equation requires.
(c) For fixed nonzero $x$, $\varphi(t,x)\sim\operatorname{sgn}(x)/\sqrt{2t}$ as $t\to\infty$, and $Y$ decays as a constant times $t^{-3/2}$. At the equilibrium itself $Y=1$ for every time, so no bound $|Y(t,x)|\le Ce^{-ct}$ with fixed $C<\infty,c>0$ can hold uniformly on a neighborhood containing zero for all forward times. At the finite backward endpoint for a nonzero start, the denominator tends to zero and the solution escapes to infinity. Attraction forward therefore does not give completeness in both directions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch02-volume-jacobian}
\idea The variational equation turns determinant growth into the trace of the velocity derivative.
\writeup The inverse flow identity makes $Y=D_x\varphi(t,x)$ invertible. Let $j=\det Y$. The elementary determinant expansion $\det(I+hB)=1+h\operatorname{tr}B+O(h^2)$ follows by choosing either every diagonal $1$ or exactly one linear diagonal entry in the determinant sum; all other nonconstant choices have at least two factors of $h$. Factoring $Y$ from its first-order increment gives
\[
j'=j\operatorname{tr}(Y^{-1}Y')
=j\operatorname{tr}(Y^{-1}DF(\varphi)Y)
=j\operatorname{div}F(\varphi),\qquad j(0)=1,
\]
where $\operatorname{div}F=\sum_i\partial_iF^i$ and cyclicity of trace cancels $Y,Y^{-1}$. Solving this scalar equation gives the formula, including negative times with the oriented integral. For dimension zero the empty determinant is $1$ and the empty divergence sum is zero, agreeing as well.

If divergence vanishes, each local flow Jacobian is one and change of variables preserves Lebesgue volume on every set where the flow is defined. Conversely, local volume preservation forces the continuous positive Jacobian to be one pointwise: otherwise a sufficiently small ball would have a strictly different volume under change of variables. Its time derivative at zero is then zero, forcing divergence zero at each point.

For $F=(H_y,-H_x)$, equality of mixed partials gives divergence zero. Along any integral curve, $dH/dt=H_xH_y-H_yH_x=0$, so $H$ is conserved. With $H=(x^2+y^2)/2$, the field is $(y,-x)$ and its flow is
$(x,y)\mapsto(x\cos t+y\sin t,-x\sin t+y\cos t)$,
a clockwise rotation preserving both area and the circular level sets.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch02-simultaneous-coordinates}
\idea Commuting removes the horizontal dependence of the coefficients, leaving two elementary antiderivatives.
\writeup (a) The coordinate bracket is $[X,Y]=a_x\partial_x+b_x\partial_y$, so it vanishes exactly when both coefficients are independent of $x$ on the rectangle.
(b) In that case write $a=a(y),b=b(y)$ and choose a base height $y_0$. Set
\[
u=x-\int_{y_0}^y\frac{a(w)}{b(w)}dw,\qquad
v=\int_{y_0}^y\frac1{b(w)}dw.
\]
Direct differentiation gives $Xu=1,Xv=0,Yu=0,Yv=1$. The coordinate Jacobian is $1/b(y)\ne0$, so the inverse function theorem makes this a local chart with the stated fields.
(c) For $Y=e^x\partial_y$, the bracket is $Y\ne0$, yet the span of $X,Y$ is the whole tangent plane, integrable in ordinary Cartesian coordinates. If these particular fields were both coordinate derivatives, their bracket would be zero, a contradiction. Frobenius allows changing their basis within the same planes; it does not say every given spanning basis commutes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v9-ch02-torus-averages}
\idea Irrational frequencies average out, whereas a rational slope leaves one transverse circle coordinate unchanged.
\writeup (a) A continuous invariant is constant on each orbit and therefore on its closure. Irrational orbits are dense by the chapter's torus example, so it is constant everywhere.
(b) For the mode $e_k(\theta)=e^{2\pi i(k_1\theta_1+k_2\theta_2)}$, the time frequency is $\nu=k_1+\alpha k_2$. For irrational $\alpha$ it is nonzero unless $k=0$. The normalized time integral of a nonconstant mode is
\[
e_k(\theta)\frac{e^{2\pi i\nu T}-1}{2\pi i\nu T},
\]
whose absolute value is at most $1/(\pi|\nu|T)$, uniformly in $\theta$. The constant mode stays one. Thus every finite Fourier sum averages uniformly to its torus integral. These sums form a self-conjugate algebra containing constants and separating points, so Stone--Weierstrass from Volume~7 makes them uniformly dense in continuous functions on $T^2$. For $\|f-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$, both the time-average error and the torus-mean error are at most $\varepsilon$. Passing first to large $T$ for the finite sum and then to $\varepsilon\to0$ proves the claim uniformly in initial position. No general ergodic theorem was used.
(c) For reduced $p/q$, choose $a,b\in\Z$ with $aq+bp=1$. The coordinates
$u=a\theta_1+b\theta_2$, $v=-p\theta_1+q\theta_2$ modulo one give an integer determinant-one torus diffeomorphism. The field becomes $(1/q)\partial_u$. Its flow traverses the full $u$-circle at fixed $v$, so its smooth invariants are exactly $G(v)$ for arbitrary smooth periodic $G$. For example $\cos(2\pi v)$ has torus mean zero but time average $\cos(2\pi v_0)$ on an orbit with transverse coordinate $v_0$. The rational orbit average need not be the whole-torus mean.
\end{solution}
