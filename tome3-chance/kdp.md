# Description

In 1654 Pascal and Fermat exchanged letters about how to split the stakes of an unfinished game, and probability was born. Three centuries of paradoxes later, Kolmogorov gave it axioms. Chance seems the opposite of mathematics, yet it obeys laws as precise as any, and this book proves them.

It starts with puzzles that a few lines of counting settle and paradoxes where intuition fails, then climbs to the theorems that make randomness predictable. The law of large numbers, which Jacob Bernoulli pondered for twenty years. The central limit theorem, which de Moivre found in coin tosses and which explains why the bell curve appears everywhere. Random walks, where a drunk man finds his way home but a drunk bird may get lost forever. Markov chains, first applied by Markov to the letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, and the question of how many shuffles make a deck random. Poisson processes, from Prussian cavalry kicked by horses to requests on a web server. Martingales, and why the doubling strategy at the casino fails. Brownian motion, the continuous curve a random walk becomes when seen from far away. It ends with the probabilistic method, where chance proves that objects exist without ever building them.

Every theorem is proved, apart from a few foundations of measure theory that are clearly labelled and proved in a later volume. Each chapter opens with its story and has graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that explains how to find the idea, figures, and sources for further reading.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
