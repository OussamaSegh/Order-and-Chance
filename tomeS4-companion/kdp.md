# Description

A long series of mathematics books is like a city. Each book is a district with its own character, and the streets between the districts are the theorems that one book proves and another one uses. This volume is the map of that city.

It is not written like the other books. Most of it is generated directly from their sources: every chapter, every named theorem with the number it carries in its own book, and every reference from one book to a result of another, each reference checked to point to a result that exists and has the kind the reference says. At this printing it covers 21 books, 230 chapters, more than 3000 numbered results of which more than 1500 carry a name, more than 2000 exercises and more than 2200 references between the books.

What you will find inside: a map of the series, its three parts (foundations, the graduate toolbox, toward research), the computing track, the companion volumes and the problem books, with what each book is about and how many chapters, named results and exercises it holds; a dependency picture that shows at a glance which book leans on which, and reading paths through the series for readers with different goals; the notation and conventions shared by all the books, and the few places where they differ; chapter by chapter lists of the named results of every book, the place to look when you remember Dirichlet's theorem, Perron's theorem on positive matrices or Burnside's lemma and want to know where it is proved; the threads between the books, the results that the rest of the series relies on most, such as the Euler product, the prime number theorem and Fourier analysis on finite groups; and an alphabetical index of every named result, from Abel's summation by parts to Zorn's lemma.

Use it as you would use the map of a city you are learning: to find your way back to a theorem you have met, to see which roads lead to a book you want to read next, and to notice how the same few ideas, the prime number theorem, the central limit theorem, the spectral theorem and Galois theory, connect districts that look far apart. A free companion Python notebook loads the data behind the book and lets you explore the dependency graph yourself.
