\chapter{Pseudorandomness}\label{ch:oc-pseudorandom}

\begin{center}\itshape
If the only questions you may ask of a set are about the patterns it contains, when should you call it random?
\end{center}

In 1936 Paul Erd\H{o}s and Paul Tur\'an asked whether a set of integers with positive density must contain three terms in arithmetic progression, and more generally $k$ terms for every $k$. The answer for three terms came in 1953, from Klaus Roth, by Fourier analysis. The answer for every $k$ came in 1975, from Endre Szemer\'edi, by an intricate combinatorial argument. Two years later Hillel Furstenberg gave a third proof, by ergodic theory, which looked as if it came from another planet. In 1998 and 2001 Timothy Gowers found a fourth, which brought Fourier analysis back for every $k$, at the price of inventing the norms that now carry his name. And in April 2004 Ben Green and Terence Tao posted a proof that the primes, a set of density zero, contain progressions of every length.

The series has met this story twice. Volume~11 proved Roth's theorem by ergodic theory (Volume~11, Theorem~10.15), splitting a function into a \emph{compact part}, spanned by eigenfunctions, and a \emph{weakly mixing part}, orthogonal to them. Volume~14 proved it by a density increment (Volume~14, Theorem~12.4), splitting a set into a large Fourier coefficient and the rest. This chapter shows that the two splittings are the same, and makes the dichotomy explicit once: a set either correlates with a character, or it has the number of three-term progressions that a random set of the same density would have. There is no third proof of Roth's theorem here. What the chapter adds is the language of this book: the pseudorandom alternative is exactly the statement that the set looks like independent coins to the test of three-term progressions.

\begin{reminder}[from Volumes~11 and~14]
\emph{Notation.} $N$ is odd, $\Z_N=\Z/N\Z$, $\mathbb E_x$ is the average over $x\in\Z_N$, $e(\theta)=e^{2\pi i\theta}$, $\hat f(\xi)=\mathbb E_xf(x)e(-x\xi/N)$. Fourier inversion is $f(x)=\sum_\xi\hat f(\xi)e(x\xi/N)$ and Parseval is $\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2=\mathbb E_x|f(x)|^2$.
\emph{The $U^2$ norm} (Volume~14, Definition~13.1 and Lemma~13.3). $\|f\|_{U^2}^4=\mathbb E_{x,h,k}f(x)\overline{f(x+h)}\,\overline{f(x+k)}f(x+h+k)$. It satisfies the triangle inequality (Volume~14, Exercise~13.1).
\emph{The $W$-trick} (Volume~14, Lemma~13.9). With $W=\prod_{p<w}p$ and $(b,W)=1$, the function $\Lambda_{b,W}(n)=\frac{\varphi(W)}W\Lambda(Wn+b)$ has mean $1$ and no bias modulo any prime $p<w$.
\emph{Compact and weakly mixing parts} (Volume~11, Lemmas~10.17 and~10.19). For a measure-preserving system with Koopman operator $U$, $H_c$ is the closed span of the eigenfunctions and $H_w=H_c^\perp$; for $f\in H_w$, $\frac1N\sum_{n<N}|\langle U^nf,f\rangle|^2\to0$.
\end{reminder}

\section{The $U^2$ norm is a correlation average}\label{sec:u2corr}

Write $\tau_hf(x)=f(x+h)$ for the translate, and $\langle g,f\rangle=\mathbb E_xg(x)\overline{f(x)}$.

\begin{proposition}[three faces of one number]\label{prop:u2corr}
For every $f:\Z_N\to\C$,
\[
\|f\|_{U^2}^4=\mathbb E_h\bigl|\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle\bigr|^2=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4.
\]
Consequently $\max_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|\le\|f\|_{U^2}\le\bigl(\max_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\mathbb E|f|^2\bigr)^{1/4}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand the square: $\mathbb E_h|\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle|^2=\mathbb E_h\mathbb E_{x,y}f(x+h)\overline{f(x)}\,\overline{f(y+h)}f(y)$. Put $y=x+k$: this is $\mathbb E_{x,h,k}\overline{f(x)}f(x+h)f(x+k)\overline{f(x+h+k)}$, the complex conjugate of the average defining $\|f\|_{U^2}^4$. Being a mean of squared moduli, it is real, so the two agree.

For the second equality, Fourier inversion gives $\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle=\mathbb E_x\sum_{\xi,\eta}\hat f(\xi)\overline{\hat f(\eta)}e\bigl((x+h)\xi/N-x\eta/N\bigr)=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2e(h\xi/N)$, since the average over $x$ of $e(x(\xi-\eta)/N)$ is $1$ if $\xi=\eta$ and $0$ otherwise. So, as a function of $h$, $\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle$ has Fourier coefficients $|\hat f(\xi)|^2$, and Parseval in $h$ gives $\mathbb E_h|\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle|^2=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4$.

Finally, $\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4\ge\max_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4$, and $\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4\le\max_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2=\max_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2\,\mathbb E|f|^2$ by Parseval.
\end{proof}

The middle expression is the one Volume~11 uses. In a measure-preserving system, the average $\frac1N\sum_{n<N}|\langle U^nf,f\rangle|^2$ measures how much $f$ remembers of its own translates, and Volume~11, Lemma~10.19, proves that it tends to $0$ on the weakly mixing part. On $\Z_N$ with the translation $x\mapsto x+1$, the same average is $\|f\|_{U^2}^4$, and the eigenfunctions are the characters $x\mapsto e(x\xi/N)$. A finite group has no infinite-dimensional weakly mixing part: every function is a combination of characters. So the dichotomy becomes quantitative.

\begin{proposition}[structured plus pseudorandom]\label{prop:strpsr}
Let $f:\Z_N\to\C$ and $\eta>0$. Put $S=\{\xi:|\hat f(\xi)|\ge\eta\}$, $f_{\mathrm{str}}(x)=\sum_{\xi\in S}\hat f(\xi)e(x\xi/N)$ and $f_{\mathrm{psr}}=f-f_{\mathrm{str}}$. Then $|S|\le\eta^{-2}\mathbb E|f|^2$ and $\|f_{\mathrm{psr}}\|_{U^2}^4\le\eta^2\,\mathbb E|f|^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Parseval, $|S|\eta^2\le\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2=\mathbb E|f|^2$. The coefficients of $f_{\mathrm{psr}}$ are those of $f$ off $S$, all of modulus less than $\eta$, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:u2corr}, $\|f_{\mathrm{psr}}\|_{U^2}^4=\sum_{\xi\notin S}|\hat f(\xi)|^4\le\eta^2\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2$.
\end{proof}

The structured part is a combination of at most $\eta^{-2}$ characters, the finite shadow of $H_c$. The pseudorandom part has a $U^2$ norm at most $\eta^{1/2}$, the finite shadow of $H_w$. The point of the next section is that the pseudorandom part is invisible to three-term progressions.

\section{The $U^2$ count}\label{sec:u2count}

For $A\subseteq\Z_N$, let
\[
\Lambda_3(A)=\mathbb E_{x,d}\mathbf 1_A(x)\mathbf 1_A(x+d)\mathbf 1_A(x+2d)=N^{-2}\#\{(x,d):x,\,x+d,\,x+2d\in A\}.
\]
If $A$ were a random set, each point present independently with probability $\delta$, a progression with $d\ne0$ would lie in $A$ with probability $\delta^3$, since its three points are distinct when $N$ is odd. So $\delta^3$ is the random count.

\begin{theorem}[the $U^2$ count]\label{thm:u2count}
Let $N$ be odd, let $A\subseteq\Z_N$ have density $\delta=|A|/N$, and let $f=\mathbf 1_A-\delta$ be its balanced function. Then
\[
\bigl|\Lambda_3(A)-\delta^3\bigr|\le\delta(1-\delta)\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat f(\xi)|\le\delta(1-\delta)\,\|f\|_{U^2}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Expand $\mathbf 1_A=\delta+f$ in each of the three factors of $\mathbf 1_A(x)\mathbf 1_A(x+d)\mathbf 1_A(x+2d)$. That gives $\delta^3$ and seven terms, each containing at least one $f$.

\emph{One factor $f$.} A term such as $\delta^2\,\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x+d)$ vanishes: for fixed $d$, $x+d$ runs over $\Z_N$, and $\sum_xf(x)=|A|-\delta N=0$.

\emph{Two factors $f$.} The term $\delta\,\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x)f(x+d)$ vanishes because, for fixed $x$, $x+d$ runs over $\Z_N$ as $d$ does; the term $\delta\,\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x+d)f(x+2d)$ becomes the same after putting $u=x+d$. For $\delta\,\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x)f(x+2d)$ we use that $N$ is odd: $2$ is invertible modulo $N$, so for fixed $x$ the point $x+2d$ runs over $\Z_N$ as $d$ does, and the term vanishes again.

\emph{Three factors $f$.} By Fourier inversion,
\[
\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x)f(x+d)f(x+2d)=\sum_{\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3}\hat f(\xi_1)\hat f(\xi_2)\hat f(\xi_3)\,\mathbb E_{x,d}e\Bigl(\frac{(\xi_1+\xi_2+\xi_3)x+(\xi_2+2\xi_3)d}N\Bigr).
\]
The average is $1$ when $\xi_1+\xi_2+\xi_3=0$ and $\xi_2+2\xi_3=0$, and $0$ otherwise. These conditions say $\xi_2=-2\xi_3$ and $\xi_1=\xi_3$, so the term is $\sum_\xi\hat f(\xi)^2\hat f(-2\xi)$. Since $\hat f(0)=0$, only $\xi\ne0$ contributes, and then $-2\xi\ne0$ too. Its modulus is at most $\max_{\eta\ne0}|\hat f(\eta)|\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2=\max_{\eta\ne0}|\hat f(\eta)|\,\mathbb E|f|^2$, and $\mathbb E|f|^2=\delta(1-\delta)^2+(1-\delta)\delta^2=\delta(1-\delta)$.

So $\Lambda_3(A)-\delta^3=\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x)f(x+d)f(x+2d)$, and the bound follows; the last inequality is Proposition~\ref{prop:u2corr}.
\end{proof}

The proof says more than the statement. Write $f=f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{psr}}$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:strpsr}: the three-term count of a set is decided by a bounded number of characters, up to an error controlled by the size $\eta$ of the coefficients left out. That is the whole content of the word \emph{pseudorandom} at this level.

\begin{corollary}[the dichotomy]\label{cor:dichotomy}
Let $N$ be odd and $A\subseteq\Z_N$ of density $\delta>0$. Then at least one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\Lambda_3(A)\ge\frac12\delta^3$: $A$ has at least half the random number of three-term progressions;
\item there is $\xi\ne0$ with $\bigl|\mathbb E_x\mathbf 1_A(x)e(-x\xi/N)\bigr|\ge\frac12\delta^2$: $A$ correlates with a non-trivial character.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $\xi\ne0$, $\hat f(\xi)=\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)$, because the constant $\delta$ has no coefficient at $\xi\ne0$. If (a) fails, then $\frac12\delta^3<|\Lambda_3(A)-\delta^3|\le\delta(1-\delta)\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat f(\xi)|$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}, so $\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|>\frac{\delta^2}{2(1-\delta)}\ge\frac{\delta^2}2$.
\end{proof}

A set with no progression $x,x+d,x+2d$ with $d\ne0$ has $\Lambda_3(A)=\frac\delta N$, the contribution of $d=0$, and this is less than $\frac12\delta^3$ as soon as $\delta^2N>2$. So such a set correlates with a character, which means that its density is visibly larger on some long arithmetic progression. That is where Volume~14 takes over: it embeds $\{1,\dots,N\}$ in $\Z_M$ to turn progressions modulo $M$ into genuine ones, passes to the progression where the density went up (Volume~14, Lemmas~12.2 and~12.3), and iterates. Volume~11 does the same in the infinite world: the compact part of $\mathbf 1_A$ is almost periodic and supplies the progressions (Volume~11, Lemma~10.18), and the weakly mixing part does not change the count. In both proofs the alternative is the same, and it is Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}.

\section{Three examples}\label{sec:threeexamples}

\begin{examplex}[an interval is structured]
Let $A=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}\subseteq\Z_N$, with $\delta=\frac mN$. Summing a geometric series,
\[
|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(1)|=\frac1N\Bigl|\sum_{x<m}e(-x/N)\Bigr|=\frac{|\sin(\pi m/N)|}{N\sin(\pi/N)}\longrightarrow\frac{\sin\pi\delta}\pi
\]
as $N\to\infty$ with $\delta$ fixed (Exercise~\ref{ex:intervalcoef}). For $\delta=\frac12$ this is $\frac1\pi\approx0.318$, far above $\frac12\delta^2=0.125$. An interval correlates with the slowest character, as it should: it \emph{is} a level set of a slowly turning phase.
\end{examplex}

\begin{proposition}[a random set is pseudorandom]\label{prop:randomu2}
Let each $x\in\Z_N$ belong to $A$ independently with probability $\delta$, let $\sigma^2=\delta(1-\delta)$, and $g=\mathbf 1_A-\delta$. Then
\[
\mathbb E\,\|g\|_{U^2}^4=\frac{2(N-1)\sigma^4+\mu_4}{N^2},\qquad\mu_4=\mathbb E\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{x\in A\}}-\delta\bigr)^4=\sigma^2(1-3\sigma^2).
\]
In particular $\mathbb E\|g\|_{U^2}^4\le\frac2N\sigma^4+\frac{\sigma^2}{N^2}$, so $\|g\|_{U^2}$ is of order $N^{-1/4}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $\varepsilon_x=g(x)$: independent, real, of mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2$ and fourth moment $\mu_4$. Then $\mathbb E\|g\|_{U^2}^4=\mathbb E_{x,h,k}\mathbb E[\varepsilon_x\varepsilon_{x+h}\varepsilon_{x+k}\varepsilon_{x+h+k}]$. The inner expectation vanishes unless every index appears at least twice among the four. The four indices $x,x+h,x+k,x+h+k$ pair up in one of three ways: $x=x+h$ and $x+k=x+h+k$, which means $h=0$; or $x=x+k$ and $x+h=x+h+k$, which means $k=0$; or $x=x+h+k$ and $x+h=x+k$, which means $h=k$ and $2h=0$, so $h=k=0$ since $N$ is odd. If $h=0\ne k$ or $k=0\ne h$ the expectation is $\sigma^4$; if $h=k=0$ it is $\mu_4$. Counting the pairs $(h,k)$, $\mathbb E\|g\|_{U^2}^4=N^{-2}\bigl(2(N-1)\sigma^4+\mu_4\bigr)$. For $\mu_4$: $\mathbb E(\mathbf 1-\delta)^4=\delta(1-\delta)^4+(1-\delta)\delta^4=\sigma^2\bigl((1-\delta)^3+\delta^3\bigr)=\sigma^2(1-3\sigma^2)$, and $\mu_4\le\sigma^2$.
\end{proof}

The balanced function of Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count} uses the empirical density $\hat\delta=|A|/N$, not $\delta$; the two differ by the constant $\hat\delta-\delta$, of mean square $\frac{\sigma^2}N$, and by the triangle inequality for the $U^2$ norm (whose value on a constant $c$ is $|c|$), $\|\mathbf 1_A-\hat\delta\|_{U^2}\le\|g\|_{U^2}+|\hat\delta-\delta|$. So a random set has $\Lambda_3(A)=\hat\delta^3+O_P(N^{-1/4})$: the random count, up to a vanishing error. The coins of this book pass the test of three-term progressions, as they should.

\begin{examplex}[quadratic residues]
Let $N=p$ be a prime and $A$ the set of non-zero squares modulo $p$, of density $\frac{p-1}{2p}$. By the Gauss sum $|\sum_ye(ay^2/p)|=\sqrt p$ for $a\not\equiv0$ (proved in Exercise~\ref{ex:qr}, as in Volume~14, Exercise~13.2), every coefficient $\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)$ with $\xi\ne0$ has modulus at most $\frac{\sqrt p+1}{2p}$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:qr}). So $\Lambda_3(A)=\frac18+O(p^{-1/2})$. The squares are a deterministic set, defined by the most rigid of algebraic conditions, and to three-term progressions they look exactly like coins of bias~$\frac12$.
\end{examplex}

Pseudorandomness in this sense is relative to a test. The quadratic phase $f(x)=e(x^2/N)$ has all its Fourier coefficients of modulus $N^{-1/2}$ when $N$ is prime (Volume~14, Example~13.6), so $\|f\|_{U^2}=N^{-1/4}$ is tiny. Yet $x^2-3(x+d)^2+3(x+2d)^2-(x+3d)^2=0$ identically, so $f$ is perfectly correlated along four-term progressions (Exercise~\ref{ex:fourterm}). To control four-term progressions one needs the $U^3$ norm, and the structured objects for $U^3$ are no longer characters. Volume~14 states the inverse theorem of Green, Tao and Ziegler, which describes them, as quoted (Volume~14, Theorem~13.7); this book does not develop that higher-order structure, and this chapter does not use it.

\section{The primes, after the $W$-trick}\label{sec:wtrick}

The primes fail the $U^2$ test at once, for a trivial reason: they are almost all odd. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-sieves}, the events $\{p\mid n\}$ for small $p$ are far from independent of the event $\{n\text{ prime}\}$, and every such dependence shows up as a correlation with a character of small denominator. Volume~14, Exercise~13.4, makes this exact: $\Lambda-1$ has mean $0$ and still correlates with $(-1)^n$.

The $W$-trick conditions on these events. Restrict to $n\equiv b\pmod W$ with $W=\prod_{p<w}p$ and $(b,W)=1$, and rescale: by Volume~14, Lemma~13.9, the function $\Lambda_{b,W}$ has mean $1$ and no bias modulo any prime below $w$. The small primes are structure, and they are removed by hand; the inverse theorems are applied to what remains. How much structure the small primes carry is measured by a product of local factors.

\begin{proposition}[the local factors for three-term progressions]\label{prop:local3}
For a prime $p\ge3$, the proportion of $(x,d)\in(\Z/p\Z)^2$ with $x$, $x+d$, $x+2d$ all non-zero modulo $p$ is $\frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{p^2}$; for $p=2$, the proportion of $(x,d)\in(\Z/2\Z)^2$ with $x$, $x+d$, $x+2d$ all odd is $\frac14$. Divided by $(1-\frac1p)^3$, the probability that three independent random integers are prime to $p$, these give the factors $\beta_p=1-\frac1{(p-1)^2}$ for $p\ge3$ and $\beta_2=2$. Their product converges:
\[
\mathfrak S_3=\prod_p\beta_p=2\prod_{p\ge3}\Bigl(1-\frac1{(p-1)^2}\Bigr)=1.3203\ldots
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $p\ge3$: take $x\ne0$, in $p-1$ ways. The condition $x+d\ne0$ excludes $d=-x$, and $x+2d\ne0$ excludes $d=-x/2$; these are two different values of $d$ because $-x\ne-x/2$ when $x\ne0$. So $p-2$ values of $d$ remain, and the proportion is $\frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{p^2}$. Then $\frac{(p-1)(p-2)/p^2}{(p-1)^3/p^3}=\frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2}=1-\frac1{(p-1)^2}$. For $p=2$: $x$ must be odd and $x+d$ odd forces $d$ even, which also makes $x+2d$ odd; the proportion is $\frac14$, against $(\frac12)^3=\frac18$. The product converges because $\sum_p\frac1{(p-1)^2}<\infty$.
\end{proof}

The factor $2$ at $p=2$ is the parity constraint: in a progression of odd numbers the difference is even, which a model with independent coins does not know. Volume~14, Exercise~13.5, computes the factors for every length. The prediction is then the Cramér count multiplied by $\mathfrak S_3$: the number of progressions $p_1<p_2<p_3\le N$ of primes should be about
\[
\mathfrak S_3\sum_{\substack{x\ge2,\ d\ge1\\x+2d\le N}}\frac1{\log x\,\log(x+d)\,\log(x+2d)}.
\]
This is a theorem: Johannes van der Corput proved it in 1939 by Vinogradov's method, and it is also a case of the work of Green and Tao on linear equations in primes. We quote it. The table compares it with the truth.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
$N$ & progressions of primes $\le N$ & prediction with $\mathfrak S_3$ & ratio\\\hline
$10^4$ & $59\,504$ & $62\,140$ & $0.958$\\
$10^5$ & $2\,856\,331$ & $2\,890\,267$ & $0.988$\\
$10^6$ & $157\,300\,309$ & $157\,968\,254$ & $0.996$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Without the factor $\mathfrak S_3$ the truth would exceed the prediction by $26\%$ to $32\%$. With it, the error is half a percent at $N=10^6$ and still shrinking. The coins with the small primes put back are an excellent model for three-term progressions of primes.

\section{Green and Tao, quoted}\label{sec:greentao}

\begin{remark}
These are quoted; see the Sources. (a) \emph{Green and Tao (2008).} The primes contain arithmetic progressions of every finite length (Volume~14, Theorem~13.8, also quoted). The proof transfers Szemer\'edi's theorem (Volume~14, Theorem~12.7, quoted) from dense sets of integers to dense subsets of a pseudorandom majorant of $\Lambda_{b,W}$, by the relative Szemer\'edi theorem (Volume~14, Theorem~13.10, quoted). It does not need any inverse theorem for the primes themselves. (b) \emph{Linear equations in primes.} Green and Tao (2010), with the inverse theorem of Green, Tao and Ziegler (2012) and a theorem of Green and Tao (2012) on the M\"obius function, proved the asymptotic count of $k$-term progressions of primes, with the singular series of Volume~14, Exercise~13.5. For $k=3$ this is van der Corput's theorem. (c) \emph{Bounds.} Kelley and Meka (2023) proved that a subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ without three-term progressions has at most $N\exp(-c(\log N)^{1/12})$ elements (Volume~14, Theorem~12.6, quoted), close to Behrend's construction (Volume~14, Theorem~12.5).
\end{remark}

The chapter's organisation can now be said in one sentence. A deterministic set can match the random model in mean and still be structured, which is Maier's theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-models}; or it can be pseudorandom for a given test and then have the random count for that test, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}. The primes, after the $W$-trick, are conjectured to be pseudorandom for every test built from linear forms, and they are known to be so for three-term progressions.

\begin{beauty}
A character is the simplest kind of order, a phase that turns at a constant speed. Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy} says that, for three-term progressions, there is no other kind: a set either follows some turning phase, or it behaves like a sequence of coin tosses. Furstenberg found the same alternative in an abstract dynamical system, Roth in a finite group, and the primes obey it once their small prime factors are put aside.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:intervalcoef}
Let $A=\{0,1,\dots,m-1\}\subseteq\Z_N$. Show that $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|=\frac{|\sin(\pi m\xi/N)|}{N|\sin(\pi\xi/N)|}$ for $\xi\ne0$, and that $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(1)|\to\frac{\sin\pi\delta}\pi$ when $N\to\infty$ and $\frac mN\to\delta$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:noap}
Let $N$ be odd and $A\subseteq\Z_N$ of density $\delta$, containing no $x,x+d,x+2d$ with $d\ne0$. Show that if $\delta^2N\ge4$, then $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|\ge\frac34\delta^2$ for some $\xi\ne0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:qr}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $A$ the set of non-zero squares modulo $p$. (a) Show that $\mathbf 1_A(x)=\frac12\bigl(\#\{y:y^2=x\}-\mathbf 1_{x=0}\bigr)$. (b) Show that $|\sum_ye(ay^2/p)|=\sqrt p$ for $a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, and deduce that $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|\le\frac{\sqrt p+1}{2p}$ for $\xi\ne0$. (c) Conclude that $\Lambda_3(A)=\frac18+O(p^{-1/2})$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:fourterm}
Let $N$ be odd and $f(x)=e(x^2/N)$. Show that $\mathbb E_{x,d}f(x)\overline{f(x+d)}^3f(x+2d)^3\overline{f(x+3d)}=1$. Why does this not contradict Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}, given that $\|f\|_{U^2}=N^{-1/4}$ when $N$ is prime?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:randommu4}
In Proposition~\ref{prop:randomu2}, check the value of $\mu_4$ for $\delta=\frac12$ and show that $\mathbb E\|g\|_{U^2}^4\sim\frac{2\sigma^4}N$. Deduce that $\mathbb P(\|g\|_{U^2}\ge N^{-1/5})\to0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:singular3}
Compute $\mathfrak S_3$ to four decimals from Proposition~\ref{prop:local3}. Then show that, among the progressions $p_1<p_2<p_3$ of primes, none has $p_1=2$, and exactly one has $p_1=3$ and $p_2=5$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v15-12-1}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:intervalcoef}
\idea A sum of a geometric progression of unit vectors.
\writeup With $q=e(-\xi/N)\ne1$, $\sum_{x<m}q^x=\frac{1-q^m}{1-q}$, and $|1-e(\theta)|=|e(\theta/2)|\,|e(-\theta/2)-e(\theta/2)|=2|\sin\pi\theta|$. So $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|=\frac1N\frac{2|\sin(\pi m\xi/N)|}{2|\sin(\pi\xi/N)|}$. For $\xi=1$, $N\sin(\pi/N)\to\pi$ and $\sin(\pi m/N)\to\sin\pi\delta$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:noap}
\idea A progression-free set fails alternative (a) of the dichotomy by a wide margin.
\writeup Only $d=0$ contributes to $\Lambda_3(A)$, so $\Lambda_3(A)=\frac{|A|}{N^2}=\frac\delta N\le\frac{\delta^3}4$ when $\delta^2N\ge4$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}, $\frac34\delta^3\le\delta^3-\Lambda_3(A)\le\delta(1-\delta)\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|$, so $\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|\ge\frac{3\delta^2}{4(1-\delta)}\ge\frac34\delta^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:qr}
\idea The indicator of the squares is half the number of square roots, and the Fourier transform of the number of square roots is a Gauss sum.
\writeup (a) A non-zero square has exactly two square roots and a non-square none; $0$ has one. (b) For $\xi\ne0$, $\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)=\frac1{2p}\sum_ye(-y^2\xi/p)-\frac1{2p}$, by (a). The sum $S(a)=\sum_ye(ay^2/p)$ for $a\ne0$ satisfies $|S(a)|^2=\sum_{y,z}e(a(y^2-z^2)/p)=\sum_{u,v}e(auv/p)$ with $u=y-z$, $v=y+z$ (a bijection since $p$ is odd), and $\sum_ve(auv/p)$ is $p$ if $u=0$ and $0$ otherwise, so $|S(a)|^2=p$. With $a=-\xi$, $|\hat{\mathbf 1}_A(\xi)|\le\frac{\sqrt p+1}{2p}$. (c) The density is $\delta=\frac{p-1}{2p}=\frac12+O(\frac1p)$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}, $\Lambda_3(A)=\delta^3+O(p^{-1/2})=\frac18+O(p^{-1/2})$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:fourterm}
\idea A quadratic phase is a polynomial phase of degree $2$, and the third difference of a quadratic vanishes.
\writeup The product is $e\bigl((x^2-3(x+d)^2+3(x+2d)^2-(x+3d)^2)/N\bigr)$. The coefficient of $x^2$ is $1-3+3-1=0$, of $xd$ it is $-6+12-6=0$, of $d^2$ it is $-3+12-9=0$. So each term is $1$ and so is the average. There is no contradiction: Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count} controls averages over three-term progressions only, and a four-term average needs the $U^3$ norm (Volume~14, Theorem~13.4), for which $f$ is as large as possible.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:randommu4}
\idea The exact formula leaves one dominant term.
\writeup For $\delta=\frac12$, $\sigma^2=\frac14$ and $\mu_4=\frac14(1-\frac34)=\frac1{16}$, which is indeed $(\frac12)^4$, the fourth power of $\pm\frac12$. In general $\mathbb E\|g\|_{U^2}^4=\frac{2\sigma^4}N\bigl(1-\frac1N\bigr)+\frac{\mu_4}{N^2}\sim\frac{2\sigma^4}N$. By Markov's inequality, $\mathbb P(\|g\|_{U^2}\ge N^{-1/5})=\mathbb P(\|g\|_{U^2}^4\ge N^{-4/5})\le N^{4/5}\bigl(\frac2N+\frac1{N^2}\bigr)\to0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:singular3}
\idea Euler products over $p$ converge fast when the factors are $1-O(p^{-2})$.
\writeup Multiplying the factors over $p\le10^6$ gives $\prod_{3\le p\le10^6}(1-\frac1{(p-1)^2})=0.66016$, and the tail changes the fifth decimal at most, since $\sum_{p>10^6}\frac1{(p-1)^2}<10^{-6}$. So $\mathfrak S_3=1.3203$. If $p_1=2$, then $p_3=2p_2-2$ is even and larger than $2$, so not prime. If $p_1,p_2,p_3$ are odd primes in progression with difference $d$, then $d$ is even; if $3\nmid d$, the three numbers $p_1,p_1+d,p_1+2d$ are in three different classes modulo $3$, so one is divisible by $3$ and must equal $3$, which forces $p_1=3$. With $p_1=3$ and $p_2=5$, $p_3=7$ is prime: the progression $3,5,7$ is the only one with these first terms.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-12-1}
\idea A character is a single frequency.
\writeup By definition \(\hat f(\xi)=\mathbb E_x e(x/5)e(-x\xi/5)=\mathbb E_x e(x(1-\xi)/5)\). The average is \(1\) if \(\xi\equiv1\pmod5\) and \(0\) otherwise. Proposition~\ref{prop:u2corr} gives \(\|f\|_{U^2}^4=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4=1\), so \(\|f\|_{U^2}=1\). The character is an eigenfunction of the translation in Figure~\ref{fig:v15c12-cycle}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-12-2}
\idea List the \(25\) pairs, or list the progressions that stay inside \(A\).
\writeup Here \(\delta=3/5\) and \(\delta^3=27/125=0.216\). A pair \((x,d)\) contributes when \(x\), \(x+d\) and \(x+2d\) all lie in \(A\), arithmetic in \(\Z_5\). Each pair with \(d=0\) and \(x\in A\) contributes, and there are three such \(x\). Two further pairs contribute: \((x,d)=(0,1)\), the progression \(0,1,2\), and \((x,d)=(2,4)\), the progression \(2,1,0\). No other pair works. The count is \(5\), so \(\Lambda_3(A)=5/25=1/5\). Figure~\ref{fig:v15c12-progression} shows the underlying set. The difference from \(\delta^3\) is \(|0.200-0.216|=0.016\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-12-3}
\idea The largest Fourier coefficient is at most the square root of the \(\ell^2\) mass.
\writeup Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count} gives \(|\Lambda_3(A)-\delta^3|\le\delta(1-\delta)\max_{\xi\ne0}|\hat f(\xi)|\), with \(f=\mathbf 1_A-\delta\). Parseval says \(\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2=\mathbb E|f|^2=\delta(1-\delta)\), and \(\hat f(0)=0\), so every coefficient, including the largest, satisfies \(|\hat f(\xi)|^2\le\delta(1-\delta)\). Therefore
\[
\bigl|\Lambda_3(A)-\delta^3\bigr|\le\delta(1-\delta)\cdot\bigl(\delta(1-\delta)\bigr)^{1/2}=\bigl(\delta(1-\delta)\bigr)^{3/2}.
\]
For Exercise~\ref{ex:v15-12-2}, \(\delta(1-\delta)=(3/5)(2/5)=6/25\) and \((6/25)^{3/2}=6\sqrt6\,/\,125\). Numerically \(6/25=0.24\) and \(0.24^{3/2}=0.118\), which is larger than the difference \(0.016\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-12-4}
\idea The correlation \(\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle\) is the Fourier series whose coefficients are \(|\hat f|^2\).
\writeup Fourier inversion gives \(f(x)=\sum_\xi\hat f(\xi)e(x\xi/N)\). Therefore
\[
\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle
=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^2e(h\xi/N).
\]
To see it, expand both copies of \(f\) and average in \(x\): the phase \(e(x(\xi-\eta)/N)\) averages to \(1\) when \(\xi=\eta\) and to \(0\) otherwise, and the shift by \(h\) leaves the factor \(e(h\xi/N)\). As a function of \(h\), the correlation has Fourier coefficients \(|\hat f(\xi)|^2\). Parseval in \(h\), in the form \(\mathbb E_h|g(h)|^2=\sum_\xi|\hat g(\xi)|^2\), gives
\[
\mathbb E_h\bigl|\langle\tau_hf,f\rangle\bigr|^2=\sum_\xi|\hat f(\xi)|^4.
\]
Proposition~\ref{prop:u2corr} identifies the left side with \(\|f\|_{U^2}^4\). The first equality of that proposition, comparing the \(U^2\) average with the correlation average, is the change of variables \(y=x+k\) in the proof printed above the proposition.
\end{solution}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The $U^2$ norm of a function on $\Z_N$ is at the same time a fourth moment of its Fourier coefficients and an average of its self-correlations (Proposition~\ref{prop:u2corr}), which is why the Fourier proof of Volume~14 and the ergodic proof of Volume~11 meet. Every function splits into a few characters and a part with small $U^2$ norm (Proposition~\ref{prop:strpsr}). Three-term progressions see only the characters (Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}), so a set either correlates with a character or has the random number of progressions (Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}). Random sets are pseudorandom in this sense, with a $U^2$ norm of order $N^{-1/4}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:randomu2}), and so are the quadratic residues. The primes are not, until the $W$-trick removes the small primes; the local factors that it removes multiply to $\mathfrak S_3$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:local3}), and with them the count of three-term progressions of primes is matched to half a percent.

The asymptotic count of progressions of primes, the theorem of Green and Tao, and every inverse theorem beyond $U^2$ are quoted. The next chapter turns to a place where the primes are \emph{not} pseudorandom in the naive sense, even after every correction: the race between residue classes, where a small and persistent bias decides who leads.
