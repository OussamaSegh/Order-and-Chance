\chapter{When order wins}\label{ch:oc-bias}\label{ch:bias}

\begin{center}\itshape
If the primes $4n+1$ and $4n+3$ are equally numerous in the long run, why does $4n+3$ lead almost all the time?
\end{center}

Every chapter so far has measured how well chance models the primes. This one is about a place where order wins: a bias that every random model of the primes misses, because it comes from the squares. The answer is a theorem with two hypotheses, and both are named wherever they are used: the generalised Riemann hypothesis for $L(s,\chi_{-4})$ (GRH), and the linear independence over $\Q$ of the ordinates of its zeros (LI). Under them, the race between $4n+3$ and $4n+1$ has a limiting distribution, the law of a sum of independent random waves, one for each zero, and the share of the time that $4n+3$ leads is a probability computed from that law. Volume~6 proved the identity that reduces the race to $\psi(x,\chi_{-4})$; the explicit formula for $L(s,\chi_{-4})$, the passage to a limit law and its properties are proved here. Maier's irregularities, the other way order shows through, were Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-models}.
