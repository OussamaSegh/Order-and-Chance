\chapter{Primes in short intervals}\label{ch:oc-short}

\begin{center}\itshape
Drop an interval of length $\log x$ at random near $x$ and count the primes in it.\\
Is the count a Poisson variable, as it would be for coins, and can anyone prove it?
\end{center}

Near $10^9$, cut the integers into a million intervals of length $21$, which is about $\log 10^9$. Count the primes in each interval. Volume~6 did this in Figure~10.2. The counts look like a Poisson law of mean $1$, as Cram\'er's model predicts, but the spread is wrong. The variance is $0.79$ and not $1.01$. There are too few empty intervals and too few crowded ones. The primes are a little more orderly than coins.

Three different statements hide in that picture. The first says that \emph{every} interval of a given length holds about the expected number of primes. Volume~14 proves it for intervals $]x,x+x^\theta]$ with $\theta>\frac34$ (Corollary~1.9), and nobody can prove it for intervals of length $(\log x)^2$. The second says that the count in a \emph{random} interval of length $\lambda\log x$ follows a Poisson law. The third says that the variance, on longer intervals, is smaller than the Poisson variance by a definite amount. This chapter keeps the three apart.

The tool is a constant that Hardy and Littlewood wrote down in 1923, in the third paper of their series \emph{Partitio Numerorum}: the singular series, which corrects Cram\'er's coins for divisibility by small primes. The chapter proves four things about it. The Hardy--Littlewood singular series is a product of local probabilities, and prime by prime its average is exactly $1$. The average of the pair series has a second term $-\frac12H\log H$ and a third term with the constant $1-\gamma-\log2\pi$, and we prove both (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion}). Under the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture for pairs and the Riemann hypothesis, those terms become the variance of $\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairsecond}). Finally Gallagher's average for $k$-tuples is proved in full, and from it and the uniform Hardy--Littlewood conjecture comes the Poisson law (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherpoisson}). The hypotheses are named in the statements, and stay there.

\section{The singular series as a probability}

Fix distinct integers $h_1,\dots,h_k$ and ask how often $n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k$ are all prime. Cram\'er's model of Volume~6 treats the $k$ events as independent, each with probability about $1/\log n$. The primes cannot quite do that, because divisibility by a small prime is not independent across a pattern. The correction is a product over primes, and each factor is a probability you can compute by hand.

Write $\mathbf h=(h_1,\dots,h_k)$. For a prime $p$, let $\nu_p(\mathbf h)$ be the number of distinct residues among $h_1,\dots,h_k$ modulo $p$. If $n$ is a random residue modulo $p$, the probability that none of $n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k$ is divisible by $p$ is $1-\nu_p(\mathbf h)/p$: exactly $\nu_p(\mathbf h)$ residues of $n$ are forbidden. Cram\'er's model, with $k$ independent events of probability $1-1/p$ each, would say $(1-1/p)^k$. Put
\[
b_p(\mathbf h)=\frac{1-\nu_p(\mathbf h)/p}{(1-1/p)^k}.
\]

\begin{definition}[the singular series]\label{def:singular}
For distinct integers $h_1,\dots,h_k$, the \emph{singular series} is
\[
\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)=\prod_p b_p(\mathbf h).
\]
\end{definition}

Lemma~\ref{lem:bp} shows that the product converges. For $k=1$ every factor is $1$, so $\mathfrak S(h_1)=1$. For a pair, write $\mathfrak S(h)=\mathfrak S(0,h)$ with $h\ne0$. If $p\nmid h$ the factor is $(1-2/p)(1-1/p)^{-2}=p(p-2)/(p-1)^2$. If $p\mid h$ it is $(1-1/p)^{-1}=p/(p-1)$. At $p=2$ it is $0$ for odd $h$ and $2$ for even $h$. So, with $C_2=\prod_{p>2}\bigl(1-(p-1)^{-2}\bigr)=0.6601\ldots$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairseries}
\mathfrak S(h)=2C_2\prod_{\substack{p\mid h\\p>2}}\frac{p-1}{p-2}\quad(h\text{ even}),\qquad\mathfrak S(h)=0\quad(h\text{ odd}).
\end{equation}
This is the series $\mathfrak S(\{0,h\})$ of Volume~6, Chapter~10. Twins have $\mathfrak S(2)=2C_2=1.32$.

\begin{lemma}[local factors]\label{lem:bp}
Let $h_1,\dots,h_k$ be distinct, put $\Delta=\prod_{i<j}|h_i-h_j|$, and let $p\ge2k$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $p\nmid\Delta$, then $1-k^2/p^2\le b_p(\mathbf h)\le1$.
\item If $p\mid\Delta$, then $1-k/p\le b_p(\mathbf h)\le1+4k/p$.
\end{enumerate}
So the product defining $\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)$ converges, and $\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)\ge0$, with equality exactly when some prime $p$ has $\nu_p(\mathbf h)=p$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) If $p\nmid\Delta$, the $h_i$ are distinct modulo $p$, so $\nu_p=k$. Bernoulli's inequality gives $(1-1/p)^k\ge1-k/p$, so $b_p\le1$. Also $(1-1/p)^{-1}\ge1+1/p$, so $(1-1/p)^{-k}\ge(1+1/p)^k\ge1+k/p$, and $b_p\ge(1-k/p)(1+k/p)=1-k^2/p^2$.

(b) Now $1\le\nu_p\le k$. The lower bound is $b_p\ge(1-k/p)(1-1/p)^{-k}\ge1-k/p$. For the upper bound, put $x=1/(p-1)$. Then $b_p\le(1-1/p)^{-k}=(1+x)^k\le e^{kx}$, and $kx\le k/(2k-1)\le1$. On $[0,1]$ the convex function $e^t$ lies below its chord, so $e^t\le1+(e-1)t\le1+2t$. Hence $b_p\le1+2k/(p-1)\le1+4k/p$.

Only finitely many primes divide $\Delta$, and $\sum_pk^2/p^2$ converges, so the product of the factors with $p\ge2k$ converges to a positive number. The finitely many factors with $p<2k$ are non-negative, and one of them is $0$ exactly when $\nu_p=p$.
\end{proof}

The case $\nu_p(\mathbf h)=p$ is the one where the pattern always contains a multiple of $p$. The tuple $(0,2,4)$ is the example: one of $n$, $n+2$, $n+4$ is divisible by $3$, so $3,5,7$ is the only prime triple of that shape. A tuple with $\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)>0$ is called \emph{admissible}.

Here is the first sign that Cram\'er's model is right on average. It is exact, and it takes one line.

\begin{lemma}[the local average is $1$]\label{lem:localavg}
For every prime $p$ and every $k\ge1$,
\[
\frac1{p^k}\sum_{\mathbf h\in(\Z/p\Z)^k}b_p(\mathbf h)=1.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Count pairs $(\mathbf h,r)$ with $r$ a residue that occurs among the $h_i$. For each of the $p$ values of $r$, the tuples that miss $r$ number $(p-1)^k$, so $\sum_{\mathbf h}\nu_p(\mathbf h)=p\bigl(p^k-(p-1)^k\bigr)$. The average of $1-\nu_p/p$ is therefore $1-\bigl(1-(1-1/p)^k\bigr)=(1-1/p)^k$. Divide by $(1-1/p)^k$.
\end{proof}

In words: pick the pattern at random modulo $p$, and the chance that $p$ misses the whole pattern is exactly the chance Cram\'er's model gives. The correction $b_p$ only moves probability from one pattern to another. Section~\ref{sec:gallagherk} turns this into Gallagher's theorem.

\begin{examplex}[$k=2$, $p=3$]
There are $9$ pairs modulo $3$. The $3$ pairs with $h_1\equiv h_2$ have $\nu_3=1$ and $b_3=\frac{2/3}{4/9}=\frac32$. The other $6$ have $\nu_3=2$ and $b_3=\frac{1/3}{4/9}=\frac34$. The average is $\frac{3\cdot\frac32+6\cdot\frac34}9=1$.
\end{examplex}

\section{The pair average to second order}

Volume~6 proved Gallagher's estimate $\sum_{h\le H}\mathfrak S(h)=H+O(\sqrt H)$ (Theorem~10.7). It also noticed, by computer, that the error is really about $-\frac12\log H$, and that this small term explains the narrow histogram. To use that term we need it with its constant, and in the weighted form that a variance produces. Here is the statement.

\begin{theorem}[the pair average to second order]\label{thm:pairexpansion}
For every $\varepsilon>0$ and every integer $H\ge2$,
\[
\sum_{h=1}^H(H-h)\,\mathfrak S(h)=\frac12H^2-\frac12H\log H+\frac12\bigl(1-\gamma-\log2\pi\bigr)H+O_\varepsilon\bigl(H^{1/2+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The constant is $\frac12(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)=-0.7075\ldots$. The proof has three ingredients: two integrals of fractional parts, a Riemann sum along a hyperbola, and a comparison of $\mathfrak S$ with $\zeta$. We prove the integrals first.

\begin{lemma}[two integrals]\label{lem:fracint}
\[
\int_1^\infty\frac{\{t\}}{t^2}\,\dd t=1-\gamma,\qquad\int_1^\infty\frac{\{t\}^2}{t^2}\,\dd t=\log2\pi-1-\gamma.
\]
Hence $I:=\int_1^\infty\frac{\{t\}(1-\{t\})}{t^2}\,\dd t=2-\log2\pi=0.1621\ldots$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Both integrands are at most $t^{-2}$, so it is enough to integrate over $[1,M]$ for an integer $M=N+1$ and let $N\to\infty$. On $[k,k+1]$ write $\{t\}=t-k$. We use $H_M=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1M=\log M+\gamma+o(1)$ (Volume~6, Lemma~2.5(a)) and Stirling's formula $\log M!=(M+\frac12)\log M-M+\frac12\log2\pi+o(1)$ (Volume~5, Theorem~11.8, applied to $\log\Gamma(M)=\log M!-\log M$).

\emph{First integral.} $\int_k^{k+1}\frac{t-k}{t^2}\,\dd t=\log\frac{k+1}k-\frac1{k+1}$. Summing over $k\le N$ telescopes the logarithms: the total is $\log M-(H_M-1)\to1-\gamma$.

\emph{Second integral.} $\int_k^{k+1}\frac{(t-k)^2}{t^2}\,\dd t=1-2k\log\frac{k+1}k+\frac k{k+1}$. Sum over $k\le N$. The first terms give $N$, and $\sum_{k\le N}\frac k{k+1}=N-(H_M-1)$. For the middle term,
\[
\sum_{k=1}^Nk\bigl(\log(k+1)-\log k\bigr)=M\log M-\log M!.
\]
(For $N=1$ both sides are $\log2$, and going from $N-1$ to $N$ adds $N\log(N+1)-N\log N$ to each side.) So the integral over $[1,M]$ is
\[
2N+1-H_M-2M\log M+2\log M!=2N+1-2M-H_M+\log M+\log2\pi+o(1),
\]
which is $-1-\gamma+\log2\pi+o(1)$, because $2N+1-2M=-1$ and $H_M-\log M\to\gamma$.

Finally $\{t\}(1-\{t\})=\{t\}-\{t\}^2$, so $I=(1-\gamma)-(\log2\pi-1-\gamma)=2-\log2\pi$.
\end{proof}

The second ingredient is a Riemann sum along a hyperbola. Write $\beta(t)=\{t\}(1-\{t\})$. Its graph is a row of small arches of height $\frac14$, one over each unit interval.

\begin{lemma}[a hyperbolic Riemann sum]\label{lem:riemannsum}
For real $y\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{1\le m\le y}\beta\Bigl(\frac ym\Bigr)=I\,y+O\bigl(\sqrt y\bigr),
\]
where the implied constant is at most $3$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $y<2$ the sum has one term, both sides are at most $\frac12$, and the bound is clear. So assume $y\ge2$, which makes $\lceil\sqrt y\,\rceil\le\lfloor y\rfloor$. Let $\phi(u)=\beta(y/u)$ for $0<u\le y$. The substitution $t=y/u$ gives $\int_0^y\phi(u)\,\dd u=y\int_1^\infty\beta(t)t^{-2}\,\dd t=Iy$. Compare the sum with this integral. Let $m_0=\lceil\sqrt y\,\rceil$ and $Y=\lfloor y\rfloor$. Then
\[
\sum_{m\le y}\phi(m)-\int_0^y\phi=\Bigl(\sum_{m\le m_0}\phi(m)-\int_0^{m_0}\phi\Bigr)+\sum_{m_0<m\le Y}\Bigl(\phi(m)-\int_{m-1}^m\phi\Bigr)-\int_Y^y\phi.
\]
Since $0\le\phi\le\frac14$, the first bracket is at most $m_0/4\le(\sqrt y+1)/4$ in size and the last integral at most $\frac14$. In the middle, $|\phi(m)-\int_{m-1}^m\phi|$ is at most the total variation of $\phi$ on $[m-1,m]$. These intervals lie in $[\sqrt y,y]$, and as $u$ runs over $[\sqrt y,y]$, $t=y/u$ runs over $[1,\sqrt y]$. On each unit interval $\beta$ climbs to $\frac14$ and falls back, so its variation there is $\frac12$, and $[1,\sqrt y]$ meets at most $\sqrt y+1$ unit intervals. The middle sum is at most $(\sqrt y+1)/2$. The total error is at most $\sqrt y+2\le3\sqrt y$.
\end{proof}

The third ingredient compares $\mathfrak S$ with the constant $1$. Following Volume~6, put $w(d)=\prod_{p\mid d}\frac p{p-2}$ for odd squarefree $d$, and $w(d)=0$ otherwise. Then, by~\eqref{eq:pairseries}, for $h=2m$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdivisor}
\mathfrak S(2m)=2C_2\sum_{d\mid m}\frac{w(d)}d,
\end{equation}
because both sides are multiplicative in the odd part of $m$, and at an odd prime $p$ the factor on the right is $1+\frac1{p-2}=\frac{p-1}{p-2}$. Volume~6 writes $g(d)$ for $w(d)/d$ and proves $\sum_dw(d)/d^2=1/C_2$.

Now compare $w$ with the constant function $1$. Define $\kappa$ by $w=1*\kappa$, that is, $\kappa(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)w(d)$. Since $w$ is multiplicative, so is $\kappa$, and its values at prime powers are $\kappa(p^e)=w(p^e)-w(p^{e-1})$:
\[
\kappa(2)=-1,\qquad\kappa(p)=\frac2{p-2},\qquad\kappa(p^2)=-\frac p{p-2}\quad(p>2),
\]
and $\kappa(p^e)=0$ in every other case with $e\ge1$. The function $\kappa$ is small. For $\sigma>\frac12$,
\[
M(\sigma):=\sum_n\frac{|\kappa(n)|}{n^\sigma}=\Bigl(1+\frac1{2^\sigma}\Bigr)\prod_{p>2}\Bigl(1+\frac2{(p-2)p^\sigma}+\frac{p^{1-2\sigma}}{p-2}\Bigr)<\infty,
\]
because $\frac p{p-2}\le3$ for $p\ge3$, so the extra terms are at most $\frac6{p^{1+\sigma}}+\frac3{p^{2\sigma}}$, and both series converge.

\begin{lemma}[$\kappa$ is small]\label{lem:kappa}
Let $0<\varepsilon\le\frac14$ and $y\ge1$, and put $M=M(\frac12+\varepsilon)$. Then
\[
\sum_{n\le y}|\kappa(n)|\le My^{\frac12+\varepsilon},\qquad\sum_{n\le y}\frac{|\kappa(n)|}{\sqrt n}\le My^\varepsilon,
\]
\[
\sum_{n>y}\frac{|\kappa(n)|}n\le My^{-\frac12+\varepsilon},\qquad\sum_{n>y}\frac{|\kappa(n)|}{n^2}\le My^{-\frac32+\varepsilon},\qquad\sum_{n>y}\frac{|\kappa(n)|\log n}n\le\frac M\varepsilon y^{-\frac12+2\varepsilon}.
\]
Moreover
\[
c_0:=\sum_n\frac{\kappa(n)}n=\frac1{2C_2},\qquad c_1:=\sum_n\frac{\kappa(n)\log n}n=-\frac{\log2}{2C_2}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The five bounds are Rankin's trick, as in Volume~6: multiply each term by a factor at least $1$. For $n\le y$ we have $1\le(y/n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}$, so $\sum_{n\le y}|\kappa(n)|\le y^{1/2+\varepsilon}\sum_n|\kappa(n)|n^{-1/2-\varepsilon}$, and the factor $(y/n)^\varepsilon$ gives the second bound. For $n>y$ the factors $(n/y)^{1/2-\varepsilon}$ and $(n/y)^{3/2-\varepsilon}$ give the third and fourth bounds. For the fifth use also $\log n\le n^\varepsilon/\varepsilon$.

All the series converge absolutely, so they may be computed as Euler products. The value $c_0$ is $\prod_p\sum_{e\ge0}\kappa(p^e)p^{-e}$. At $p=2$ the factor is $1-\frac12=\frac12$. At $p>2$ it is $1+\frac2{p(p-2)}-\frac1{p(p-2)}=\frac{(p-1)^2}{p(p-2)}$, the reciprocal of the factor of $C_2$. So $c_0=\frac1{2C_2}$.

For $c_1$, write $\log n=\sum_{p^e\parallel n}e\log p$ and split $n=p^em$ with $p\nmid m$. Absolute convergence lets us regroup:
\[
c_1=\sum_p\Bigl(\sum_{e\ge1}\frac{e\log p\,\kappa(p^e)}{p^e}\Bigr)\sum_{p\nmid m}\frac{\kappa(m)}m=c_0\sum_p\frac{\sum_{e\ge1}e\log p\,\kappa(p^e)p^{-e}}{\sum_{e\ge0}\kappa(p^e)p^{-e}}.
\]
For $p>2$ the numerator is $\log p\bigl(\frac2{p(p-2)}-\frac{2p}{(p-2)p^2}\bigr)=0$. For $p=2$ it is $-\frac12\log2$, and the denominator is $\frac12$. So $c_1=-c_0\log2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion}]
Put $D=H/2$, and let $\varepsilon\in]0,\frac18]$.

\emph{Step 1: a divisor sum.} Odd $h$ contribute nothing. For $h=2m$ use~\eqref{eq:sdivisor} and exchange the sums. The even $h\le H$ divisible by $2d$ are $h=2dk$ with $1\le k\le D/d$, so
\[
\sum_{h\le H}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)=2C_2\sum_{d\le D}\frac{w(d)}d\sum_{1\le k\le D/d}(H-2dk).
\]
Put $t=D/d$ and $N=\lfloor t\rfloor=t-\{t\}$. The inner sum is $2d\sum_{k\le N}(t-k)=2d\bigl(Nt-\frac{N(N+1)}2\bigr)$, and substituting $N=t-\{t\}$ gives $2d\bigl(\frac{t^2}2-\frac t2+\frac{\beta(t)}2\bigr)$. Since $2dt=H$,
\[
\sum_{1\le k\le D/d}(H-2dk)=\frac{H^2}{4d}-\frac H2+d\,\beta\Bigl(\frac Dd\Bigr).
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threesums}
\sum_{h\le H}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)=2C_2\Bigl(\frac{H^2}4B-\frac H2A+T\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with $A=\sum_{d\le D}\frac{w(d)}d$, $B=\sum_{d\le D}\frac{w(d)}{d^2}$ and $T=\sum_{d\le D}w(d)\beta(D/d)$.

\emph{Step 2: the sum $A$.} Since $w=1*\kappa$,
\[
A=\sum_{n\le D}\frac{\kappa(n)}n\sum_{m\le D/n}\frac1m=\sum_{n\le D}\frac{\kappa(n)}n\Bigl(\log\frac Dn+\gamma+O\Bigl(\frac nD\Bigr)\Bigr),
\]
using $\sum_{m\le y}\frac1m=\log y+\gamma+O(1/y)$ for $y\ge1$ (Volume~6, Lemma~2.5(a)). The error is $O(D^{-1}\sum_{n\le D}|\kappa(n)|)=O(D^{-1/2+\varepsilon})$. Completing the sums over $n$ with the tail bounds of Lemma~\ref{lem:kappa} costs $O_\varepsilon(D^{-1/2+2\varepsilon}\log D)$. So
\[
A=c_0(\log D+\gamma)-c_1+O_\varepsilon\bigl(D^{-\frac12+3\varepsilon}\bigr)=\frac1{2C_2}\bigl(\log H+\gamma\bigr)+O_\varepsilon\bigl(D^{-\frac12+3\varepsilon}\bigr),
\]
because $\log D+\log2=\log H$.

\emph{Step 3: the sum $B$.} In the same way, with $\sum_{m\le y}m^{-2}=\zeta(2)-\frac1y+O(y^{-2})$ for $y\ge1$ (compare the tail $\sum_{m>y}m^{-2}$ with $\int_y^\infty t^{-2}\,\dd t$),
\[
B=\sum_{n\le D}\frac{\kappa(n)}{n^2}\Bigl(\zeta(2)-\frac nD+O\Bigl(\frac{n^2}{D^2}\Bigr)\Bigr)=\zeta(2)\sum_n\frac{\kappa(n)}{n^2}-\frac{c_0}D+O_\varepsilon\bigl(D^{-\frac32+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
Here $\zeta(2)\sum_n\kappa(n)n^{-2}=\sum_dw(d)d^{-2}=\frac1{C_2}$, the value from Volume~6. Since $D=H/2$, $B=\frac1{C_2}-\frac1{C_2H}+O_\varepsilon(D^{-3/2+\varepsilon})$.

\emph{Step 4: the sum $T$.} Again $w=1*\kappa$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:riemannsum} with $y=D/n$ gives
\[
T=\sum_{n\le D}\kappa(n)\sum_{m\le D/n}\beta\Bigl(\frac{D/n}m\Bigr)=\sum_{n\le D}\kappa(n)\Bigl(\frac{ID}n+O\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac Dn}\Bigr)\Bigr)=c_0ID+O_\varepsilon\bigl(D^{\frac12+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
With $I=2-\log2\pi$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:fracint}, $T=\frac{H}{4C_2}(2-\log2\pi)+O_\varepsilon(D^{1/2+\varepsilon})$.

\emph{Step 5: assemble.} Put the three values into~\eqref{eq:threesums}:
\[
2C_2\Bigl(\frac{H^2}{4C_2}-\frac H{4C_2}-\frac H{4C_2}(\log H+\gamma)+\frac H{4C_2}(2-\log2\pi)\Bigr)=\frac{H^2}2-\frac12H\log H+\frac12(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)H.
\]
The errors are $H^2D^{-3/2+\varepsilon}$, $HD^{-1/2+3\varepsilon}$ and $D^{1/2+\varepsilon}$, all $O_\varepsilon(H^{1/2+3\varepsilon})$. Rename $3\varepsilon$ as $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

The theorem says exactly how the pair series fails to be $1$. Its mean is $1$, and the deficit $-\frac12H\log H$ comes from the sum $A$ of Step~2: every odd modulus $d$ up to $H/2$ contributes, and together they add up to a logarithm. That deficit is what makes the primes spread less than coins.

\section{The variance of primes in short intervals}

The question now is the size of $\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)-H$ for a typical $n$. Cram\'er's model has an answer. In that model each $m$ near $X$ is ``prime'' with probability $1/\log m$, independently, and weighting by $\log m$ gives each term mean $1$ and variance $\log m-1$. So the model predicts a variance of $H\log X+O(H)$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:cramervar}). Montgomery and Soundararajan found that the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture predicts something smaller. Here is their computation, with its two hypotheses stated.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Hypothesis HL$_2$.} For every $\varepsilon>0$, uniformly for integers $1\le h\le X^{1/2}$,
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)\Lambda(n+h)=\mathfrak S(h)\,X+O_\varepsilon\bigl(X^{\frac12+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
\end{quote}

Hypothesis HL$_2$ is a conjecture. No case of it is known: for $h=2$ it contains the twin prime conjecture. The second hypothesis is the Riemann hypothesis, in the form $\psi(Y)=Y+O_\varepsilon(Y^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ that Volume~6 proves equivalent to it (von Koch's theorem, Theorem~7.10).

\begin{theorem}[Montgomery and Soundararajan, 2004]\label{thm:pairsecond}
Assume the Riemann hypothesis and Hypothesis HL$_2$. Let $\varepsilon>0$, let $X$ and $H$ be integers with $2\le H\le X^{1/2}$, and put
\[
V(X,H)=\frac1X\sum_{n\le X}\bigl(\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)-H\bigr)^2.
\]
Then
\[
V(X,H)=H\log\frac XH-\bigl(\gamma+\log2\pi\bigr)H+O_\varepsilon\Bigl(H^{\frac12+\varepsilon}+\frac{H^2}{X^{1/2-\varepsilon}}\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

For $H\le X^{1/2-\delta}$, taking $\varepsilon<\delta$, both error terms are $o(H)$, so the constant $\gamma+\log2\pi=2.4151\ldots$ is part of the statement.

\begin{proof}
Write $\theta(n)=\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)=\sum_{j=1}^H\Lambda(n+j)$. Then
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\bigl(\theta(n)-H\bigr)^2=\sum_n\theta(n)^2-2H\sum_n\theta(n)+H^2X,
\]
and $\sum_n\theta(n)^2=\sum_{j,k=1}^H\sum_n\Lambda(n+j)\Lambda(n+k)$. We evaluate three pieces: the linear sum, the diagonal $j=k$, and the pairs $j\ne k$. All the $O$-constants below may depend on $\varepsilon$.

\emph{The linear sum.} For each $j$, $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n+j)=\psi(X+j)-\psi(j)$. The Riemann hypothesis gives $\psi(X)=X+O(X^{1/2+\varepsilon})$, and $\psi(X+j)-\psi(X)$ and $\psi(j)$ are both at most $j\log(2X)$. Summing over $j\le H$,
\[
\sum_n\theta(n)=HX+O\bigl(HX^{\frac12+\varepsilon}+H^2\log X\bigr).
\]

\emph{The diagonal.} First, $\sum_{m\le Y}\Lambda(m)^2=Y\log Y-Y+O(Y^{1/2+\varepsilon})$. Indeed, Abel summation (Volume~6, Lemma~1.1) and the Riemann hypothesis give
\[
\sum_{m\le Y}\Lambda(m)\log m=\psi(Y)\log Y-\int_1^Y\frac{\psi(t)}t\,\dd t=Y\log Y-Y+O\bigl(Y^{\frac12+\varepsilon}\log Y\bigr),
\]
and $\Lambda(m)^2$ differs from $\Lambda(m)\log m$ only at prime powers $p^a$ with $a\ge2$. There the difference is $(a-1)\log^2p\le\log^2Y$, and there are $O(Y^{1/2}\log Y)$ such $p^a\le Y$. Now $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n+j)^2$ is the sum over $j<m\le X+j$, which differs from the sum over $m\le X$ by at most $2j\log^2(2X)$. So each diagonal term is $X\log X-X+O(X^{1/2+\varepsilon}+H\log^2X)$, and the $H$ of them give
\[
HX\log X-HX+O\bigl(HX^{\frac12+\varepsilon}+H^2\log^2X\bigr).
\]

\emph{The pairs.} For $j\ne k$ put $h=|j-k|$, so $1\le h\le H-1\le X^{1/2}$. Substituting $m=n+\min(j,k)$,
\[
\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n+j)\Lambda(n+k)=\sum_{m\le X}\Lambda(m)\Lambda(m+h)+O\bigl(H\log^2(3X)\bigr),
\]
because the two ranges of $m$ differ by at most $2H$ terms, each at most $\log^2(3X)$. Hypothesis HL$_2$ turns the main term into $\mathfrak S(h)X+O(X^{1/2+\varepsilon})$. Each $h$ arises from $2(H-h)$ ordered pairs $(j,k)$, so the pairs contribute
\[
2X\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)+O\bigl(H^2X^{\frac12+\varepsilon}+H^3\log^2X\bigr).
\]

\emph{Assemble.} Dividing by $X$,
\[
V(X,H)=H\log X-H+2\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)-2H^2+H^2+E,
\]
where $E$ collects the errors and is $O(H^2X^{-1/2+\varepsilon}+H^3X^{-1}\log^2X)$. Since $H\le X^{1/2}$, the second term is at most the first. The term $h=H$ has weight $0$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion} evaluates the sum:
\[
2\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)=H^2-H\log H+(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)H+O\bigl(H^{\frac12+\varepsilon}\bigr).
\]
The $H^2$ terms cancel, and what is left is $H\log X-H\log H-H+(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)H=H\log\frac XH-(\gamma+\log2\pi)H$.
\end{proof}

Compare the two predictions. Cram\'er's model says $H\log X$. The Hardy--Littlewood conjecture says $H\log(X/H)$. At $H=X^{1/3}$ the conjectured variance is two thirds of the model's. The model is wrong at long range because it ignores divisibility by all the small primes at once: the $-H\log H$ here is the deficit $-\frac12H\log H$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion}, doubled, and that came from the sum $A$ of Step~2, whose moduli run up to $H/2$.

\begin{remark}[counting primes instead of weighting them]
Volume~6 measured the number of primes $\pi(n+H)-\pi(n)$, not $\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)$. It also counted in a window near $10^9$, while Theorem~\ref{thm:pairsecond} averages over all $n\le X$, where the mean of $\log n$ is $\log X-1$. In a window near $X$, both $\Lambda$ and the weighted indicator $\log X\cdot\mathbf 1_{\text{prime}}$ have mean square about $\log X$ and the same correlations $\mathfrak S(h)$, so one $H$ moves and the constant becomes $\gamma+\log2\pi-1=1.415$. (For $n$ in $]0.9\cdot10^8,10^8]$ and $H=100$, the variance is $1237.8$. The formula with $X=0.95\cdot10^8$, the middle of the window, gives $1234.9$ with the constant $1.415$ and $1134.9$ with $2.415$.) The prediction for the variance of the count is then $\lambda\bigl(1-(\log H+1.415)/\log X\bigr)$, where $\lambda=H/\log X$ is the mean. With $H=21$ and $\log X=20.7$ this is $0.785\lambda$, and with $\lambda=1.01$ it is $0.79$, which is what Volume~6 observed. We do not carry out the partial summation that turns one form into the other.
\end{remark}

\section{Gallagher's average for \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}-tuples}\label{sec:gallagherk}

For a Poisson law we need every moment, so every $k$. Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion} was a precise statement for $k=2$. For general $k$ a weaker statement is enough: on average over $k$-tuples, $\mathfrak S$ is $1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:localavg} says this is true prime by prime. The work is to show that the infinitely many primes do not spoil it.

\begin{theorem}[Gallagher, 1976]\label{thm:gallagherk}
Fix $k\ge1$, and let $\mathcal H_k(H)$ be the set of $k$-tuples of distinct integers in $[1,H]$. Then, as $H\to\infty$,
\[
\sum_{\mathbf h\in\mathcal H_k(H)}\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)=H^k\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The plan is to cut the product at a height $z$. The finite product $\mathfrak S_z(\mathbf h)=\prod_{p\le z}b_p(\mathbf h)$ is periodic in each $h_i$, so its average is $1$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:localavg}. The tail $\prod_{p>z}b_p$ is close to $1$ except when a large prime divides a difference $h_i-h_j$, and that is rare.

\begin{proof}
For $k=1$, $\mathfrak S=1$ and there is nothing to prove, so let $k\ge2$. Write $\mathbb E$ for the average over $\mathcal H_k(H)$, which has $H(H-1)\cdots(H-k+1)=H^k(1+O(k^2/H))$ elements. We may take $H\ge2k$. Let $z\ge8k^2$, and put $M_z=\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)^{-k}$, so that $0\le\mathfrak S_z\le M_z$. By Mertens's theorem (Volume~6, Theorem~1.6), $M_z\le C_k(\log z)^k$.

\emph{Step 1: the truncated series averages to $1$.} Let $P=\prod_{p\le z}p$. The value of $\mathfrak S_z(\mathbf h)$ depends only on $\mathbf h$ modulo $P$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, as $\mathbf h$ runs over $(\Z/P\Z)^k$, its reductions modulo the primes $p\le z$ run over $\prod_{p\le z}(\Z/p\Z)^k$, each combination once. So the average of $\mathfrak S_z$ over $(\Z/P\Z)^k$ is the product of the averages of the $b_p$, which is $1$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:localavg}. The box $[1,H]^k$ contains $\lfloor H/P\rfloor^k$ disjoint translates of a full period, and the points of the box outside them number at most $kPH^{k-1}$. The tuples with a repeated entry number at most $k^2H^{k-1}$. Hence
\[
\mathbb E\,\mathfrak S_z=1+O_{k,z}\Bigl(\frac1H\Bigr).
\]

\emph{Step 2: the tail is small on average.} Fix $\mathbf h\in\mathcal H_k(H)$ and put $f=\prod_{p>z}b_p(\mathbf h)$, so $\mathfrak S=\mathfrak S_zf$. For $|u|\le\frac12$ we have $|\log(1+u)|\le2|u|$. Since $p>z\ge8k^2$, the deviations from $1$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:bp} are at most $\frac12$, so $|\log b_p|\le2k^2/p^2$ when $p\nmid\Delta$ and $|\log b_p|\le8k/p$ when $p\mid\Delta$. With $\sum_{p>z}p^{-2}\le\sum_{n>z}\frac1{n(n-1)}\le\frac2z$,
\[
|\log f|\le L:=\frac{4k^2}z+8k\,\Omega_z,\qquad\Omega_z=\sum_{i<j}\omega_z\bigl(|h_i-h_j|\bigr),\quad\omega_z(n)=\sum_{\substack{p\mid n\\p>z}}\frac1p.
\]
So $|f-1|\le e^L-1\le Le^L$, and $|\mathfrak S-\mathfrak S_z|\le M_zLe^L$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
\[
\mathbb E|\mathfrak S-\mathfrak S_z|\le M_z\bigl(\mathbb E L^2\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\mathbb E e^{2L}\bigr)^{1/2}.
\]

We need the law of one difference. For $i<j$ the pair $(h_i,h_j)$ is uniform over ordered pairs of distinct integers in $[1,H]$, so for $1\le n<H$,
\[
P\bigl(|h_i-h_j|=n\bigr)=\frac{2(H-n)}{H(H-1)}\le\frac2H.
\]
Put $K=\binom k2$, the number of pairs.

\emph{The second moment of $L$.} By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $\Omega_z^2\le K\sum_{i<j}\omega_z(|h_i-h_j|)^2$. For one pair,
\[
\mathbb E\,\omega_z(|h_i-h_j|)^2\le\frac2H\sum_{n<H}\sum_{\substack{p,q\mid n\\p,q>z}}\frac1{pq}\le2\sum_{p,q>z}\frac1{pq\operatorname{lcm}(p,q)}=2\sum_{p>z}\frac1{p^3}+2\sum_{\substack{p\ne q\\p,q>z}}\frac1{p^2q^2}\le\frac{12}{z^2}.
\]
Hence $\mathbb E\,\Omega_z^2\le12K^2/z^2$, and $\mathbb E L^2\le2(4k^2/z)^2+128k^2\,\mathbb E\,\Omega_z^2\le C'_k/z^2$.

\emph{The exponential moment.} Since $z\ge8k^2$, $e^{2L}\le e\cdot e^{16k\Omega_z}$. For $p\ge2$, $\log(1+1/p)\ge1/(2p)$, so $e^{16k/p}\le(1+1/p)^{32k}$ and $e^{16k\,\omega_z(n)}\le W(n):=\prod_{p\mid n}(1+1/p)^{32k}$. Hölder's inequality with $K$ factors gives
\[
\mathbb E\,e^{16k\Omega_z}\le\mathbb E\prod_{i<j}W\bigl(|h_i-h_j|\bigr)\le\prod_{i<j}\Bigl(\mathbb E\,W\bigl(|h_i-h_j|\bigr)^K\Bigr)^{1/K}.
\]
Let $A=32kK$, so $W(n)^K=\prod_{p\mid n}(1+1/p)^A=\sum_{d\mid n}c(d)$, where $c$ is multiplicative, supported on squarefree $d$, with $c(p)=(1+1/p)^A-1\le A2^A/p$ by the mean value theorem. Then
\[
\mathbb E\,W\bigl(|h_i-h_j|\bigr)^K\le\frac2H\sum_{n<H}\sum_{d\mid n}c(d)\le2\sum_d\frac{c(d)}d\le2\prod_p\Bigl(1+\frac{A2^A}{p^2}\Bigr)=:C''_k,
\]
a constant that depends only on $k$. So $\mathbb E\,e^{2L}\le eC''_k$.

Putting the pieces together, for $z\ge8k^2$ and all $H\ge2k$,
\[
\mathbb E|\mathfrak S-\mathfrak S_z|\le C_k(\log z)^k\cdot\frac{\sqrt{C'_k}}z\cdot\sqrt{eC''_k}=O_k\Bigl(\frac{(\log z)^k}z\Bigr).
\]

\emph{Step 3: conclusion.} By Steps 1 and 2, $|\mathbb E\,\mathfrak S-1|\le\mathbb E|\mathfrak S-\mathfrak S_z|+|\mathbb E\,\mathfrak S_z-1|$, so $\limsup_{H\to\infty}|\mathbb E\,\mathfrak S-1|=O_k((\log z)^k/z)$ for every $z\ge8k^2$. Let $z\to\infty$: $\mathbb E\,\mathfrak S\to1$. Multiply by $|\mathcal H_k(H)|=H^k(1+o(1))$.
\end{proof}

The proof uses only one fact about the primes beyond Mertens's theorem: that the local average is exactly $1$. Everything else is the observation that a large prime rarely divides a difference. That is why Cram\'er's model, so wrong about individual patterns, is right about all of them at once.

\section{Gallagher's Poisson law}

A random variable $N$ with values in $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ is pinned down by its \emph{binomial moments} $S_j=\mathbb E\binom Nj$, when they do not grow too fast. The next lemma makes that precise in the form we need. It is Bonferroni's inequality, which Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-sieves} will prove for events, written here for counts.

\begin{lemma}[from binomial moments to probabilities]\label{lem:binmoments}
Let $N$ be a bounded random variable with values in $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$, and $S_j=\mathbb E\binom Nj$. For all integers $m\ge0$ and $r\ge0$,
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{2r+1}(-1)^j\binom{m+j}mS_{m+j}\le P(N=m)\le\sum_{j=0}^{2r}(-1)^j\binom{m+j}mS_{m+j}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a value $N=n$ and a cut-off $J$. Choosing $m+j$ objects out of $n$ and then $m$ of those is the same as choosing $m$ out of $n$ and then $j$ more out of the other $n-m$, so $\binom{m+j}m\binom n{m+j}=\binom nm\binom{n-m}j$. Hence
\[
\sum_{j=0}^J(-1)^j\binom{m+j}m\binom n{m+j}=\binom nm\sum_{j=0}^J(-1)^j\binom{n-m}j.
\]
If $n<m$ this is $0=\mathbf 1_{n=m}$. If $n=m$ it is $1=\mathbf 1_{n=m}$. If $n>m$, put $s=n-m\ge1$. By induction on $J$, using Pascal's rule, $\sum_{j=0}^J(-1)^j\binom sj=(-1)^J\binom{s-1}J$, which is $\ge0$ for even $J$ and $\le0$ for odd $J$, while $\mathbf 1_{n=m}=0$. So for every outcome the truncated sum lies above $\mathbf 1_{N=m}$ when $J$ is even and below it when $J$ is odd. Take expectations.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis for the Poisson law is the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture for all $k$, uniform over tuples of logarithmic size.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Hypothesis HL$^*$.} For each fixed $k\ge1$ and $C>0$,
\[
\#\bigl\{X<n\le2X:\ n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k\text{ all prime}\bigr\}=\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)\frac X{(\log X)^k}+o\Bigl(\frac X{(\log X)^k}\Bigr),
\]
uniformly for distinct integers $h_1,\dots,h_k\in[1,C\log X]$, as $X\to\infty$.
\end{quote}

For $k=1$ this is the prime number theorem, since $\mathfrak S(h_1)=1$. For $k\ge2$ it is open.

\begin{theorem}[Gallagher, 1976]\label{thm:gallagherpoisson}
Assume Hypothesis HL$^*$. Fix $\lambda>0$, and let $H=H(X)$ be integers with $H/\log X\to\lambda$. Let $n$ be uniform among the integers of $]X,2X]$, and let $N=\pi(n+H)-\pi(n)$ be the number of primes in $]n,n+H]$. Then for every integer $m\ge0$,
\[
P(N=m)\longrightarrow e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\qquad(X\to\infty).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The binomial moments.} $\binom Nk$ counts the sets $\{h_1<\dots<h_k\}\subseteq[1,H]$ with all $n+h_i$ prime. Exchange the average over $n$ with the sum over sets, and then pass from sets to ordered tuples:
\[
\mathbb E\binom Nk=\frac1X\sum_{h_1<\dots<h_k}\#\{X<n\le2X:\text{all }n+h_i\text{ prime}\}=\frac1{k!}\sum_{\mathbf h\in\mathcal H_k(H)}\Bigl(\frac{\mathfrak S(\mathbf h)}{(\log X)^k}+o\Bigl(\frac1{(\log X)^k}\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
We used HL$^*$ with $C=2\lambda$, valid once $H\le2\lambda\log X$. The $o$-terms number at most $H^k$, and $H^k/(\log X)^k\to\lambda^k$, so together they are $o(1)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherk} evaluates the main term. Hence
\[
\mathbb E\binom Nk=\frac1{k!}\frac{H^k(1+o(1))}{(\log X)^k}+o(1)\longrightarrow\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}.
\]
These are the binomial moments of the Poisson law of mean $\lambda$.

\emph{From moments to probabilities.} $N\le H$ is bounded, so Lemma~\ref{lem:binmoments} applies. Letting $X\to\infty$ with $r$ fixed,
\[
\limsup P(N=m)\le\sum_{j=0}^{2r}(-1)^j\binom{m+j}m\frac{\lambda^{m+j}}{(m+j)!}=\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\sum_{j=0}^{2r}\frac{(-\lambda)^j}{j!},
\]
and $\liminf P(N=m)$ is at least the same expression with $2r+1$ in place of $2r$. As $r\to\infty$ both bounds tend to $\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}e^{-\lambda}$.
\end{proof}

Check the mean. With $k=1$ the computation says $\mathbb E N\to\lambda$, and that part is the prime number theorem, with no hypothesis at all. Everything past the mean is conditional.

\section{What stays open}

Three results mark the limits of what this chapter can say.

\emph{Maier's theorem.} The Poisson law is about a random starting point. It cannot be about every starting point. Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-models} proved Maier's theorem: for $H=(\log X)^\lambda$ with $\lambda>1$ fixed, the ratio $(\pi(x+H)-\pi(x))/(H/\log x)$ has limsup above $1$ and liminf below $1$. Maier's intervals start near multiples of a primorial, a thin set of starting points, so they do not contradict Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherpoisson}. They do show that $\pi(x+H)-\pi(x)\sim H/\log x$ fails for some $x$, so no statement about every interval of this length can be true.

\emph{Short intervals, unconditionally.} Volume~14 proves that every interval $]x,x+x^\theta]$ with $\theta>\frac34$ holds about $x^\theta/\log x$ primes (Corollary~1.9). Under the Riemann hypothesis Selberg proved in 1943 that almost every interval of length $h(x)$ contains a prime once $h(x)/(\log x)^2\to\infty$. Neither result reaches intervals of length $\lambda\log x$.

\emph{Beyond the second moment, and beyond $X^{1/2}$.} Montgomery and Soundararajan conjectured that $\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)-H$, divided by $\sqrt{H\log(X/H)}$, is asymptotically Gaussian for $X^\delta\le H\le X^{1-\delta}$. Assuming a strong uniform form of HL$^*$, they computed the $k$-th moment and found the Gaussian value when $H\le X^{1/k-\varepsilon}$. The Gaussian law for $H$ a fixed power of $X$ remains a conjecture. Theorem~\ref{thm:pairsecond} stops at $H=X^{1/2}$. Past that point the variance is tied to the zeros of $\zeta$: Goldston and Montgomery proved that, under the Riemann hypothesis, the asymptotic $V(X,H)\sim H\log(X/H)$ for $X^\varepsilon\le H\le X^{1-\varepsilon}$ is equivalent to a strong form of Montgomery's pair-correlation conjecture. That conjecture is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-gue}.

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:triples}
\idea Only the primes $p\le k$ can kill a $k$-tuple, so look at $2$ and $3$.
\writeup (a) Modulo $3$ the residues of $0,2,4$ are $0,2,1$, so $\nu_3=3$ and $b_3=0$. (b) Modulo $2$ all of $0,2,6$ are $0$, so $\nu_2=1$ and $b_2=\frac{1/2}{1/8}=4$. Modulo $3$ the residues are $0,2,0$, so $\nu_3=2$ and $b_3=\frac{1/3}{8/27}=\frac98$. For $p>3$ the differences $2,4,6$ are not divisible by $p$, so $\nu_p=3$ and $b_p=\frac{1-3/p}{(1-1/p)^3}=\frac{p^2(p-3)}{(p-1)^3}$. The product of the first two factors is $4\cdot\frac98=\frac92$. Each remaining factor is positive, and for $p\ge7$ it is at least $1-9/p^2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:bp}(a), while $p=5$ gives $\frac{50}{64}>0$. So the infinite product converges to a positive number.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:cramervar}
\idea A sum of independent centred terms has variance equal to the sum of the variances.
\writeup Put $Y_m=\xi_m\log m-1$. Then $\mathbb EY_m=\frac{\log m}{\log m}-1=0$ and $\mathbb EY_m^2=\log^2m\cdot\frac1{\log m}-2+1=\log m-1$. The $Y_{n+j}$ are independent, so the expectation is $\sum_{j=1}^H(\log(n+j)-1)$. For $X<n\le2X$ and $j\le H\le X$, $\log(n+j)=\log X+O(1)$, and the sum is $H\log X+O(H)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:volume6check}
\idea Divide both predictions by $H\log X$.
\writeup (a) The ratio is $1-\frac{\log H+\gamma+\log2\pi}{\log X}=1-\frac{3.045+2.415}{20.72}=0.737$. (b) With $1.415$ it is $1-\frac{3.045+1.415}{20.72}=0.785$. For the count, Cram\'er's model gives a variance equal to the mean $1.01$, and $0.785\times1.01=0.79$, the measured value.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:paircheck}
\idea Differencing a smooth main term is harmless, but differencing an error term of size $H^{1/2+\varepsilon}$ leaves an error of the same size.
\writeup Let $F(H)=\sum_{h\le H}(H-h)\mathfrak S(h)$. Then $F(H+1)-F(H)=\sum_{h\le H}\mathfrak S(h)$, because each $h\le H$ gains weight $1$ and $h=H+1$ enters with weight $0$. By the theorem, $F(H+1)-F(H)$ equals the difference of the main terms plus $O_\varepsilon(H^{1/2+\varepsilon})$. The main terms differ by $\frac12(2H+1)-\frac12\bigl((H+1)\log(H+1)-H\log H\bigr)+\frac12(1-\gamma-\log2\pi)=H+O(\log H)$. So $\sum_{h\le H}\mathfrak S(h)=H+O_\varepsilon(H^{1/2+\varepsilon})$. The term $-\frac12\log H$ is much smaller than the error, so it is lost. The theorem sees it only in integrated form, through the weights $H-h$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:poissonzero}
\idea The truncations of $e^{-\lambda}=1-\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}2-\cdots$ alternate around it.
\writeup In the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherpoisson}, $S_k\to\lambda^k/k!$. Lemma~\ref{lem:binmoments} with $m=0$ gives $1-S_1+S_2-S_3\le P(N=0)\le1-S_1+S_2$. Let $X\to\infty$: $\limsup P(N=0)\le1-\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}2$ and $\liminf P(N=0)\ge1-\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}2-\frac{\lambda^3}6$. For $\lambda=1$ these are $0.5$ and $0.333$. The theorem gives the limit $e^{-1}=0.368$, between the two.
\end{solution}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The epigraph asked whether the number of primes in a random interval of length $\lambda\log x$ is a Poisson variable. The answer has three layers. The mean is $\lambda$, a theorem with no hypothesis: it is the prime number theorem. The Poisson law follows from the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture for all $k$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherpoisson}), because Gallagher's average (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherk}) is proved unconditionally; the conjecture is the only missing piece. On longer intervals the variance is \emph{not} the Poisson variance: the pair average to second order (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairexpansion}) is proved, and under the Riemann hypothesis and HL$_2$ it gives $H\log\frac XH-(\gamma+\log2\pi)H$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairsecond}), smaller than Cram\'er's $H\log X$. That deficit is what Volume~6 saw as a narrow histogram, and it is a consequence of divisibility by small primes, which the coins do not know about.

So the primes in short intervals carry one theorem with no hypothesis (the mean), one proved computation that becomes a theorem the day HL$_2$ does (the variance), and one law that waits for all of HL$^*$ (Poisson). The binomial moments did the work in the last proof, and Lemma~\ref{lem:binmoments} was inclusion--exclusion written as an expectation. The next chapter takes that sentence seriously: a sieve is an expectation, and the parity barrier is what an expectation cannot see.
