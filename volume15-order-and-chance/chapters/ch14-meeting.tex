\chapter{The meeting}\label{ch:meeting}

\begin{center}\itshape
Can pure order and pure chance be told apart, and why do the primes look random and yet obey laws?
\end{center}

That question has been asked, in one form or another, since the first volume of this series. This chapter answers it as far as mathematics can today. The first section is a ledger: every statistic the book has studied, the random model for it, and an exact statement of what is known, with the place where it is proved. The second section gives the answer in two pages. The last sections end the series the way research begins: with a method for reading a paper, and two projects that start from proved theorems and stop on a line that nobody has crossed.

\section{The ledger}\label{sec:ledger}

Each line of the ledger says three things. \emph{The model}: which random object the statistic is compared with. \emph{The agreement}: whether the match is a theorem of this series, a theorem quoted from the literature, a theorem under a named hypothesis, or a conjecture. \emph{The failure}: where the model is known to be wrong, if anywhere. A model can be right in bulk and wrong at a fine scale, and the ledger keeps the two apart. The summary comes first; the entries that follow give the references.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{1. Prime factors.} \emph{Model:} the events $\{p\mid n\}$, independent with probabilities $\frac1p$ (the Kubilius model). \emph{Agreement:} Tur\'an--Kubilius (Theorem~\ref{thm:tkgeneral}), Kubilius's comparison (Theorem~\ref{thm:kubilius}), Erd\H{o}s--Wintner (Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoswintner}) and Erd\H{o}s--Kac (Volume~6, Theorem~9.7, and again here as Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac-cor}) are theorems. \emph{Failure:} at the scale of large deviations the coins miss a factor: Selberg--Delange (Theorem~\ref{thm:sd}, Corollary~\ref{cor:omegasd}) shows that the generating function of $\omega(n)$ carries $\frac1{\Gamma(z+1)}$ and an Euler product, which no independent model produces. \emph{Open:} Erd\H{o}s--Kac in intervals as short as $(\log x)^A$ (Section~\ref{sec:projectomega}).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{2. Survivors of a sieve.} \emph{Model:} the same events, for $p<z$. \emph{Agreement:} a theorem for $z\le x^{1/(10\log\log x)}$, by Brun's pure sieve (Corollary~\ref{cor:brunpure}). \emph{Failure:} at $z=x^{1/u}$ with $u$ fixed the ratio to the model tends to $e^\gamma\omega(u)$, not $1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sievefail}); at $u=2$ the model is $12\%$ too large. This failure is a theorem, and it is the seed of Maier's.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{3. Primes in long intervals.} \emph{Model:} Cram\'er's coins, $P(n\text{ prime})=\frac1{\log n}$ (Volume~6, Definition~10.1). \emph{Agreement:} the prime number theorem (Volume~6, Theorem~5.5) is a theorem. \emph{Open:} the model's error term $O(\sqrt{x\log x})$ (Volume~6, Theorem~10.2) is, for the primes, the Riemann hypothesis.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{4. Primes in short intervals.} \emph{Model:} Cram\'er's coins predict $(\log x)^{\lambda-1}$ primes in $]x,x+(\log x)^\lambda]$, for every $\lambda>2$ almost surely (Proposition~\ref{prop:cramershort}). \emph{Failure, a theorem:} Maier's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:maier}) proves that the count is infinitely often larger, and infinitely often smaller, by a constant factor, for every $\lambda>1$. It is unconditional. \emph{Conditional:} Gallagher's Poisson law for $\lambda=1$ under the Hardy--Littlewood conjectures (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagherpoisson}), and the pair average to second order under RH and HL$_2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairsecond}). \emph{Proved for longer intervals:} Hoheisel's theorem, primes in $]x,x+x^\theta]$ for some $\theta<1$ (Volume~14, Theorem~1.9).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{5. Primes in progressions.} \emph{Model:} the coins split evenly among the $\varphi(q)$ classes. \emph{Agreement:} Siegel--Walfisz (Volume~14, Corollary~2.12) for small moduli, Bombieri--Vinogradov (Volume~14, Theorem~4.7) on average up to $\sqrt x$, Gallagher for one large modulus with its exceptional term (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagher}), all theorems. \emph{Failure:} the race between classes is biased towards the non-squares; under GRH and LI the bias has a limit law (Theorem~\ref{thm:rubinsteinsarnak}), and $4n+3$ leads on a share $0.9959$ of the logarithmic scale. \emph{Open:} GRH, LI, and the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{6. Gaps.} \emph{Model:} Cram\'er's coins predict a largest gap of size $\log^2x$ (Volume~6, Theorem~10.4); Granville's refinement, which sieves by the small primes first, predicts at least $2e^{-\gamma}\log^2x$. \emph{Agreement:} Erd\H{o}s--Rankin (Theorem~\ref{thm:erdosrankin}) proves gaps of size $\frac{\log X\log_2X\log_4X}{(\log_3X)^2}$, far below the model. For small gaps, the series proved that $p_{n+1}-p_n\le4580$ infinitely often (Volume~14, Theorem~9.8 and Exercise~9.1); the bounds $600$ (Maynard) and $246$ (Polymath) are quoted records. \emph{Open:} Cram\'er's conjecture and Granville's correction are conjectures; the twin prime conjecture is open.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{7. Values of $\mu$.} \emph{Model:} a random multiplicative function with values $\pm1$ on the primes. \emph{Agreement:} for the model, Wintner's square-root cancellation (Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}) and its orthogonality to fixed sequences and to its own shifts (Proposition~\ref{prop:modelchowla}) are theorems. For $\mu$ itself, $M(x)=o(x)$ is the prime number theorem (Volume~6, Exercise~5.3). \emph{Open:} square-root cancellation for $\mu$ is the Riemann hypothesis; Chowla's and Sarnak's conjectures are conjectures, with quoted partial results (Matom\"aki--Radziwi\l\l, Tao).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{8. Class groups.} \emph{Model:} the Cohen--Lenstra measure, $P(G)\propto\frac1{|\operatorname{Aut}G|}$, realised by cokernels of random matrices. \emph{Agreement:} the measure, its moments and their uniqueness are theorems (Theorems~\ref{thm:cokmoments}, \ref{thm:fw}, \ref{thm:woodunique}). The match with class groups of quadratic fields is the Cohen--Lenstra heuristic; its one proved case, the average of the $3$-torsion (Davenport--Heilbronn), is quoted. \emph{Failure:} the $2$-part, which genus theory controls, does not follow the measure.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{9. Ranks and Selmer groups.} \emph{Model:} the kernel of a random alternating matrix. \emph{Agreement:} the law of that kernel, the Poonen--Rains law and its moments, are theorems (Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw}, Corollary~\ref{cor:prmoments}). Bhargava and Shankar's averages of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_n$ for $n\le5$ are quoted, and they bound the average rank (Proposition~\ref{prop:avgrank}). \emph{Open:} the Poonen--Rains heuristic, Goldfeld's conjecture and the minimalist conjecture.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{10. Pairs of zeros.} \emph{Model:} the eigenvalues of a large random unitary or Hermitian matrix, with the sine kernel (Volume~12, Theorem~10.4). \emph{Agreement:} Montgomery's theorem (Volume~14, Theorem~14.15) under RH, for test functions with Fourier support in $|\alpha|<1$, where it agrees with the form factor of the sine kernel (Theorem~\ref{thm:formfactor}). \emph{Open:} Montgomery's conjecture for $|\alpha|\ge1$, and the identification of the zeros with a spectrum.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{11. Moments of $\zeta$.} \emph{Model:} a random Euler product times the characteristic polynomial of a random unitary matrix. \emph{Agreement:} the second moment (Theorem~\ref{thm:ingham}) and lower bounds of the right order for every moment (Theorem~\ref{thm:momentlower}) are theorems; Ingham's fourth moment is quoted; the two factors are theorems in their own models (Proposition~\ref{prop:eulermoment}; Volume~12, Theorem~10.10). \emph{Failure:} neither factor alone gives the right constant. \emph{Open:} the Keating--Snaith conjecture for $k\ge3$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{12. Values of $\zeta$.} \emph{Model:} the random Euler product, and on the critical line a Gaussian. \emph{Agreement:} Bohr--Jessen off the line (Theorem~\ref{thm:bohrjessen}) and Selberg's central limit theorem on it (Theorem~\ref{thm:selberg}) are theorems, unconditional; so is the independence at fixed distinct shifts (Exercise~\ref{ex:multipoint}). \emph{Failure:} at every computed height the variance is larger than $\frac12\log\log T$ by a constant that random matrices predict. \emph{Open:} the Fyodorov--Hiary--Keating conjecture for the maximum.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{13. Three-term progressions.} \emph{Model:} a random set of the same density; for the primes, the coins with the small primes put back. \emph{Agreement:} a set either correlates with a character or has the random count (Theorem~\ref{thm:u2count}, Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}); random sets and quadratic residues are pseudorandom (Proposition~\ref{prop:randomu2}). For the primes, the count with the local factors $\mathfrak S_3$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:local3}) is van der Corput's theorem, quoted, and progressions of every length are the Green--Tao theorem, quoted. \emph{Failure:} without the $W$-trick, the primes correlate with $(-1)^n$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{14. The prime race.} \emph{Model:} a fair coin between two classes. \emph{Failure, a theorem:} Littlewood (quoted) proved that the lead changes infinitely often, and under GRH and LI the share of $4n+3$ is $\delta(4;3,1)\in\,]\frac12,1[$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:biasdensity}). \emph{Open:} GRH and LI themselves.

\section{The answer}\label{sec:answer}

\emph{In bulk, order and chance cannot be told apart, and that is a collection of theorems.} The number of prime factors of a random integer is Gaussian, exactly as independent coins would make it (line~1). The primes up to $x$ number $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ plus an error, exactly as Cram\'er's coins would make it (line~3). They split evenly among residue classes, on average over moduli up to $\sqrt x$ (line~5). The logarithm of $\zeta$ at a random height is a sum of independent random rotations, one for each prime, off the critical line and on it (line~12). In each case the deterministic object passes the test that a random object would pass, and in each case the proof is the same in spirit: the arithmetic object is compared with a sum of nearly independent pieces, and the comparison is controlled by a mean value. Unique factorisation makes the pieces independent: the numbers $\log p$ are linearly independent over $\Q$, the events $p\mid n$ are nearly independent for small $p$, and nothing more is needed.

\emph{At fine scales, order and chance can be told apart, and Maier's theorem is the proof.} In an interval of length $(\log x)^\lambda$, Cram\'er's coins predict, almost surely, the expected number of primes; the primes themselves overshoot and undershoot by a constant factor, infinitely often (line~4). The reason is visible in the proof. The small primes are not coins: they are a fixed sieve, the same in every interval, and when an interval is aligned with a product of small primes, the sieve's own irregularity, measured by Buchstab's function, shows through. The same mechanism makes the sieve fail at $z=x^{1/u}$ (line~2), makes the gaps longer than independent coins could explain (line~6), and makes the race between residue classes biased (line~14). Every failure of the coins in this book is a failure to account for the small primes, or for the squares, which are the same thing seen from the other side. Put the small primes back, by a singular series or a $W$-trick, and the coins become an excellent model again (line~13), as far as anyone has been able to test.

So the primes are \emph{random given their local structure}. That sentence is a summary of the theorems, not a theorem. What is proved is a list of statistics in which the corrected model and the primes agree, and a list in which the uncorrected model fails; what is conjectured, in the Hardy--Littlewood conjectures and their descendants, is that the corrected model never fails at all.

\emph{The second half of the question has a different answer.} The primes obey laws because the integers have a multiplicative structure; they look random because that structure, seen through addition, has no reason to align with anything. That explains the Euler product side of every formula in this book. It does not explain the other side. The zeros of $\zeta$ have the statistics of the eigenvalues of a random unitary matrix: their pair correlation, as far as Montgomery's theorem reaches (line~10), the moments of $\zeta$, where the matrix factor is needed to get the constants right (line~11), the variance of $\log|\zeta|$ at every height computed (line~12), and the maximum, as far as anyone has checked. \emph{Why the zeros of $\zeta$ follow the statistics of a random unitary matrix is not known.} No operator whose eigenvalues are the zeros has been found, and no argument says that one must exist.

The same is true of the other two models of Part~II. Class groups follow the Cohen--Lenstra measure in every table ever computed, and ranks follow the alternating matrices in every average that has been proved, and nobody knows why arithmetic objects should be distributed like cokernels of random matrices. In each case the random model is a theorem about matrices, the match with arithmetic is a theorem in a few cases, and the reason is a mystery.

This is where the series stops. It has proved, with full proofs, almost every statement in the first column of the ledger that the mathematics of today can prove at this level, and it has marked each of the others as quoted, conditional or conjectural. The two halves of the answer, in bulk the primes are random and at fine scales they are not, are both theorems, and they are proved in this book. The third half, why spectra appear in arithmetic, is the question that the next generation of mathematicians will inherit.

\section{Reading a paper: three passes}\label{sec:threepasses}

A research paper is not a textbook chapter. Its first page is written for a reader who must decide whether the result is the one they need; its middle is written for the reader who will check a lemma; its last page says which tradition the argument comes from. Reading it in one pass, from the first line to the last, is the wrong tool.

\emph{The first pass} asks what is claimed. Read the statement of the main theorem and the shape of the hypotheses, and not the proof. For Maier's theorem, Theorem~\ref{thm:maier}, the claim is that for every $\lambda>1$ the $\limsup$ of $(\pi(x+(\log x)^\lambda)-\pi(x))/(\log x)^{\lambda-1}$ is strictly above $1$ and the $\liminf$ strictly below $1$. The hypotheses are none: the theorem is unconditional. Its main input, Gallagher's prime number theorem for one large modulus (Theorem~\ref{thm:gallagher}), is unconditional too: it carries the possible exceptional zero as an explicit term, and the proof of Maier's theorem chooses its moduli so that this term is absent.

\emph{The second pass} asks what the skeleton is. Maier's proof builds a matrix whose rows are short intervals and whose columns are residue classes modulo a product $P$ of small primes. Counted by columns, the matrix holds the primes of a few arithmetic progressions modulo $P$, and Gallagher's theorem counts them. Only the columns prime to $P$ contain primes, and the proportion of such columns in an interval of length $(\log x)^\lambda$ is governed by Buchstab's function $\omega(u)$. Since $e^\gamma\omega(u)$ oscillates around $1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:buchstabsign}), choosing $u$ on one side or the other gives a row with too many or too few primes, which are the $\limsup$ and the $\liminf$. A reader who can say that paragraph can say what the proof is for.

\emph{The third pass} checks a lemma. The lemma worth checking is the deletion of one prime factor so that the modulus has no exceptional zero, Lemma~\ref{lem:goodmoduli}. It uses the repulsion of exceptional zeros, the theorem of Landau and Page from Volume~14, and it uses nothing else.

The same three passes apply to Selberg's central limit theorem, Theorem~\ref{thm:selberg}. The claim is a Gaussian with variance $\frac12\log\log T$, at a random height, with no Riemann hypothesis in the heading. The skeleton is: move slightly off the line, replace the logarithm by a short prime sum, and compare the two in mean square. The lemma worth checking is the mean-square comparison, Proposition~\ref{prop:mollmeansq}, which is where a zero-density estimate would have entered if the argument had needed one. It does not.

\section{Project: prime factors in a short interval}\label{sec:projectomega}

For $y\ge2$ let $\omega_y(n)$ be the number of primes $p\le y$ dividing $n$, and let $\mu_y=\sum_{p\le y}1/p$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:tkshort}
For real $x\ge0$, $h\ge1$ and $y\ge2$,
\[
\sum_{x<n\le x+h}\bigl(\omega_y(n)-\mu_y\bigr)^2\le h\,\mu_y+5\,\pi(y)^2.
\]
In particular, if $y\le\sqrt h$, the left side is at most $h(\mu_y+5)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $N_d$ be the number of multiples of $d$ in $]x,x+h]$, so $N_d=h/d+\theta_d$ with $|\theta_d|\le1$. Then $\sum_n\omega_y(n)=\sum_pN_p$ and $\sum_n\omega_y(n)^2=\sum_pN_p+\sum_{p\ne q}N_{pq}$, the sums over primes $p,q\le y$. Expanding,
\[
\sum_n(\omega_y-\mu_y)^2=\sum_pN_p+\sum_{p\ne q}N_{pq}-2\mu_y\sum_pN_p+\mu_y^2N_1.
\]
The main terms add up to $h\bigl(\mu_y+\sum_{p\ne q}\frac1{pq}-2\mu_y^2+\mu_y^2\bigr)\le h\mu_y$, because $\sum_{p\ne q}\frac1{pq}\le\mu_y^2$. The errors are at most $\pi(y)+\pi(y)^2+2\mu_y\pi(y)+\mu_y^2$, and since $\mu_y\le\frac12\pi(y)$ this is at most $5\pi(y)^2$. If $y\le\sqrt h$, then $\pi(y)^2\le y^2\le h$.
\end{proof}

With $h=x^\theta$ and $y=x^{\theta/2}$, an integer near $x$ has at most $\frac2\theta$ prime factors larger than $y$, and $\mu_y=\log\log x+O_\theta(1)$. The proposition gives the normal order of $\omega(n)$ in intervals of length $x^\theta$, for every fixed $\theta>0$. That is the proved step.

The open line is the short-interval Erd\H{o}s--Kac theorem: for which $h=h(x)$, in particular for $h=(\log x)^A$, does $(\omega(n)-\log\log x)/\sqrt{\log\log x}$ still tend to a Gaussian as $n$ runs through $]x,x+h]$? Proposition~\ref{prop:tkshort} does not reach those intervals, because it needs $y\le\sqrt h$, while the primes up to $y$ must carry the whole variance $\log\log x$. A first project: decide how far a sieve argument of Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-sieves} pushes $h$ below $x^\theta$, and where exactly it stops.

\section{Project: consecutive residues}\label{sec:projectconsecutive}

Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-bias} computes, under GRH and LI, the logarithmic density of the set of $x$ for which there are more primes up to $x$ in one residue class than in another. Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan observed a different bias, quoted in that chapter: among \emph{consecutive} primes, the pairs of residue classes are not equally likely, and the diagonal is light. Among the first $10^8$ primes, a prime ending in $1$ is followed by another ending in $1$ about $18\%$ of the time, not $25\%$.

What the series can prove is the input to their heuristic, not the bias. For a pair of classes $a,b$ modulo $q$, the probability that $n\equiv a$ and $n+h\equiv b$ are both prime is, by the Hardy--Littlewood conjectures, the coin probability times a singular series $\mathfrak S(h)$, which depends on $h$ through its prime factors (Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-short} computes its averages). Consecutive primes need, in addition, that no prime lies between $n$ and $n+h$; averaging $\mathfrak S$ over the gaps $h\equiv b-a\pmod q$, with the weight of an empty interval, produces a correction of relative size $\frac1{\log x}$ that favours $b\ne a$. A second project: compute that average for $q=10$ from the singular series alone, compare it with a table of consecutive primes up to $10^9$, and say which parts of the comparison are theorems (the averages of $\mathfrak S$), which are conjectures (the Hardy--Littlewood input) and which are computations (the table). The line between the last two is the open one.

\section{What the series proved, and what comes next}

This volume proved the two climaxes it promised. Maier's theorem shows that the primes fail Cram\'er's model in short intervals, unconditionally (Theorem~\ref{thm:maier}). Selberg's theorem shows that $\log|\zeta(\frac12+it)|$ is Gaussian at a random height, unconditionally (Theorem~\ref{thm:selberg}). Between them, the volume proved the comparison of every statistic in the ledger with its model wherever a proof is within reach: the Kubilius model and Selberg--Delange, Brun's sieve and its failure, Wintner's random multiplicative functions, the moments of random cokernels and of random alternating kernels, the form factor, the mean square of $\zeta$ and the lower bounds for its moments, Bohr--Jessen, the $U^2$ count, and the limit law of the prime race. What it could not prove, it quoted with a source or stated as a conjecture, and the heading says which.

There is no next volume. What comes next is the literature: the Sources of each chapter, read in three passes, and the open lines of the ledger, each of which is a research problem.
