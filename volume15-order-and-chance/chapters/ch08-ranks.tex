\chapter{Ranks of elliptic curves}\label{ch:oc-ranks}

\begin{center}\itshape
Choose an elliptic curve over $\Q$ at random.\\
How likely is it to have infinitely many rational points, and what can a random matrix say about it?
\end{center}

In 1922 Louis Mordell proved that the rational points of an elliptic curve form a finitely generated group, $E(\Q)\cong E(\Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}\times\Z^r$ (Volume~13, Theorem~15.7). The torsion part was classified by Barry Mazur in 1977. The rank $r$ is still a mystery. Nobody knows an algorithm that is proved to compute it, nobody knows whether it can be arbitrarily large, and the largest known value keeps moving: in 2006 Noam Elkies found a curve whose rank is at least $28$. Yet when the curves are counted, a pattern appears. In 1979 Dorian Goldfeld conjectured that, in a family of quadratic twists, half of the curves have rank $0$, half have rank $1$, and the curves of rank at least $2$, Elkies's monsters included, have density zero.

Ranks are hard to compute, but an upper bound comes from \emph{descent}, which Volume~13 carried out for $y^2=x^3-x$. Descent produces a finite $\mathbb F_p$-vector space, the $p$-Selmer group $\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$, that contains $E(\Q)/pE(\Q)$. In 2012 Bjorn Poonen and Eric Rains proposed a model for it: $\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ behaves like the intersection of two random maximal isotropic subspaces of a large quadratic space over $\mathbb F_p$. Three years later Manjul Bhargava, Daniel Kane, Hendrik Lenstra, Poonen and Rains showed that the same law is the law of the kernel of a large random \emph{alternating} matrix, a cousin of the symmetric matrices of Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-classgroups}.

This chapter proves the probability: the kernel of a random alternating matrix is a birth--death chain in the size of the matrix, its law is computed exactly, and its limit is the Poonen--Rains law, with average size $p+1$ and a parity that is a fair coin. It rewrites the descent of Volume~13 as a Selmer group. The arithmetic side is quoted: the theorems of Bhargava and Shankar on average Selmer sizes, and the conjectures of Goldfeld and of Poonen and Rains. The orbit methods behind Bhargava's theorems are not attempted.

\begin{reminder}[from Volumes~3, 11 and~13]
\emph{Alternating forms} (Volume~3). A matrix $A$ over a field is \emph{alternating} if $A^{\mathsf T}=-A$ and its diagonal is zero. Its rank is even, and its image is the orthogonal of its kernel for the dot product: $\operatorname{im}A=(\ker A)^\perp$.
\emph{Markov chains} (Volume~11 uses them for shuffling). A sequence $(K_n)$ of random variables is a Markov chain with transition probabilities $P(k,l)$ if the conditional law of $K_{n+1}$ given $K_0,\dots,K_n$ is $P(K_n,\cdot)$.
\emph{Mordell's theorem and descent} (Volume~13, Theorems~15.7 and~15.10). $E(\Q)$ is finitely generated, and $E(\Q)/2E(\Q)$ is finite, through an injective map into a finite group of square classes.
\end{reminder}

\section{Selmer groups}

Let $E$ be an elliptic curve over $\Q$ and $p$ a prime. Multiplication by $p$ on $E(\Q)$ has cokernel $E(\Q)/pE(\Q)$, an $\mathbb F_p$-vector space of dimension $r+\dim_{\mathbb F_p}E(\Q)[p]$, where $E(\Q)[p]$ is the $p$-torsion. For $p=2$, Volume~13 embedded $E(\Q)/2E(\Q)$ into a group of square classes, and showed that the image is constrained at every place: at the real place by signs, at the primes of bad reduction by valuations. The set of square classes that satisfy every local constraint is the $2$-Selmer group. For a general $p$ the same construction, with Galois cohomology in place of square classes, gives a finite $\mathbb F_p$-vector space $\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ with an injection
\[
E(\Q)/pE(\Q)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Sel}_p(E),
\]
whose cokernel is the $p$-torsion of the Tate--Shafarevich group $\text{Ш}(E)$. We use the general definition only through this injection and the theorems we quote; the definition itself is in the Sources. In particular
\begin{equation}\label{eq:selmerbound}
p^{\,r}\le\#\operatorname{Sel}_p(E).
\end{equation}
A Selmer group is computable: it is cut out by finitely many local conditions. That is why it is the object a model can be compared with.

\section{The congruent-number curve}

Volume~13 proves that $E_1:y^2=x^3-x$ has rank $0$ (Theorem~15.15). The map in that proof is the Kummer map, and the proof bounds its image using only local information. So it bounds the $2$-Selmer group of this one curve.

The $2$-torsion is $\{O,(0,0),(1,0),(-1,0)\}$. An affine point $(x,y)$ with $y\ne0$ is sent to the pair of square classes of $x$ and $x-1$ in $\Q^\times/(\Q^\times)^2$. The image lands in the classes generated by $-1$ and $2$. The $2$-Selmer group is the set of those classes which lie in the local image at every place. The proof in Volume~13 uses two local facts: the sign of $x(x-1)(x+1)=y^2$, which is the real place, and $2$-adic valuations, which is the place $2$. Those two facts already cut the sixteen candidate pairs down to the image of the torsion:
\[
\delta(O)=(1,1),\quad \delta(0,0)=(-1,-1),\quad \delta(1,0)=(1,2),\quad \delta(-1,0)=(-1,-2).
\]
The $2$-adic step is a short form of the one given there: if the first class contains the factor $2$, then $v_2(x)$ is odd. The denominator of $x$ is a square, so $v_2(x)>0$. Then $x-1$ and $x+1$ are $2$-adic units, and $v_2(y^2)=v_2(x)$ is odd, which is impossible. So the first class is $1$ or $-1$. The second class may contain the factor $2$, and by the real place its sign is the sign of the first class. That leaves exactly the four pairs above. Thus $\operatorname{Sel}_2(E_1)$ has order $4$. Since $E_1(\Q)/2E_1(\Q)$ injects into it and already has order $4$ from the torsion, the rank is $0$ and this descent sees no element of the Tate--Shafarevich group.

\begin{examplex}[$n=5$]
The curve $y^2=x^3-25x$ has the rational point $(-4,6)$. Volume~13 turns it into a right triangle of area $5$ (Proposition~15.13). By Volume~13, Proposition~15.14, the torsion of this curve is its $2$-torsion, so $(-4,6)$ has infinite order and the rank is positive. The descent sees it. With $x$, $x-5$, $x+5$ in place of $x$, $x-1$, $x+1$, and the primes $2$ and $5$ allowed in the square classes, the point $(-4,6)$ goes to the pair of classes of $-4$ and $-9$, which is $(-1,-1)$. The torsion goes to $(1,1)$, $(-1,-5)$, $(5,2)$ and $(-5,-10)$. The new pair is not among them, so the $2$-Selmer group has order at least $8$.
\end{examplex}

So one curve has Selmer group as small as its torsion allows, and its neighbour has one more dimension. The model of the next section says how often each happens.

\section{The kernel of a random alternating matrix}

Fix a prime $p$. For $n\ge0$ let $A_n$ be a uniform random alternating $n\times n$ matrix over $\mathbb F_p$: zero diagonal, $A^{\mathsf T}=-A$, and the $\binom n2$ entries above the diagonal independent and uniform. Let $K_n=\dim\ker A_n$. The rank of an alternating matrix is even, so $K_n\equiv n\pmod2$.

The matrices can be built one row and column at a time: $A_{n+1}$ has the same law as
\[
\begin{pmatrix}A_n&b\\-b^{\mathsf T}&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
with $b$ uniform in $\mathbb F_p^n$ and independent of $A_n$.

\begin{lemma}[a birth--death chain]\label{lem:altchain}
$(K_n)_{n\ge0}$ is a Markov chain on $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$, started at $K_0=0$, with
\[
P(K_{n+1}=k+1\mid K_n=k)=p^{-k},\qquad P(K_{n+1}=k-1\mid K_n=k)=1-p^{-k}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $A=A_n$, $K=\dim\ker A$, and $M$ for the bordered matrix. A vector $(v,t)\in\mathbb F_p^n\times\mathbb F_p$ is in $\ker M$ if and only if $Av+tb=0$ and $b^{\mathsf T}v=0$.

\emph{If $b\in\operatorname{im}A$}, say $b=Ac$. Every $v\in\ker A$ satisfies $b^{\mathsf T}v=c^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}v=-c^{\mathsf T}Av=0$, so $(v,0)\in\ker M$. Also $(c,-1)\in\ker M$: $Ac-b=0$ and $b^{\mathsf T}c=c^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}c=-c^{\mathsf T}Ac=0$, because $c^{\mathsf T}Ac=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}(c_ic_j-c_jc_i)=0$ for an alternating $A$. So $\dim\ker M\ge K+1$. As $\dim\ker M\equiv n+1\equiv K+1\pmod2$ and $\operatorname{rank}M\ge\operatorname{rank}A$, which gives $\dim\ker M\le K+1$, we get $\dim\ker M=K+1$.

\emph{If $b\notin\operatorname{im}A$}, then $Av+tb=0$ forces $t=0$ and $v\in\ker A$. Since $\operatorname{im}A=(\ker A)^\perp$ and $b\notin(\ker A)^\perp$, the linear form $v\mapsto b^{\mathsf T}v$ is non-zero on $\ker A$, and its kernel there has dimension $K-1$. So $\dim\ker M=K-1$.

Given $A$, the vector $b$ is uniform and independent of $A$, so it lies in $\operatorname{im}A$, a subspace of dimension $n-K$, with probability $p^{n-K}/p^n=p^{-K}$. The conditional law of $K_{n+1}$ given $A_0,\dots,A_n$ (built successively) depends only on $K_n$, which is the Markov property.
\end{proof}

From state $0$ the chain always moves up; from any $k\ge1$ it moves down with probability at least $\frac12$. It cannot wander far.

\begin{theorem}[the law of the kernel]\label{thm:altlaw}
For $n\ge0$ and $0\le k\le n$ with $k\equiv n\pmod2$,
\[
P(K_n=k)=p^{-\binom k2}\,\frac{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}\bigl(1-p^{-j}\bigr)}{\prod_{i=1}^{(n-k)/2}\bigl(1-p^{-2i}\bigr)}.
\]
Consequently, as $n\to\infty$ through integers $n\equiv k\pmod2$,
\[
P(K_n=k)\longrightarrow2\pi_p(k),\qquad\pi_p(k)=\frac{p^{-\binom k2}}{\prod_{j\ge0}(1+p^{-j})\,\prod_{j=1}^k(1-p^{-j})}.
\]
The numbers $\pi_p(k)$, $k\ge0$, form a probability distribution, which gives total mass $\frac12$ to the even integers and $\frac12$ to the odd ones.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The formula.} Call the right side $f_n(k)$, and put $f_n(k)=0$ when $k<0$, $k>n$ or $k\not\equiv n$. For $n=0$ it gives $f_0(0)=1$, which is the law of $K_0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain}, the law of $K_{n+1}$ is obtained from that of $K_n$ by
\[
P(K_{n+1}=k)=P(K_n=k-1)\,p^{-(k-1)}+P(K_n=k+1)\bigl(1-p^{-(k+1)}\bigr),
\]
so it suffices to check that $f$ satisfies the same recursion. Fix $k\equiv n+1$, put $m=\frac{n+1-k}2$, $D_n(k)=\prod_{j=k+1}^n(1-p^{-j})$ and $E_m=\prod_{i=1}^m(1-p^{-2i})$. Using $\binom{k-1}2+(k-1)=\binom k2$, $\binom{k+1}2=\binom k2+k$, $D_n(k-1)=(1-p^{-k})D_n(k)$ and $(1-p^{-k-1})D_n(k+1)=D_n(k)$,
\[
f_n(k-1)p^{-(k-1)}+f_n(k+1)\bigl(1-p^{-k-1}\bigr)=\frac{p^{-\binom k2}D_n(k)}{E_m}\Bigl((1-p^{-k})+p^{-k}\bigl(1-p^{-2m}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
The bracket is $1-p^{-k-2m}=1-p^{-(n+1)}$, and $D_n(k)(1-p^{-(n+1)})=D_{n+1}(k)$, so the right side is $f_{n+1}(k)$. (For $k=0$ the first term is absent, and the identity still holds because $D_n(-1)$ contains the factor $1-p^0=0$.)

\emph{The limit.} As $n\to\infty$ with $k$ fixed, $D_n(k)\to\prod_{j>k}(1-p^{-j})$ and $E_{(n-k)/2}\to\prod_{i\ge1}(1-p^{-2i})$. Since $1-p^{-2i}=(1-p^{-i})(1+p^{-i})$,
\[
\frac{\prod_{j>k}(1-p^{-j})}{\prod_{i\ge1}(1-p^{-2i})}=\frac1{\prod_{j=1}^k(1-p^{-j})\prod_{i\ge1}(1+p^{-i})},
\]
and the limit is $2\pi_p(k)$, the factor $j=0$ of $\prod_{j\ge0}(1+p^{-j})$ being $2$.

\emph{Total mass.} Euler's identity $\prod_{j\ge0}(1+zq^j)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{q^{\binom k2}z^k}{\prod_{j=1}^k(1-q^j)}$, valid for $|q|<1$ (proof: the product $F(z)$ satisfies $F(z)=(1+z)F(qz)$, so its coefficients satisfy $a_k=q^ka_k+q^{k-1}a_{k-1}$ with $a_0=1$, which gives $a_k=q^{k-1}a_{k-1}/(1-q^k)$ and the formula by induction; Exercise~\ref{ex:euler} asks for the details), at $q=\frac1p$ and $z=1$ gives $\sum_k\pi_p(k)=1$, and at $z=-1$ gives $\sum_k(-1)^k\pi_p(k)=\prod_{j\ge0}(1-p^{-j})/\prod_{j\ge0}(1+p^{-j})=0$, because the factor $j=0$ of the numerator vanishes. So the even and the odd $k$ each carry mass $\frac12$.
\end{proof}

So the kernel of a large random alternating matrix has dimension $k$ with probability $2\pi_p(k)$ when $n$ has the parity of $k$, and $0$ otherwise. Choose the parity of $n$ by a fair coin, and the limit law is $\pi_p$ itself, the \emph{Poonen--Rains law}. For $p=2$ it gives dimension $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ with probabilities $0.210$, $0.419$, $0.280$, $0.080$.

\begin{corollary}[moments of the Poonen--Rains law]\label{cor:prmoments}
If $K$ has law $\pi_p$, then $\mathbb E\,p^{mK}=\prod_{i=1}^m(1+p^i)$ for every $m\ge0$. In particular a random $\mathbb F_p$-space of law $\pi_p$ has average size $p+1$. The same holds for $K_n$ in the limit $n\to\infty$, through either parity.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $a_n(m)=\mathbb Ep^{mK_n}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain}, $\mathbb E[p^{mK_{n+1}}\mid K_n=k]=p^{m(k+1)}p^{-k}+p^{m(k-1)}(1-p^{-k})=p^{-m}p^{mk}+(p^m-p^{-m})p^{(m-1)k}$, so
\[
a_{n+1}(m)=p^{-m}a_n(m)+(p^m-p^{-m})\,a_n(m-1),\qquad a_n(0)=1.
\]
Let $a(m)=\prod_{i=1}^m(1+p^i)$. Then $a(m)=p^{-m}a(m)+(p^m-p^{-m})a(m-1)$, since $(1-p^{-m})(1+p^m)=p^m-p^{-m}$. Subtracting, $e_n(m)=a_n(m)-a(m)$ satisfies $e_{n+1}(m)=p^{-m}e_n(m)+(p^m-p^{-m})e_n(m-1)$ with $e_n(0)=0$, and by induction on $m$, $e_n(m)\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ (a sequence with $e_{n+1}=\theta e_n+\varepsilon_n$, $0\le\theta<1$, $\varepsilon_n\to0$, tends to $0$). For $K$ of law $\pi_p$: along even $n$ and along odd $n$, $K_n$ converges in law to $\pi_p$ conditioned on even, resp.\ odd, values, and $p^{mK_n}$ is uniformly integrable because $a_n(m+1)$ is bounded; so the two conditional moments both equal $a(m)$, and so does their average.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[when moments do not decide]\label{rem:parity}
The two limits, along even $n$ and along odd $n$, are different laws, carried by disjoint sets, and yet they have the same moments $\mathbb Ep^{mK}$ for every $m$. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:oc-classgroups}: the number of injections of $\mathbb F_p^k$ into the kernel has limiting average $p^{k(k+1)/2}=|\operatorname{Sym}^2\mathbb F_p^k|$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:altsubspaces}), larger than the bound $|\wedge^2G|=p^{k(k-1)/2}$ under which Wood's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:woodunique}) guarantees uniqueness. The bound in Wood's theorem is not an artefact of the proof: one step beyond it, the moments no longer determine the law, and the missing information is exactly a parity.
\end{remark}

Poonen and Rains reached the same law from a different matrix model. In a quadratic space of dimension $2n$ over $\mathbb F_p$, take two maximal isotropic subspaces $Z$ and $W$, with $W$ uniform; then $\dim(Z\cap W)$ converges in law to $\pi_p$ as $n\to\infty$. In their model the parity of $\dim(Z\cap W)$ is a fair coin from the start, because the maximal isotropics fall into two classes. We quote this; the alternating matrices give the same law with a proof that fits on a page.

\section{The model and the theorems}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{The Poonen--Rains heuristic (2012), a conjecture.} \emph{Let $p$ be a prime. As $E$ runs over the elliptic curves over $\Q$ ordered by height, $\dim_{\mathbb F_p}\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ becomes distributed according to $\pi_p$. In particular the average of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ is $p+1$, and the parity of $\dim\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ is even for half of the curves.}

\medskip
The parity half of the heuristic has an arithmetic shadow: the root number $w(E)=\pm1$ of the functional equation of $L(E,s)$, which is expected to be $(-1)^r$, is $+1$ for half of the curves. The model's prediction for the average size has been tested against theorems.

\begin{remark}
These are quoted; see the Sources. (a) \emph{Average Selmer sizes.} Manjul Bhargava and Arul Shankar proved that, for elliptic curves over $\Q$ ordered by height, the average of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_n(E)$ is $\sigma(n)$, the sum of the divisors of $n$, for $n=2,3,4,5$. For a prime $p$ this is $p+1$, as the model predicts. (b) \emph{Rank $0$ and rank $1$.} A positive proportion of curves have rank $0$ (Bhargava and Shankar, from the $3$-Selmer average) and a positive proportion have rank $1$ (Bhargava and Skinner, 2014). (c) \emph{Goldfeld's conjecture.} For the quadratic twists of the congruent number curve, the $2^\infty$-Selmer groups were shown by Alexander Smith (2017) to follow the distribution predicted by the models, which gives the Selmer version of Goldfeld's conjecture for that family.
\end{remark}

From an average Selmer size to a bound for the average rank is one line, and it is the same line for every $p$.

\begin{proposition}[from Selmer averages to ranks]\label{prop:avgrank}
Let $p$ be a prime and $\mathcal F_1\subseteq\mathcal F_2\subseteq\cdots$ finite sets of elliptic curves over $\Q$ such that the average of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)$ over $\mathcal F_X$ is at most $p+1+o(1)$ as $X\to\infty$. Then the average rank over $\mathcal F_X$ is at most $1+\frac1{p(p-1)}+o(1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The function $t\mapsto p^t$ is convex, so on the integers outside $]1,2[$ it lies above the line through $(1,p)$ and $(2,p^2)$: $p^r\ge(p^2-p)r-(p^2-2p)$ for every integer $r\ge0$ (at $r=0$ the right side is $2p-p^2\le0<1$). With~\eqref{eq:selmerbound}, $(p^2-p)r(E)\le\#\operatorname{Sel}_p(E)+p^2-2p$ for every $E$. Average over $\mathcal F_X$: the average rank is at most $\frac{p+1+p^2-2p+o(1)}{p^2-p}=1+\frac1{p(p-1)}+o(1)$.
\end{proof}

With Bhargava and Shankar's averages this gives $\frac32$ for $p=2$, $\frac76$ for $p=3$ and $\frac{21}{20}$ for $p=5$: the average rank of elliptic curves is bounded, a statement nobody could prove before 2010. Bhargava and Shankar went below $1$, to $0.885$, by adding the equidistribution of root numbers in their families. The model predicts more.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Goldfeld's conjecture (1979) and the minimalist conjecture, conjectures.} \emph{In the family of quadratic twists $E_d$ of a fixed curve, ordered by $|d|$, the rank is $0$ for a proportion $\frac12$ of the twists and $1$ for a proportion $\frac12$; the average rank is $\frac12$. The same holds for all elliptic curves ordered by height.}

\medskip
How does a model of Selmer groups say anything about ranks? Through a second model. Bhargava, Kane, Lenstra, Poonen and Rains also modelled $\text{Ш}(E)[p^\infty]$, and in their model the Selmer dimension beyond $0$ or $1$ is accounted for, with probability $1$, by the Tate--Shafarevich group rather than by the rank: $\text{Ш}$ carries an alternating pairing, so it comes in squares, which is exactly how the extra kernel of an alternating matrix comes. The model then predicts that the rank is $0$ or $1$ with the parity of the Selmer dimension, which is Goldfeld's conjecture. Elkies's curve of rank $28$ is, for the model, an event of probability zero, which is not the same as impossible.

\begin{beauty}
The rank of an elliptic curve is the most rigid of arithmetic invariants, and the alternating matrix is the most featureless of random objects. The Weil pairing makes the Selmer group the kernel of an alternating form, and a random alternating form already knows three facts about ranks that took arithmetic decades to establish: the average Selmer size $p+1$, the even split of parities, and the rarity of large Selmer groups, with tails like $p^{-k^2/2}$. The model does not know about any particular curve. That is precisely why it is right on average.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:alt3}
Compute the law of $K_3$ directly, for $p=2$, by listing the $8$ alternating $3\times3$ matrices over $\mathbb F_2$, and compare with Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:altmean}
Deduce from Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain} that $\mathbb Ep^{K_n}=p+1-p^{1-n}$ for every $n\ge1$. Check the case $n=2$ by hand.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:altsubspaces}
Let $W\subseteq\mathbb F_p^n$ be a subspace of dimension $k$. Show that $P(W\subseteq\ker A_n)=p^{-nk+\binom{k+1}2}$. Deduce that the expected number of injective linear maps $\mathbb F_p^k\to\ker A_n$ tends to $p^{k(k+1)/2}$ as $n\to\infty$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:prnumbers}
Compute $\pi_2(k)$ for $k=0,1,2,3$, and the probability under $\pi_2$ that the Selmer dimension is at least $4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:euler}
Prove Euler's identity $\prod_{j\ge0}(1+zq^j)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{q^{\binom k2}z^k}{\prod_{j=1}^k(1-q^j)}$ for $|q|<1$ and $z\in\C$. (Show that the left side $F(z)$ satisfies $F(z)=(1+z)F(qz)$, and compare coefficients.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:rankfive}
Suppose that over a family the average of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_5(E)$ is $6$, and that exactly half of the curves have even rank. Use $5^r\ge1+12r$ for even $r\ge0$ and $5^r\ge5+20(r-1)$ for odd $r\ge1$ (check these) to bound the average rank. Compare with Proposition~\ref{prop:avgrank}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v15-08-1}
Take \(p=5\). Using Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain} and \(K_0=0\), show that \(K_1=1\) and that \(P(K_2=0)=\tfrac45\), \(P(K_2=2)=\tfrac15\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v15-08-2}
Corollary~\ref{cor:prmoments} gives \(\mathbb E\,p^{mK}=\prod_{i=1}^m(1+p^i)\). Compute the products for \(m=1\) and \(m=2\) at \(p=3\). They are the average size of the model Selmer group and its second moment.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:v15-08-3}
The rank of an alternating matrix is even. Deduce that \(K_n\equiv n\pmod2\). This is the content of Figure~\ref{fig:v15c08-parity}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:v15-08-4}
From the chain, prove that \(P(K_2=0)=1-1/p\), \(P(K_2=2)=1/p\), \(P(K_3=3)=p^{-3}\) and \(P(K_3=1)=1-p^{-3}\). Check each identity against the formula in Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw}.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:alt3}
\idea For $3\times3$ alternating matrices the rank is $0$ or $2$, so only two kernel dimensions occur.
\writeup The zero matrix has kernel of dimension $3$; the other $7$ have rank $2$, since the rank is even and non-zero, so kernel dimension $1$. So $P(K_3=3)=\frac18$ and $P(K_3=1)=\frac78$. Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw} gives $P(K_3=3)=2^{-3}=\frac18$ and $P(K_3=1)=\frac{(1-\frac14)(1-\frac18)}{1-\frac14}=\frac78$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:altmean}
\idea The chain gives a one-step recursion for $\mathbb Ep^{K_n}$.
\writeup By Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain}, $\mathbb E[p^{K_{n+1}}\mid K_n=k]=p^{k+1}p^{-k}+p^{k-1}(1-p^{-k})=p+p^{k-1}-p^{-1}$. So $a_{n+1}=p-p^{-1}+\frac{a_n}p$ with $a_0=1$, and by induction $a_n=p+1-p^{1-n}$: indeed $p-p^{-1}+\frac{p+1-p^{1-n}}p=p+1-p^{-n}$. For $n=2$: $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a&0\end{pmatrix}$ is zero for one value of $a$, with kernel of size $p^2$, and invertible for the other $p-1$, so $\mathbb Ep^{K_2}=\frac{p^2+p-1}p=p+1-p^{-1}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:altsubspaces}
\idea Change basis, which preserves the uniform law, and count the entries forced to vanish.
\writeup For $g\in GL_n(\mathbb F_p)$, $A\mapsto g^{\mathsf T}Ag$ is a bijection of the alternating matrices, and $W\subseteq\ker A$ if and only if $g^{-1}W\subseteq\ker(g^{\mathsf T}Ag)$. So we may take $W=\langle e_1,\dots,e_k\rangle$. Then $W\subseteq\ker A$ means that the first $k$ columns vanish, hence, by antisymmetry, the first $k$ rows. The free entries $a_{ij}$, $i<j$, that must vanish are those with $i\le k$: there are $\binom n2-\binom{n-k}2=nk-\binom{k+1}2$ of them, each zero with probability $\frac1p$. The number of injections $\mathbb F_p^k\to\ker A$ is $|GL_k(\mathbb F_p)|$ times the number of $k$-dimensional subspaces of $\ker A$, and $\mathbb F_p^n$ has $\frac{\prod_{i<k}(p^n-p^i)}{|GL_k(\mathbb F_p)|}$ subspaces of dimension $k$. So the expectation is $\prod_{i<k}(p^n-p^i)\cdot p^{-nk+\binom{k+1}2}=\prod_{i<k}(1-p^{i-n})\cdot p^{\binom{k+1}2}\to p^{k(k+1)/2}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:prnumbers}
\idea Each term is the previous one times $\frac{p^{-(k-1)}}{1-p^{-k}}$.
\writeup With $P=\prod_{j\ge0}(1+2^{-j})=4.7685$, $\pi_2(0)=\frac1P=0.2097$, $\pi_2(1)=\frac{\pi_2(0)}{1/2}=0.4194$, $\pi_2(2)=\pi_2(1)\cdot\frac{1/2}{3/4}=0.2796$, $\pi_2(3)=\pi_2(2)\cdot\frac{1/4}{7/8}=0.0799$. The rest is $1-0.9886=0.0114$: about one curve in ninety should have a $2$-Selmer group of dimension at least $4$. The even and the odd values carry $0.2097+0.2796+\dots=\frac12$ and $0.4194+0.0799+\dots=\frac12$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:euler}
\idea A functional equation in $z$ determines the coefficients one by one.
\writeup The product converges absolutely and locally uniformly in $z$, since $\sum_j|q|^j<\infty$, so $F$ is entire, $F(z)=\sum_kc_kz^k$ with $c_0=F(0)=1$. Splitting off the factor $j=0$, $F(z)=(1+z)F(qz)$. Comparing the coefficients of $z^k$: $c_k=q^kc_k+q^{k-1}c_{k-1}$, so $c_k=\frac{q^{k-1}}{1-q^k}c_{k-1}$ and $c_k=\frac{q^{0+1+\dots+(k-1)}}{\prod_{j\le k}(1-q^j)}=\frac{q^{\binom k2}}{\prod_{j\le k}(1-q^j)}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rankfive}
\idea Parity removes the case $r=0$ from the odd half, which is where the line of Proposition~\ref{prop:avgrank} was weakest.
\writeup For even $r\ge0$: $5^r\ge1+24\cdot\frac r2$ (true for $r=0$, $r=2$ gives $25=25$, and $5^r$ grows faster), that is $r\le\frac{5^r-1}{12}$. For odd $r$: $5^r\ge5+20(r-1)$ (equality at $r=1$, and at $r=3$: $125\ge45$), that is $r\le1+\frac{5^r-5}{20}$. Let $S_e$ and $S_o$ be the averages of $\#\operatorname{Sel}_5\ge5^r$ over the even and odd halves, so $\frac{S_e+S_o}2=6$, with $S_e\ge1$ and $S_o\ge5$. The average rank is at most $\frac12\bigl(\frac{S_e-1}{12}+1+\frac{S_o-5}{20}\bigr)$. This is largest, under $S_e+S_o=12$, when $S_e$ is as large as possible, $S_e=7$, $S_o=5$, which gives $\frac12(\frac12+1)=0.75$; at the other extreme, $S_e=1$, $S_o=11$, it gives $\frac12(0+1+0.3)=0.65$. So the average rank is at most $0.75$, against $1.05$ without the parity information. Bhargava and Shankar's $0.885$ uses a weaker parity statement, valid for their families.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-08-1}
\idea The chain has only one legal step out of \(0\).
\writeup Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain} gives \(P(K_1=1\mid K_0=0)=p^{0}=1\), so \(K_1=1\). From state \(1\), the probability of stepping up is \(p^{-1}=1/5\), and the probability of stepping down is \(4/5\). Thus \(P(K_2=2)=1/5\) and \(P(K_2=0)=4/5\). Figure~\ref{fig:v15c08-chain} is this transition.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-08-2}
\idea Read the product in the corollary at two values of \(m\).
\writeup For \(m=1\), \(\mathbb Ep^{K}=1+p\). At \(p=3\) this is \(4\): the model Selmer group has average cardinality \(4\). For \(m=2\), \(\mathbb Ep^{2K}=(1+p)(1+p^2)=(1+3)(1+9)=40\).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-08-3}
\idea Subtract an even integer from \(n\).
\writeup The rank of an alternating matrix is even, as recalled at the beginning of the chapter. Therefore \(K_n=n-\operatorname{rank}A_n\) differs from \(n\) by an even integer, so \(K_n\equiv n\pmod2\). Figure~\ref{fig:v15c08-parity} is that congruence.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:v15-08-4}
\idea Compute the first two steps of the chain, then cancel factors in the closed formula.
\writeup As in Exercise~\ref{ex:v15-08-1}, \(K_1=1\), the step from \(1\) to \(2\) has probability \(p^{-1}\), and the step from \(1\) to \(0\) has probability \(1-p^{-1}\). So \(P(K_2=2)=1/p\) and \(P(K_2=0)=1-1/p\).

The only path to \(K_3=3\) is \(0\to1\to2\to3\), with probabilities \(1\), \(p^{-1}\) and \(p^{-2}\), hence \(P(K_3=3)=p^{-3}\). The paths to \(K_3=1\) are \(0\to1\to0\to1\) and \(0\to1\to2\to1\):
\[
P(K_3=1)=\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)\cdot1+\frac1p\bigl(1-p^{-2}\bigr)=1-p^{-3}.
\]
There is no other parity available.

Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw} at \(n=2\), \(k=0\) gives
\[
\frac{(1-p^{-1})(1-p^{-2})}{1-p^{-2}}=1-\frac1p,
\]
and at \(k=2\) one has \(\binom{2}{2}=1\), both products are empty and equal to \(1\), and the prefactor is \(p^{-1}\). At \(n=3\), \(k=3\) one has \(\binom{3}{2}=3\), both products are empty, and the value is \(p^{-3}\). At \(k=1\),
\[
\frac{(1-p^{-2})(1-p^{-3})}{1-p^{-2}}=1-p^{-3}.
\]
The chain and the formula agree.
\end{solution}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The epigraph asked how likely a random elliptic curve is to have infinitely many points, and what a random matrix can say. The matrix side is proved: the kernel of a random alternating matrix is a birth--death chain (Lemma~\ref{lem:altchain}), its law is explicit (Theorem~\ref{thm:altlaw}), and in the limit it is the Poonen--Rains law $\pi_p$, with average size $p+1$, moments $\prod_{i\le m}(1+p^i)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:prmoments}) and a fair parity. The arithmetic side is partly proved and partly quoted: the injection of $E(\Q)/pE(\Q)$ into the Selmer group, made explicit for the congruent number curves; Bhargava and Shankar's average $p+1$ for $p\le5$, quoted, from which the bounded average rank follows in one line (Proposition~\ref{prop:avgrank}). That a Selmer group \emph{is} distributed like the kernel of a random alternating matrix is the Poonen--Rains heuristic, and that half of all curves have rank $0$ and half rank $1$ is Goldfeld's conjecture. Both remain conjectures.

Part~III turns from algebra to analysis. The zeros of the Riemann zeta function will be compared with the eigenvalues of random unitary matrices, and here the meeting has a theorem in it: Montgomery's pair correlation.
