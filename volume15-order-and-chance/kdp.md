# Description

Can pure order and pure chance be told apart? The primes are defined without any probability, yet they look random. The Möbius function looks like a coin toss, the class groups of quadratic fields look like random groups, and the zeros of the zeta function look like the eigenvalues of a random matrix. This last volume of the series asks, one statistic at a time, when that resemblance is a theorem and when it is only a model.

The book opens with Cramér's model of the primes and proves where it breaks: Maier's theorem shows that the primes in certain short intervals are irregular, unconditionally. The prime factors of a typical integer are then made exact: the Turán–Kubilius inequality, the Erdős–Kac theorem, the Kubilius model and the Selberg–Delange method. Random multiplicative functions give Wintner's theorem and the setting of Chowla's conjecture. Primes in short intervals meet Gallagher's Poisson law, under the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture named in its heading. Sieves are read as probability, up to the point where they fail.

Random groups follow: the Cohen–Lenstra measure for class groups and the law of the kernel of a random alternating matrix for Selmer groups. The spectral part compares the zeros of zeta with random matrices, proves the mean square of zeta and lower bounds for all its moments, and proves the Bohr–Jessen theorem and Selberg's central limit theorem. The last part asks where structure wins: Gowers' U² norm and three-term progressions, Chebyshev's bias in the prime race, and the bias of consecutive primes.

Every result is proved on the page, quoted with its source, or labelled as a conjecture or conditional, and the label says which. The book ends with a ledger of what the whole series proved, a method for reading a research paper, and two projects that stop on open questions.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
