# Description

In the ring of numbers a + b√−5, the number 6 factors in two different ways: 2 · 3, and (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5). Unique factorisation, the foundation of ordinary arithmetic, is lost. In the 1840s Kummer, chasing Fermat's Last Theorem, found the same failure in the cyclotomic integers and repaired it with "ideal numbers". Dedekind turned them into ideals, and a whole world opened. This book is a journey through that world, from number fields to the p-adic numbers to curves, ending with the modular forms that tie them together.

The first part studies number fields. Their integers form a lattice, primes split into prime ideals according to a single symmetry, the Frobenius, and Minkowski's geometry of numbers proves that class groups are finite and that units form a lattice. Kummer's cyclotomic fields give the first serious attack on Fermat's Last Theorem.

The second part looks at one prime at a time, then at all of them together. The p-adic numbers and their extensions, the local-global principle of Hasse and Minkowski, which decides every quadratic equation by congruences and settles which integers are sums of three squares, zeta functions and the class number formula, and Chebotarev's theorem, which says how often each symmetry occurs as a Frobenius. Adèles put the real and p-adic worlds on an equal footing, and Tate's thesis derives the functional equation of the zeta function from Fourier analysis. Class field theory describes all abelian extensions of the rationals: the Kronecker-Weber theorem is proved in full and used to decide which primes have the form x² + ny².

The third part is arithmetic geometry. Bézout's theorem and Riemann-Roch for plane curves, the group of points on an elliptic curve and Mordell's theorem that it is finitely generated, the congruent number problem, the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields by the elementary method of Stepanov and Bombieri, and Hasse's bound. The book ends with modular forms: the discriminant function and Ramanujan's tau, Hecke operators, Hecke's functional equation, and a power series whose coefficients count the points of an elliptic curve modulo every prime, a first glimpse of the modularity theorem behind the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

The beauty of the subject lies in coincidences that turn out not to be coincidences. Whether a prime splits in a cubic field is decided by writing it as x² + 27y². The points of y² = x³ − x modulo p are predicted by Gaussian integers. Each is a shadow of one large structure, and the book tries to make that structure visible.

Every result is proved in full, except two clearly marked theorems of class field theory. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas, checked against the original sources, and has pause-and-think questions, figures, and graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
