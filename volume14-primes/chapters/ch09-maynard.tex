\chapter{Bounded Gaps between Primes}\label{ch:maynard}
\begin{center}\itshape
In May 2013 the record for small gaps was seventy million.\\
By November it was six hundred.\\
Can a book prove a bound of its own, with every step on the page?
\end{center}

On 19 November 2013 a paper called \emph{Small gaps between primes} appeared on the arXiv. Its author, James Maynard, was a postdoctoral fellow of the Centre de recherches mathématiques at the Université de Montréal. Seven months earlier Zhang had astonished the world by proving that $p_{n+1}-p_n<7\cdot10^7$ infinitely often; a large online collaboration, Polymath8a, had since pushed the bound down to $4680$ by sharpening every part of his argument. Maynard's paper gave $600$, and it did not need Zhang's hard equidistribution theorem at all: the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem of Chapter~\ref{ch:largesieve}, the tool that GPY had found just insufficient, was now enough. The same paper proved what no one had dared to expect, that for every $m$ there are infinitely many intervals of bounded length containing $m$ primes.

The new ingredient was a choice. Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy} set up the sieve with a function $F$ of $k$ variables and GPY, following decades of sieve tradition, took $F$ to depend on $t_1+\cdots+t_k$ alone. Maynard let $F$ depend on all the $t_i$ separately. Terence Tao had independently arrived at the same idea; rather than race, he and the Polymath project used it at once to improve the numbers further, down to $246$. Maynard received a Fields Medal in 2022, for this and for much else.

This chapter proves Maynard's theorems. Everything rests on Theorem~\ref{thm:maynardsums} and Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion}: we only have to show that the variational quantity $M_k$ is large. That is a pleasant problem in calculus, and its solution is a piece of probability: the law of large numbers for a sum of independent random variables. Because every constant in that argument can be written down, the chapter ends with a number of its own: there are infinitely many pairs of consecutive primes less than $4580$ apart. The famous $600$ and $246$ need computer certificates that the book does not print, and they are quoted as records.

\begin{reminder}[from Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy}]
For $\mathbf t\in\mathcal R_k=\{\mathbf t\in[0,1]^k:\sum t_i\le1\}$,
\[
I_k(F)=\int_{\mathcal R_k}F^2\dd\mathbf t,\qquad J_k^{(m)}(F)=\int\Bigl(\int_0^1F\dd t_m\Bigr)^2\dd t_1\cdots\dd t_{m-1}\dd t_{m+1}\cdots\dd t_k,
\]
and $M_k=\sup_F\frac{\sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)}{I_k(F)}$ over the $F$ of Definition~\ref{def:yweights}. \emph{Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion}}: if the primes have level of distribution $\theta$ and $\frac\theta2M_k>\rho$, then every admissible $k$-tuple $\mathcal H$ has infinitely many translates $n+\mathcal H$ containing at least $\lfloor\rho\rfloor+1$ primes. \emph{Bombieri--Vinogradov} (Corollary~\ref{cor:bvpi}) gives level $\theta$ for every $\theta<\frac12$: the range $q\le x^\theta$ lies inside $q\le\sqrt x(\log x)^{-A-6}$, and replacing $\operatorname{li}x$ by $\pi(x)$ costs $|\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}x|\sum_{q\le x}\frac1{\varphi(q)}\ll x e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}\log x$.
\end{reminder}

\section{The variational problem}

The functions allowed in Definition~\ref{def:yweights} are smooth on the simplex. The function we shall use is not even continuous, so we first check that this makes no difference to $M_k$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ltwo}
For every non-zero $F\in L^2(\mathcal R_k)$, extended by $0$ to $[0,1]^k$, $\frac{\sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)}{I_k(F)}\le M_k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Polynomials are dense in $L^2(\mathcal R_k)$: continuous functions are dense, and by the Weierstrass theorem polynomials approximate a continuous function uniformly on the compact set $\mathcal R_k$. A polynomial, restricted to $\mathcal R_k$ and extended by $0$, is allowed in Definition~\ref{def:yweights}. Take polynomials $G_j\to F$ in $L^2(\mathcal R_k)$. Then $I_k(G_j)=\|G_j\|^2\to\|F\|^2=I_k(F)\ne0$. For $J$, let $T_mG(\mathbf t')=\int_0^1G\dd t_m$, a function of the other $k-1$ variables. By Cauchy--Schwarz on an interval of length at most $1$, $(T_mG)^2\le\int_0^1G^2\dd t_m$, so $\|T_mG\|\le\|G\|$ and $T_m$ is a bounded linear map. Hence $T_mG_j\to T_mF$ and $J_k^{(m)}(G_j)=\|T_mG_j\|^2\to J_k^{(m)}(F)$. The ratio for $G_j$ is at most $M_k$, and it converges to the ratio for $F$.
\end{proof}

For computations we need the volume integrals of the simplex. They are Dirichlet's generalization of Euler's Beta integral (Volume~5, Theorem~5.11).

\begin{lemma}[Dirichlet]\label{lem:dirichletint}
For integers $a,c_1,\dots,c_k\ge0$,
\[
\int_{\mathcal R_k}\Bigl(1-\sum_{i=1}^kt_i\Bigr)^a\prod_{i=1}^kt_i^{c_i}\dd\mathbf t=\frac{a!\,c_1!\cdots c_k!}{(k+a+c_1+\cdots+c_k)!}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $u>0$ the substitution $t=uv$ and Euler's Beta integral give
\[
\int_0^u(u-t)^at^c\dd t=u^{a+c+1}\int_0^1(1-v)^av^c\dd v=\frac{a!\,c!}{(a+c+1)!}u^{a+c+1}.
\]
For $k=1$ this is the claim with $u=1$. For $k\ge2$, integrate first over $t_k$, from $0$ to $u=1-\sum_{i<k}t_i$: the integrand becomes $\frac{a!c_k!}{(a+c_k+1)!}(1-\sum_{i<k}t_i)^{a+c_k+1}\prod_{i<k}t_i^{c_i}$, and induction on $k$, with $a+c_k+1$ in place of $a$, gives $\frac{a!c_k!}{(a+c_k+1)!}\cdot\frac{(a+c_k+1)!\,c_1!\cdots c_{k-1}!}{(k-1+a+c_k+1+c_1+\cdots+c_{k-1})!}$, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Many primes in bounded intervals}

Here is the heart of the chapter. The GPY functions of Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy} gave ratios that never reach much beyond $4$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:hardyineq}); the next proposition shows that $M_k$ is unbounded.

\begin{proposition}[Maynard]\label{prop:mklower}
For all sufficiently large $k$, $M_k>\log k-2\log\log k-2$.
\end{proposition}

The idea is to take $F$ a product, $F(\mathbf t)=\prod_ig(kt_i)$ on $\mathcal R_k$. If the simplex were the whole cube, everything would factorize and the ratio would be $k$ times a ratio of one-dimensional integrals. The simplex cuts off the corner where $\sum t_i>1$; and if $g$ is chosen so that $\sum_i kt_i$, with the $kt_i$ distributed according to $g^2$, is rarely larger than $k$, the cut costs almost nothing. That is a law of large numbers. We first prove an inequality valid for every $k$, with all constants written, because the same inequality will give an explicit bounded gap.

\begin{lemma}[the product function]\label{lem:productF}
Let $k\ge2$, $A>0$ and $0<T<k$. Put $V=1+AT$ and
\[
\gamma=\frac TV,\qquad L=\frac{\log V}A,\qquad\mu=\frac{\log V-1+\frac1V}{\gamma A^2},\qquad m_2=\frac{V-2\log V-\frac1V}{\gamma A^3},\qquad\sigma^2=m_2-\mu^2.
\]
If $\delta=k-T-(k-1)\mu>0$, then
\[
M_k\ge\frac{L^2}\gamma\Bigl(1-\frac{(k-1)\sigma^2}{\delta^2}\Bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $g(t)=\frac1{1+At}$ for $0\le t\le T$ and $g(t)=0$ for $t>T$, and let $F(\mathbf t)=\prod_{i=1}^kg(kt_i)$ on $\mathcal R_k$, $F=0$ elsewhere. It is bounded, so Lemma~\ref{lem:ltwo} applies. With $v=1+At$,
\[
\int_0^Tg^2\dd t=\frac1A\Bigl(1-\frac1V\Bigr)=\gamma,\qquad\int_0^Tg\dd t=L,
\]
and, for a random variable $X$ on $[0,T]$ with density $\frac{g^2}\gamma$,
\[
\mathbb EX=\frac1{\gamma A^2}\int_1^V\frac{v-1}{v^2}\dd v=\mu,\qquad\mathbb EX^2=\frac1{\gamma A^3}\int_1^V\frac{(v-1)^2}{v^2}\dd v=m_2,
\]
so $\operatorname{Var}X=\sigma^2$.

\emph{The denominator.} Dropping the condition $\sum t_i\le1$ and substituting $u_i=kt_i$, $I_k(F)\le\prod_i\int_0^\infty g(kt_i)^2\dd t_i=(\frac\gamma k)^k$.

\emph{The numerator.} By symmetry all $J_k^{(m)}(F)$ are equal; take $m=k$, and write $\mathbf t'=(t_1,\dots,t_{k-1})$, $s=\sum_{i<k}t_i$. If $s\le1-\frac Tk$, the condition $t_k\le1-s$ is implied by $g(kt_k)\ne0$, so
\[
\int_0^1F\dd t_k=\prod_{i<k}g(kt_i)\int_0^{T/k}\frac{\dd t}{1+Akt}=\frac Lk\prod_{i<k}g(kt_i).
\]
Keeping only these $\mathbf t'$ and substituting $u_i=kt_i$,
\[
J_k^{(k)}(F)\ge\frac{L^2}{k^2}\int_{s\le1-T/k}\prod_{i<k}g(kt_i)^2\dd\mathbf t'=\frac{L^2}{k^2}\Bigl(\frac\gamma k\Bigr)^{k-1}\,\mathbb P\bigl(X_1+\cdots+X_{k-1}\le k-T\bigr),
\]
where $X_1,\dots,X_{k-1}$ are independent copies of $X$. So
\[
\frac{\sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)}{I_k(F)}\ge k\cdot\frac{L^2}{k^2}\Bigl(\frac\gamma k\Bigr)^{k-1}\Bigl(\frac k\gamma\Bigr)^k\,\mathbb P=\frac{L^2}\gamma\,\mathbb P.
\]

\emph{The probability.} The sum $X_1+\cdots+X_{k-1}$ has mean $(k-1)\mu=k-T-\delta$ and variance $(k-1)\sigma^2$. If it exceeds $k-T$, it is more than $\delta$ away from its mean, so Chebyshev's inequality (Volume~1, Chapter~10) gives $\mathbb P\ge1-\frac{(k-1)\sigma^2}{\delta^2}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ltwo}, $M_k$ is at least the ratio.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:mklower}]
Let $A=\log k-2\log\log k$ and $T=\frac{e^A-1}A$, so that $V=e^A=\frac k{(\log k)^2}$ and $L=1$; we take $k$ so large that $A\ge2$ and $T<k$. Then $\gamma=\frac TV=\frac{1-e^{-A}}A$, and
\[
\mu=\frac{A-1+e^{-A}}{A(1-e^{-A})},\qquad1-\mu=\frac{1-e^{-A}-Ae^{-A}}{A(1-e^{-A})}\ge\frac1A\bigl(1-2Ae^{-A}\bigr),
\]
because $1-e^{-A}\ge\frac12$. Since $\frac{t^2}{(1+At)^2}\le\frac1{A^2}$, $\sigma^2\le m_2\le\frac T{\gamma A^2}=\frac{e^A}{A^2}$. Next, $\delta\ge k(1-\mu)-T$. Using $Ae^{-A}=\frac{A(\log k)^2}k\le\frac{(\log k)^3}k$ and $T\le\frac{e^A}A=\frac k{A(\log k)^2}$,
\[
\delta\ge\frac kA\Bigl(1-\frac{2(\log k)^3}k-\frac1{(\log k)^2}\Bigr)\ge\frac kA\Bigl(1-\frac2{(\log k)^2}\Bigr)
\]
for $k$ large. Hence
\[
\frac{(k-1)\sigma^2}{\delta^2}\le\frac{ke^A}{A^2}\cdot\frac{A^2}{k^2(1-2(\log k)^{-2})^2}=\frac1{(\log k)^2(1-2(\log k)^{-2})^2}\le\frac2{(\log k)^2}
\]
for $k$ large. Lemma~\ref{lem:productF} gives $M_k\ge\frac1\gamma(1-\frac2{(\log k)^2})\ge A(1-\frac2{(\log k)^2})\ge A-\frac2{\log k}$, since $\frac1\gamma\ge A$ and $A\le\log k$, and this is larger than $\log k-2\log\log k-2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{think}
At the end of Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy} we asked how to reward $n+h_1$ and $n+h_2$ being prime separately. Look at the product $F=\prod g(kt_i)$ with $g$ decreasing: the weight $y_{\mathbf r}$ is large when every $r_i$ is small, which means each $n+h_i$ separately has few small prime factors, and the sieve treats the $k$ coordinates as $k$ independent, small sieve problems glued together. Why does the density $g(t)=\frac1{1+At}$ appear? Compare with the function that maximizes $\frac{(\int_0^Tg)^2}{\int_0^Tg^2}$ alone: it is constant. What does the factor $t$ in the mean $\mu$ penalize?
\end{think}

\begin{theorem}[Maynard, 2013]\label{thm:maynard}
Let $m\ge1$, and let $k$ satisfy $M_k>4m$, which holds for all large $k$. Then for every admissible $k$-tuple $\mathcal H$ there are infinitely many $n$ such that at least $m+1$ of the numbers $n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k$ are prime.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $M_k>4m$, there is $\theta<\frac12$ with $\frac\theta2M_k>m$. The primes have level of distribution $\theta$ by Bombieri--Vinogradov, and Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion} with $\rho=m$ gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mprimes}
There is an absolute constant $C$ such that for every $m\ge1$, $p_{n+m}-p_n\le Cm^3e^{4m}$ for infinitely many $n$. In particular $\liminf(p_{n+1}-p_n)<\infty$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $k=\lceil m^2e^{4m+8}\rceil$. Then $\log k\ge4m+8+2\log m$, and $\log k\le4m+9+2\log m\le15m$, so $\log\log k\le\log m+\log15$ and
\[
\log k-2\log\log k-2\ge4m+8+2\log m-2\log m-2\log15-2>4m+0.5.
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:mklower} there is $m_0$ such that $M_k>4m$ for this $k$ whenever $m\ge m_0$; for $m<m_0$ take instead a fixed $k_0$ with $M_{k_0}>4m_0$. Let $\mathcal H$ be the set of the $k$ smallest primes larger than $k$, admissible by Exercise~\ref{ex:admiss}. Since $\pi(k)\le k$, its diameter is less than $p_{2k}\ll k\log k\ll m^3e^{4m}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:maynard} infinitely many intervals $[n+h_1,n+h_k]$ contain $m+1$ primes, so $p_{j+m}-p_j\le h_k-h_1$ for infinitely many $j$.
\end{proof}

GPY's theorem, $\liminf\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$, which needed all of Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy}, is now a trivial consequence. The bound $Cm^3e^{4m}$ is far from the truth, which should be about $m\log m$ (the narrowest admissible $(m+1)$-tuple); the exponential comes from $M_k$ growing only like $\log k$, and Exercise~\ref{ex:upper} shows that this is the true size of $M_k$: the method cannot do better than an exponential in $m$.

\section{An explicit bounded gap}

How large must $k$ be before $M_k>4$? Proposition~\ref{prop:mklower} only says ``sufficiently large'', and its bound $\log k-2\log\log k-2$ passes $4$ only near $k=4\cdot10^4$. But Lemma~\ref{lem:productF} holds for every $k$, and its parameters $A$ and $T$ are ours to choose. Asking a computer for good values, and then checking the inequality by hand, gives a modest $k$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mk582}
$M_{582}>4$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:productF} with $k=582$, $A=7$ and $T=39$. Then $V=274$, $\log V=5.6131281\ldots$, $\gamma=\frac{39}{274}=0.1423357\ldots$ and $L=\frac{\log274}7=0.8018754\ldots$, so
\[
\frac{L^2}\gamma=\frac{274\,(\log274)^2}{49\cdot39}=4.5175168\ldots
\]
Next,
\[
\mu=\frac{274\,(\log274-1+\frac1{274})}{39\cdot49}=0.6619555\ldots,\qquad m_2=\frac{274\,(274-2\log274-\frac1{274})}{39\cdot343}=5.3822984\ldots,
\]
so $\sigma^2=m_2-\mu^2=4.9441132\ldots$, and $\delta=582-39-581\mu=158.40381\ldots>0$. Hence
\[
\frac{(k-1)\sigma^2}{\delta^2}=\frac{581\cdot4.9441132\ldots}{(158.40381\ldots)^2}=0.1144809\ldots,
\]
and $M_{582}\ge4.5175168\ldots\times0.8855190\ldots=4.000347\ldots>4$. Every number here is a closed expression in $\log274$, evaluated to $40$ digits for this book; the margin $0.0003$ is far larger than any rounding in the last printed digit.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[an explicit bounded gap]\label{thm:explicitgap}
Let $\mathcal H_{582}$ be the set of the $582$ smallest primes larger than $582$, from $587$ to $5167$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $M_k>4$, then every admissible $k$-tuple $\mathcal H$ has infinitely many translates $n+\mathcal H$ containing at least two primes.
\item $\mathcal H_{582}$ is admissible, and infinitely many of its translates contain two primes.
\item $p_{n+1}-p_n\le4580$ for infinitely many $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) is Theorem~\ref{thm:maynard} with $m=1$. (b) No element of $\mathcal H_{582}$ is divisible by a prime $p\le582$, so the class $0$ modulo such a $p$ is missed; for $p>582$ the $582$ elements cannot cover all $p$ classes. So $\mathcal H_{582}$ is admissible (Exercise~\ref{ex:admiss}), and (a) with Proposition~\ref{prop:mk582} gives the translates. (c) If $n+h<n+h'$ are two primes of such a translate, the consecutive primes between them differ by at most $h'-h\le5167-587=4580$.
\end{proof}

A narrower admissible $582$-tuple would give a smaller number, and there is no reason to stop at our $A$ and $T$; the point is that every step from the sieve to the number $4580$ is on the page. For comparison, Zhang's paper of May 2013 gave $7\cdot10^7$, and the Polymath project, working on Zhang's method, had reached $4680$ when Maynard's paper appeared.

\section{Five primes, and the records}

For moderate $k$ the product function is not the best choice. Maynard took $F$ to be a symmetric polynomial, in fact a polynomial in $P_1=\sum t_i$ and $P_2=\sum t_i^2$, and computed the quadratic forms exactly.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:symint}
For integers $a,b\ge0$ write $\langle a,b\rangle_k=\int_{\mathcal R_k}(1-P_1)^aP_2^b\dd\mathbf t$. Then
\[
\langle a,b\rangle_k=\frac{a!\,b!}{(k+a+2b)!}\sum_{\substack{b_1+\cdots+b_k=b\\b_i\ge0}}\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{(2b_i)!}{b_i!},
\]
and, with $u=1-\sum_{i<m}t_i-\sum_{i>m}t_i$ and $P_1',P_2'$ the sums over $i\ne m$,
\[
\int_0^u(u-t_m)^a(P_2'+t_m^2)^b\dd t_m=\sum_{j=0}^b\binom bj\frac{a!\,(2j)!}{(a+2j+1)!}(1-P_1')^{a+2j+1}P_2'^{\,b-j}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Expand $P_2^b=\sum\frac{b!}{b_1!\cdots b_k!}\prod t_i^{2b_i}$ by the multinomial theorem and apply Lemma~\ref{lem:dirichletint} to each term. For the second formula expand $(P_2'+t_m^2)^b$ by the binomial theorem and use $\int_0^u(u-t)^at^{2j}\dd t=\frac{a!(2j)!}{(a+2j+1)!}u^{a+2j+1}$ from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:dirichletint}.
\end{proof}

So for $F=\sum c_{ab}(1-P_1)^aP_2^b$ on $\mathcal R_k$, $I_k(F)$ and $\sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)=kJ_k^{(k)}(F)$ are quadratic forms in the coefficients $c_{ab}$ with explicit rational entries, sums of the numbers $\langle a,b\rangle_k$ and $\langle a,b\rangle_{k-1}$. The best $F$ of a given degree is the top generalized eigenvector of the pair of Gram matrices, exactly as for one variable in Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy}, and the ratio for any rational choice of coefficients can be checked in exact arithmetic.

\begin{theorem}[Maynard, 2013; the second part conditional]\label{thm:mfive}
(a) $M_5\ge\frac{1417255}{708216}=2.00116\ldots$
(b) If the primes have level of distribution $\theta$ for some $\theta>\frac{2\cdot708216}{1417255}=0.99942\ldots$, as the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture predicts for every $\theta<1$, then $p_{n+1}-p_n\le12$ for infinitely many $n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) For $k=5$, Maynard's polynomial
\[
F=(1-P_1)P_2+\tfrac7{10}(1-P_1)^2+\tfrac1{14}P_2-\tfrac3{14}(1-P_1)
\]
gives, by Lemma~\ref{lem:symint} and exact rational arithmetic,
\[
I_5(F)=\frac{29509}{1222452000},\qquad\sum_{m=1}^5J_5^{(m)}(F)=\frac{40493}{838252800},
\]
whose ratio is $\frac{1417255}{708216}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ltwo}, $M_5$ is at least this ratio.
(b) Then $\frac\theta2M_5>1$. The $5$-tuple $\{0,2,6,8,12\}$ is admissible (Exercise~\ref{ex:admiss}), and Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion} with $\rho=1$ gives two primes among $n,n+2,n+6,n+8,n+12$ for infinitely many $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Records, quoted]
The following bounds are not proved in this book. (a) Maynard's paper gives $p_{n+1}-p_n\le600$ infinitely often. It rests on $M_{105}>4.002$, obtained from a polynomial in $P_1,P_2$ with $42$ coefficients whose top generalized eigenvalue is $4.0020697\ldots$, and on an admissible $105$-tuple of diameter $600$ found by Engelsma. Neither the coefficients nor the tuple is printed here. Under the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture the same data give $p_{n+2}-p_n\le600$. (b) The Polymath8b project enlarged the support of $F$ slightly beyond the simplex, at the price of a more delicate argument, optimized over far larger families of polynomials, and proved $p_{n+1}-p_n\le246$ infinitely often, with $k=50$. Under a generalized Elliott--Halberstam conjecture, which allows convolutions other than the primes, their bound is $6$. The book's own theorem is $4580$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap}).
\end{remark}

Six is where the method stops, even with every conjecture about distribution in progressions. The reason is Selberg's parity obstruction of Chapter~\ref{ch:sieves} (Theorem~\ref{thm:parity}): a sieve whose input is distribution in progressions cannot tell numbers with an even number of prime factors from numbers with an odd number, and the twin prime conjecture is exactly a statement that needs this distinction. For the weights of this chapter one sees the wall directly: with $k=2$, two primes among $n,n+2$ need $\frac\theta2M_2>1$, but $M_2\le2\log2<2$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:upper}), so not even $\theta=1$ would suffice.

\begin{beauty}
The sieve problem was about divisibility of $n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k$ by small primes, a question of arithmetic. After Theorem~\ref{thm:maynardsums} it became a question about functions on a simplex, a question of analysis. And the analysis was solved by probability: a product function is a product measure, the simplex constraint is an event about a sum of independent variables, and the law of large numbers says that the event is almost certain. Three branches of mathematics, each doing what it does best, and between them they found infinitely many intervals of bounded length with as many primes as one likes.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:pigeon}
(a) Deduce from Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap} that there is an even $h\le4580$ such that $p_{n+1}-p_n=h$ for infinitely many $n$.
(b) Deduce that $\liminf\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$, and more generally that $\liminf\frac{p_{n+m}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$ for every $m$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:mk581}
With $A=7$ and $T=39$, evaluate the bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:productF} for $k=581$ and for $k=600$. Which side of $4$ is each? Why does a larger $k$ help, although the factor $\frac{L^2}\gamma$ does not depend on $k$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:mtwo}
For $k=2$ and $F=1$ on $\mathcal R_2$, compute $I_2(F)$, $J_2^{(1)}(F)$ and the ratio. Then do the same for $F=1+c(1-t_1-t_2)$ and find the best $c$. Compare with $2\log2$ and with Figure~\ref{fig:mk}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:progs}
Let $q\ge1$ and $a$ with $(a,q)=1$, and let $p'_1<p'_2<\cdots$ be the primes congruent to $a$ modulo $q$. Show that for every $m$, $p'_{n+m}-p'_n$ is bounded for infinitely many $n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:gooddiff}
Call an even number $h$ \emph{good} if $p'-p=h$ for infinitely many pairs of primes $p<p'$, not necessarily consecutive. Let $Q=\prod_{p\le582}p$. Show that among any $582$ multiples of $Q$ there are two whose difference is good, and deduce that the good numbers have positive lower density: $\#\{h\le x:h\text{ good}\}\ge\frac x{2400Q}$ for $x$ large.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:upper}
(a) Show that $M_k\le\frac{k\log k}{k-1}$ for every $k\ge2$.
(b) Deduce that Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion} can never give two primes among $n,n+2$, whatever the level of distribution, and that it needs $k\ge e^{(1-1/k)\cdot2\rho/\theta}$, roughly, to give $\rho+1$ primes.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:pigeon}
\idea Bounded gaps give finitely many possible values, and one of them must recur forever.
\writeup (a) By Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap}, the set $E=\{n:p_{n+1}-p_n\le4580\}$ is infinite. For $n\in E$ with $p_n>2$ the gap is even, so it is one of the $2290$ values $2,4,\dots,4580$. A partition of the infinite set $E$ into finitely many classes has an infinite class: some $h$ occurs infinitely often.
(b) By Corollary~\ref{cor:mprimes}, $p_{n+m}-p_n\le Cm^3e^{4m}$ for infinitely many $n$, and for those $n$, $\frac{p_{n+m}-p_n}{\log p_n}\le\frac{Cm^3e^{4m}}{\log p_n}\to0$. The quotient is positive, so the $\liminf$ is $0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:mk581}
\idea The variance of the sum grows like $k$ and its distance to the threshold like $k$ too, so Chebyshev's ratio decays like $\frac1k$.
\writeup With $\mu=0.6619555\ldots$ and $\sigma^2=4.9441132\ldots$: for $k=581$, $\delta=581-39-580\mu=158.0657\ldots$ and $\frac{580\sigma^2}{\delta^2}=0.11477\ldots$, so the bound is $4.5175168\ldots\times0.88523\ldots=3.99903\ldots<4$. For $k=600$, $\delta=164.4886\ldots$, $\frac{599\sigma^2}{\delta^2}=0.10946\ldots$ and the bound is $4.02304\ldots>4$. The factor $\frac{L^2}\gamma$ is what the product function would give on the whole cube; the probability that the simplex cuts it off is the only place where $k$ enters. The mean of $X_1+\cdots+X_{k-1}$ is about $0.662k$, its standard deviation about $2.22\sqrt k$, and the threshold $k-39$: for larger $k$ the threshold is more standard deviations away.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:mtwo}
\idea On the triangle everything is a Beta integral.
\writeup For $F=1$: $I_2=\frac12$, and $\int_0^1F\dd t_1=1-t_2$, so $J_2^{(1)}=\int_0^1(1-t_2)^2\dd t_2=\frac13$. By symmetry $\sum_mJ_2^{(m)}=\frac23$ and the ratio is $\frac43=1.3333$. For $F=1+cx$ with $x=1-t_1-t_2$, Lemma~\ref{lem:dirichletint} gives $\int_{\mathcal R_2}x^j=\frac{j!}{(j+2)!}=\frac1{(j+1)(j+2)}$, so
\[
I_2=\frac12+\frac{2c}6+\frac{c^2}{12}=\frac{6+4c+c^2}{12}.
\]
The inner integral is $\int_0^{1-t_2}(1+c(1-t_2-t_1))\dd t_1=u+\frac c2u^2$ with $u=1-t_2$, so
\[
J_2^{(1)}=\int_0^1\Bigl(u^2+cu^3+\frac{c^2}4u^4\Bigr)\dd u=\frac13+\frac c4+\frac{c^2}{20},
\]
and the ratio is
\[
\rho(c)=\frac{2(\frac13+\frac c4+\frac{c^2}{20})}{\frac{6+4c+c^2}{12}}=\frac{40+30c+6c^2}{5(6+4c+c^2)},
\]
which is $\frac43$ at $c=0$, as it should be. Setting $\rho'(c)=0$: $(30+12c)(6+4c+c^2)=(40+30c+6c^2)(4+2c)$, which simplifies to $20-8c-6c^2=0$, i.e.\ $3c^2+4c-10=0$, $c=\frac{-2+\sqrt{34}}3=1.2770$. Then $\rho=1.38310$, already close to $2\log2=1.38629$ and to the degree-$11$ value $1.38593$ of Figure~\ref{fig:mk}. Even the best $F$ falls short of $2$: two primes among $n,n+h_1,n+h_2$ are out of reach.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:progs}
\idea Primes in a fixed progression are found by the same sieve, run inside that progression; nothing in Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy} cares which reduced class modulo a fixed number we sit in.
\writeup Let $k$ be as in Theorem~\ref{thm:maynard}, $\mathcal H'$ an admissible $k$-tuple, and $\mathcal H=q\mathcal H'$. It is admissible: for $p\mid q$ all elements are $0$ modulo $p$, so $\nu_p=1<p$; for $p\nmid q$ multiplication by $q$ permutes the classes modulo $p$, so $\nu_p(\mathcal H)=\nu_p(\mathcal H')<p$. Run Chapter~\ref{ch:gpy} with $W$ replaced by $W'=qW$ and with $\nu_0$ chosen modulo $W'$ such that $\nu_0\equiv a\pmod q$ and $(\nu_0+h_i,W)=1$; this is possible since for $p\mid q$, $\nu_0+h_i\equiv a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, and for the other $p\le D_0$ we choose as before. Once $D_0$ exceeds the primes dividing $q$, the primes dividing $W'$ are exactly those up to $D_0$, so $\frac{\varphi(W')}{W'}=\frac{\varphi(W)}W$, the weights are supported on $d$ prime to $W'$, and $W'\le q(\log\log N)^2$ is still small enough for every error term. Every step of Theorem~\ref{thm:maynardsums} goes through with the main terms of $S_1$ and $S_2$ multiplied by the same factor $\frac1q$, so the ratio, and Corollary~\ref{cor:mkcriterion}, are unchanged. Bombieri--Vinogradov still applies, since the moduli are now $qW\prod[d_i,e_i]$, at most $q$ times larger. So infinitely many $n\equiv a\pmod q$ have $m+1$ primes among $n+h_i$, all congruent to $a$ modulo $q$ since $q\mid h_i$, and $p'_{j+m}-p'_j\le\max\mathcal H-\min\mathcal H$ infinitely often.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:gooddiff}
\idea Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap}(a) holds for every admissible $582$-tuple, not just $\mathcal H_{582}$; a combinatorial argument turns ``every such tuple contains a good difference'' into ``good differences are dense''.
\writeup Any set $\mathcal H$ of $582$ multiples of $Q$ is admissible: for $p\le582$ all elements are $0$ modulo $p$, so only one class is covered, and for $p>582$ the $582$ elements cannot cover $p$ classes. Since $M_{582}>4$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:mk582}), Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap}(a) gives infinitely many translates $n+\mathcal H$ containing two primes. There are $\binom{582}2$ pairs in $\mathcal H$, so one pair $h<h'$ is the pair for infinitely many $n$, and $h'-h$ is good.
Now let $N$ be large and consider $Q,2Q,\dots,NQ$. Choose a maximal subset $S$ in which no difference is good. Then $|S|\le581$. By maximality, every $jQ\notin S$ differs from some element of $S$ by a good number $d\le NQ$. Each $s\in S$ and each good $d\le NQ$ account for at most two values $jQ=s\pm d$, so
\[
N\le|S|+2|S|\cdot\#\{d\le NQ\text{ good}\}\le581+1162\,\#\{d\le NQ\text{ good}\}.
\]
For large $x$ take $N=\lfloor x/Q\rfloor\ge\frac xQ-1$: then $\#\{h\le x\text{ good}\}\ge\frac{x/Q-582}{1162}\ge\frac x{2400Q}$ once $x\ge1200Q$. Maynard proved much more: for each $m$, a positive proportion of admissible $m$-tuples $\mathcal H$ have infinitely many $n$ with \emph{all} of $n+\mathcal H$ prime.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:upper}
\idea Cauchy--Schwarz with a well-chosen weight turns each $J$ into an integral of $F^2$, and the weights sum to a constant.
\writeup (a) By Lemma~\ref{lem:ltwo}'s density argument it is enough to treat $F\in L^2(\mathcal R_k)$. Fix $\mathbf t'=(t_1,\dots,t_{k-1})$ with $s=\sum_{i<k}t_i<1$, and let $w(t)=1-s+(k-1)t$ for $0\le t\le1-s$. By Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
\Bigl(\int_0^{1-s}F\dd t_k\Bigr)^2\le\int_0^{1-s}\frac{\dd t}{w(t)}\int_0^{1-s}w(t_k)F^2\dd t_k,\qquad\int_0^{1-s}\frac{\dd t}{1-s+(k-1)t}=\frac{\log k}{k-1}.
\]
Integrating over $\mathbf t'$, $J_k^{(k)}(F)\le\frac{\log k}{k-1}\int_{\mathcal R_k}\bigl(1-\sum_{i\ne k}t_i+(k-1)t_k\bigr)F^2\dd\mathbf t$. The same holds for every $m$, and with $S=\sum_it_i$,
\[
\sum_{m=1}^k\Bigl(1-(S-t_m)+(k-1)t_m\Bigr)=k-kS+S+(k-1)S=k.
\]
So $\sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)\le\frac{k\log k}{k-1}I_k(F)$.
(b) For $k=2$, $M_2\le2\log2<2\le\frac2\theta$ for every $\theta\le1$, so $\frac\theta2M_2>1$ is impossible: no level of distribution gives two primes among $n+h_1,n+h_2$, in particular twin primes. In general, $\frac\theta2M_k>\rho$ forces $\frac{k\log k}{k-1}>\frac{2\rho}\theta$, that is $\log k>(1-\frac1k)\frac{2\rho}\theta$. To get $\rho+1$ primes one needs $k$ exponentially large in $\rho$, which explains the $e^{4m}$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:mprimes}.
\end{solution}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The epigraph asked whether the book could prove a bounded gap of its own. It can. Maynard's sieve reduces bounded gaps to one inequality, $M_k>4$, about functions on a simplex (Theorem~\ref{thm:maynard}). A product function turns that inequality into a statement about a sum of independent random variables, and Chebyshev's inequality bounds the chance that the sum is too large (Lemma~\ref{lem:productF}). For large $k$ this gives $M_k>\log k-2\log\log k-2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:mklower}), hence $m+1$ primes in bounded intervals for every $m$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:mprimes}). For $k=582$, with $A=7$ and $T=39$, it gives $M_{582}>4$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:mk582}), and the $582$ primes from $587$ to $5167$ give $p_{n+1}-p_n\le4580$ infinitely often (Theorem~\ref{thm:explicitgap}). Under the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture the exact value for $k=5$ gives $12$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:mfive}). The records $600$ and $246$ are quoted, with the reason. The parity wall of Chapter~\ref{ch:sieves} stops every such method before twin primes.

Part~III leaves the gaps and asks what the primes look like as a whole. A multiplicative function is decided by its values at the primes; when are its averages decided too? Chapter~\ref{ch:halasz} answers with Halász's theorem, and it gives a second proof that the Möbius function averages to zero, a proof that never mentions the zeros of $\zeta$.
