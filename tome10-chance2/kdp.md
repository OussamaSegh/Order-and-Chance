# Description

Toss a fair coin forever. The proportion of heads tends to one half, but how fast? How rarely does it stray far, and what shape does the whole path take when you look at it from far away? This book answers with the great limit theorems of probability in their sharp, modern forms, each proved in full.

The first part is about sums of independent variables. Kolmogorov's zero-one law and Etemadi's strong law of large numbers open the way. Donsker's theorem turns every random walk into Brownian motion and gives, at no extra cost, the arcsine law and the law of the iterated logarithm. Berry and Esseen bound the error of the central limit theorem, the Lindeberg-Feller theorem says exactly when it holds, and the stable laws of Lévy replace the bell curve when variances are infinite.

The second part is about approximation and rare events. Stein's method measures the distance to the normal and Poisson laws with explicit constants. Cramér and Sanov compute the probability of rare events, and explain why the Gibbs distributions of physics appear whenever we condition on an average. The concentration of measure, through the inequalities of Efron-Stein, McDiarmid, the Gaussian log-Sobolev inequality and Talagrand's convex distance, explains why functions of many independent variables are almost constant, and proves the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma behind dimension reduction.

The third part leaves independence behind. Martingales return in full strength: uniform integrability, backward martingales, de Finetti's theorem on exchangeable sequences, Kakutani's dichotomy, and Ville's inequality behind the anytime-valid tests of modern A/B testing. Birkhoff's ergodic theorem computes the statistics of the continued fraction of almost every real number, and Furstenberg's method proves that every set of integers of positive density contains three terms in arithmetic progression.

The book ends where probability meets arithmetic. Mod-phi convergence sharpens the central limit theorem until it sees the same factor, one over the Gamma function, in the cycles of a random permutation and in the prime factors of a random integer. Finally, the Poisson-Dirichlet law: the cycle lengths of a random permutation and the sizes of the prime factors of a random integer, on a logarithmic scale, follow the same broken stick, and Dickman's function counts the smooth numbers behind modern factoring algorithms.

Every result is proved in full, with one clearly marked exception from analytic number theory. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas, checked against the original sources, and has pause-and-think questions, figures, and graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea.

Every chapter comes with a Python notebook that checks the numbers in the text and opens a window on computer science: Monte Carlo error, tests for random number generators, A/B testing, hash collisions, importance sampling, dimension reduction, bandit algorithms, Markov chain Monte Carlo, irreducible polynomials for finite fields, and the analysis of factoring by trial division.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
