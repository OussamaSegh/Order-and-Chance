\chapter{The Erd\H{o}s--Kac Theorem}\label{ch:erdoskac}

\begin{center}\itshape
Primes play a game of chance.\\
\upshape --- Mark Kac, \emph{Statistical Independence in Probability, Analysis and Number Theory} (1959)
\end{center}

How many distinct prime factors does a large integer have? The power $2^{100}$ has one. The product of the first twenty primes has twenty. A large prime has one. Those three integers do not look like one another.

Ask about a typical integer instead. In 1917 Hardy and Ramanujan proved that almost every $n$ has about $\log\log n$ distinct prime factors. This logarithm grows slowly. Near $10^{9}$ one has $\log\log n=3.03$. Near $10^{99}$, a hundred-digit integer, one has $\log\log n=5.43$. Their argument was long. In 1934 Turán computed a variance, and the proof fitted on a page.

Kac later wrote that he first stated the Gaussian conjecture in a lecture at Princeton in March 1939. He treated ``$p$ divides $n$'' as a coin toss, heads with probability $\frac1p$, one coin for each prime, and the coins independent. Mertens's theorem then makes the mean and the variance both about $\log\log n$. Kac had the probability. He did not have a sieve. Erd\H{o}s was in the audience. Kac recalled that Erd\H{o}s had the proof before the lecture ended. The announcement is a note communicated on 13 March 1939. The proof is the paper of 1940, cited at the end of the chapter.

\begin{think}
Suppose that for each prime $p\le n$ the event $p\mid n$ has probability $\frac1p$, and that these events are independent. What are the mean and the variance of the number of such primes? Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2} gives the mean. Why should the variance sit close to the mean?
\end{think}

Figure~\ref{fig:omega60} is the count for every $n\le 60$. Three integers in that range have three distinct prime factors, and none has four. The mean is $1.6$. The value $\log\log 60$ is $1.41$. The same count up to $10^{7}$, in Figure~\ref{fig:omega}, is still far from a Gaussian curve. The theorem proved below is a limit. The limit arrives slowly.

\section{Turán's variance}

Write $\omega(n)$ for the number of distinct primes dividing $n$, and $\Omega(n)$ for the number counted with multiplicity. Thus $\omega(12)=2$ and $\Omega(12)=3$, because $12=2^2\cdot 3$. Both functions are additive: $\omega(mn)=\omega(m)+\omega(n)$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. The function $\omega$ is strongly additive, which means $\omega(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}1$. Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens} already has the mean, in Exercise~\ref{ex:averageomega}: $\sum_{n\le x}\omega(n)=x\log\log x+O(x)$. Turán's step is to compute the variance as well. The same computation works for every strongly additive function.

Take $f(p)=1$ for every prime, so that $f(n)=\omega(n)$. The sums in the next statement are then $A(x)=\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p$ and $B(x)^2=\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p$. Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2} says both are $\log\log x+O(1)$. The inequality below turns that into a variance bound.

\begin{theorem}[the Turán--Kubilius inequality]\label{thm:tk}
There is an absolute constant $C$ such that, for every $x\ge3$ and every real function $f$ on the primes, the strongly additive function $f(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}f(p)$ satisfies
\[
\begin{gathered}\sum_{n\le x}\bigl(f(n)-A(x)\bigr)^2\le CxB(x)^2,\qq\text{where}\\A(x)=\sum_{p\le x}\frac{f(p)}p,\qq B(x)^2=\sum_{p\le x}\frac{f(p)^2}p.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{theorem}

Read the inequality in probability language. Draw $n$ uniformly from $\{1,\dots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}$. The variance of $f(n)$ is at most a constant times $B(x)^2$. Up to the factors $1-\frac1p$, that quantity $B(x)^2$ is the variance of $\sum_pf(p)X_p$ for independent coins $X_p$ of mean $\frac1p$. Dependence among the events $p\mid n$ can cost only a constant factor.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Small primes.} Suppose first that $f(p)=0$ for $p>\sqrt x$, and write $\sum_p$ for sums over $p\le\sqrt x$. Every $p\mid n$ with $n\le x$ is counted by $\lfloor\frac xp\rfloor$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}f(n)&=\sum_pf(p)\Bigl\lfloor\frac xp\Bigr\rfloor=xA+E_2,\eqbr\text{with }|E_2|\le\sum_p|f(p)|.\end{aligned}
\]
For the square, $f(n)^2=\sum_{p,q\mid n}f(p)f(q)$, and for $p\ne q$ both divide $n$ exactly when $pq$ does. Since $pq\le x$ for $p,q\le\sqrt x$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}f(n)^2&=\sum_{p\ne q}f(p)f(q)\Bigl\lfloor\frac x{pq}\Bigr\rfloor+\sum_pf(p)^2\Bigl\lfloor\frac xp\Bigr\rfloor\\&\le x\Bigl(A^2-\sum_p\frac{f(p)^2}{p^2}\Bigr)+|E_1|+xB^2,\end{aligned}
\]
with $|E_1|\le\bigl(\sum_p|f(p)|\bigr)^2$. Expanding the square and using $\lfloor x\rfloor\le x$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}(f(n)-A)^2&=\sum f(n)^2-2A\sum f(n)\eqbr+\lfloor x\rfloor A^2\\&\le xB^2+|E_1|+2|A||E_2|.\end{aligned}
\]
By Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_p|f(p)|&\le\Bigl(\sum_p\frac{f(p)^2}p\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{p\le\sqrt x}p\Bigr)^{1/2}\eqbr\le B\sqrt{\sqrt x\,\pi(\sqrt x)},\end{aligned}
\]
so $|E_1|\le B^2\sqrt x\,\pi(\sqrt x)=O(\frac{xB^2}{\log x})$ by Chebyshev's bound
(Proposition~\ref{prop:chebyshev}), and $|E_2|\le Bx^{1/2}$. Also $|A|\le B\bigl(\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p\bigr)^{1/2}=O(B\sqrt{\log\log x})$, so $2|A||E_2|=O(B^2\sqrt x\log x)$. All three terms are $O(xB^2)$.

\emph{Large primes.} In general write $f=f_1+f_2$, where $f_1(p)=f(p)$ for $p\le\sqrt x$ and $f_2(p)=f(p)$ for $\sqrt x<p\le x$; then $A=A_1+A_2$, and $B_1,B_2\le B$. An integer $n\le x$ has at most
one prime factor $p>\sqrt x$, so $f_2(n)^2=f_2(p)^2$ or $0$, and $\sum_{n\le x}f_2(n)^2\le\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}f(p)^2\frac xp\le xB^2$. By Cauchy--Schwarz, $A_2^2\le B^2\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}\frac1p=O(B^2)$ by
Mertens's theorem. Hence $\sum_{n\le x}(f_2(n)-A_2)^2\le2\sum f_2(n)^2+2xA_2^2=O(xB^2)$, and $(a+b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2$ combines the two parts.
\end{proof}

The case $f(p)=1$ is the theorem of Hardy and Ramanujan, with Turán's proof.

\begin{theorem}[Hardy and Ramanujan 1917, Turán 1934]\label{thm:hr}
$\displaystyle\sum_{n\le x}\bigl(\omega(n)-\log\log x\bigr)^2=O(x\log\log x)$. Consequently, for every function $\psi(x)\to\infty$, the proportion of integers $n\le x$ with $|\omega(n)-\log\log x|>\psi(x)\sqrt{\log\log x}$ tends to $0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
With $f(p)=1$, $A(x)=B(x)^2=\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+O(1)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2}. So $\sum_{n\le x}(\omega(n)-\log\log x)^2\le2\sum(\omega(n)-A)^2+2x(A-\log\log x)^2=O(x\log\log x)$. The second
statement is Chebyshev's inequality: the number of exceptions is at most $\frac{O(x\log\log x)}{\psi(x)^2\log\log x}$.
\end{proof}

One says that $\omega(n)$ has normal order $\log\log n$. The meaning is: for every $\varepsilon>0$, the integers $n\le x$ with $|\omega(n)-\log\log n|>\varepsilon\log\log n$ have density $0$. Replacing $\log\log x$ by $\log\log n$ does not change the count. For $\sqrt x<n\le x$ one has $\log\log n=\log\log x+O(1)$. The same normal order holds for $\Omega(n)$, by Exercise~\ref{ex:omegaOmega}. That exercise keeps a promise of Chapter~\ref{ch:averages}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:taunormal}
For every $\varepsilon>0$, the integers $n$ with $(\log n)^{\log2-\varepsilon}\le\tau(n)\le(\log n)^{\log2+\varepsilon}$ have density $1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For every $n$, $2^{\omega(n)}\le\tau(n)\le2^{\Omega(n)}$, since $\tau(n)=\prod_{p^a\,\|\,n}(a+1)$ and $2\le a+1\le2^a$. For almost all $n$, both $\omega(n)$ and $\Omega(n)$ lie between $(1-\delta)\log\log n$ and $(1+\delta)\log\log n$,
and $2^{\log\log n}=(\log n)^{\log2}$.
\end{proof}

So a typical integer has about $(\log n)^{\log 2}$ divisors. Since $\log 2=0.693$, that is about $(\log n)^{0.693}$ divisors. The average number of divisors is $\log n$, from Chapter~\ref{ch:averages}. The two statements live together because the average is carried by rare integers with very many divisors. Exercise~\ref{ex:taumean} makes that split precise. The same split appears whenever a quantity is the exponential of a sum.

\section{The model}

Kac's picture is a probability space on which divisibility by different primes is exactly independent. Fix $z\ge2$. Let $(X_p)_{p\le z}$ be independent random variables with $P(X_p=1)=\frac1p$ and $P(X_p=0)=1-\frac1p$. Volume~4 builds such finite families. The model of $\omega$ is the sum $\sum_{p\le z}X_p$. Its centred version is
\[
\begin{gathered}S_z=\sum_{p\le z}\Bigl(X_p-\frac1p\Bigr),\qq\sigma_z^2=\operatorname{Var}S_z=\sum_{p\le z}\frac1p\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr).\end{gathered}
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:coincount} draws the four coins for $z=7$. One outcome is filled in: heads on $2$ and $5$, tails on $3$ and $7$. Its probability is
\[
\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac15\cdot\frac67=\frac2{35}.
\]
Among the integers $n\le 60$, the same pattern of divisibility holds for $10$, $20$, $40$ and $50$, four integers out of sixty. The proportion is $\frac1{15}=0.0667$, and $\frac2{35}=0.0571$. At this scale the coins and the integers are close, and they are not identical.

The expected number of heads among these four coins is
\[
\frac12+\frac13+\frac15+\frac17=\frac{247}{210}=1.176.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2} gives $\sigma_z^2=\log\log z+O(1)$, so $\sigma_z^2\to\infty$ as $z\to\infty$. The sum $S_z$ is made of many small independent pieces. Its moments should approach the Gaussian moments. Those moments are what the next lemma records.

Let $m_k$ be the moments of the standard Gaussian law. Then $m_k=0$ for odd $k$, and
\[
m_{2j}=\frac{(2j)!}{2^j j!}.
\]
For even $k=2j$ this is the double factorial $m_{2j}=(2j-1)!!=1\cdot 3\cdots(2j-1)$. The two formulas agree because $(2j)!/(2^j j!)=(2j-1)!!$. If $Z$ is standard normal, integration by parts gives $\mathbb E[Z^k]=(k-1)\mathbb E[Z^{k-2}]$. Check the first two even values by hand. The recurrence gives $\mathbb E[Z^2]=1$ and $\mathbb E[Z^4]=3\cdot\mathbb E[Z^2]=3$. The factorial formula gives $m_2=2!/2=1$ and $m_4=24/(4\cdot 2)=3$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:modelmoments}
For every fixed $k\ge1$, $\mathbb E\bigl[(S_z/\sigma_z)^k\bigr]\to m_k$ as $z\to\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $Y_p=X_p-\frac1p$, so $\mathbb E Y_p=0$, $|Y_p|\le1$ and, for $j\ge2$, $|\mathbb E Y_p^j|\le\mathbb E Y_p^2=v_p$, where $v_p=\frac1p(1-\frac1p)\le\frac1p$. Expand
\[
\mathbb E[S_z^k]=\sum_{p_1,\dots,p_k\le z}\mathbb E[Y_{p_1}\cdots Y_{p_k}].
\]
Group the tuples $(p_1,\dots,p_k)$ according to the partition of $\{1,\dots,k\}$ into blocks of equal indices. If some block is a singleton $\{i\}$, then $Y_{p_i}$ is independent of the others and the term
vanishes. Consider a partition with blocks of sizes $j_1,\dots,j_b\ge2$, all at least $2$, so $b\le\frac k2$. By independence the term is $\prod_l\mathbb E[Y_{q_l}^{j_l}]$, for distinct primes $q_1,\dots,q_b$; its modulus is at
most $\prod_lv_{q_l}$, so the whole partition contributes at most $\bigl(\sum_{p\le z}v_p\bigr)^b=\sigma_z^{2b}$.

If $k$ is odd, every admissible partition has $b\le\frac{k-1}2$, and there are finitely many partitions (their number depends only on $k$), so $\mathbb E[S_z^k]=O_k(\sigma_z^{k-1})$, which is $o(\sigma_z^k)$.

If $k$ is even, the partitions with $b<\frac k2$ again contribute $O_k(\sigma_z^{k-2})$. The remaining ones are the pairings, all blocks of size $2$; there are $(k-1)!!$ of them (pair $1$ with one of the $k-1$ others,
and continue). Each pairing contributes $\sum v_{q_1}\cdots v_{q_{k/2}}$ over \emph{distinct} $q_1,\dots,q_{k/2}$. Without the distinctness condition the sum would be $\sigma_z^k$; the tuples with a repetition
contribute at most $\binom{k/2}2\bigl(\sum_pv_p^2\bigr)\sigma_z^{k-4}=O_k(\sigma_z^{k-4})$, since $\sum_pv_p^2\le\sum_p\frac1{p^2}<1$. So $\mathbb E[S_z^k]=(k-1)!!\,\sigma_z^k+O_k(\sigma_z^{k-2})$.
\end{proof}

Now the integers. For $n\ge1$ let $\omega_z(n)$ be the number of primes $p\le z$ dividing $n$, and $\mu_z=\sum_{p\le z}\frac1p$. Choose $n$ uniformly in $\{1,\dots,N\}$ and write $\mathbb E_N$ for the
average over $n\le N$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:compare}
For all $k\ge1$, $N\ge1$ and $z\ge2$,
\[
\Bigl|\mathbb E_N\bigl[(\omega_z(n)-\mu_z)^k\bigr]-\mathbb E[S_z^k]\Bigr|\le\frac{(2z)^k}N.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Both sides are sums over $(p_1,\dots,p_k)$ with $p_i\le z$: on the left, $\omega_z(n)-\mu_z=\sum_{p\le z}(\mathbf 1_{p\mid n}-\frac1p)$. Fix such a tuple. Expanding the product,
\[
\prod_{i=1}^k\Bigl(\mathbf 1_{p_i\mid n}-\frac1{p_i}\Bigr)=\sum_{I\subset\{1,\dots,k\}}\ \prod_{i\notin I}\Bigl(-\frac1{p_i}\Bigr)\,\mathbf 1_{r_I\mid n},
\]
where $r_I$ is the product of the \emph{distinct} primes among the $p_i$, $i\in I$: the $p_i$ with $i\in I$ all divide $n$ exactly when $r_I$ does. The same expansion holds with $X_{p_i}$ in place of
$\mathbf 1_{p_i\mid n}$, and $\mathbb E\bigl[\prod_{i\in I}X_{p_i}\bigr]=\frac1{r_I}$ by independence and $X_p^2=X_p$. On the integers, $\mathbb E_N[\mathbf 1_{r\mid n}]=\frac1N\lfloor\frac Nr\rfloor=\frac1r-\frac\theta N$ with
$0\le\theta<1$. So the tuple contributes a difference of at most $\frac1N\sum_I\prod_{i\notin I}\frac1{p_i}=\frac1N\prod_i\bigl(1+\frac1{p_i}\bigr)\le\frac{2^k}N$. There are at most $z^k$ tuples.
\end{proof}

Here is a small case of the same comparison. Take $k=2$, the primes $p=2$ and $q=3$, and $N=60$. The model probability that both coins are heads is $\frac16$. The proportion of $n\le 60$ divisible by $6$ is $\frac{10}{60}=\frac16$. The two sides agree. For $p=5$ and $q=7$ the model probability is $\frac1{35}=0.0286$, and exactly one integer up to $60$ is divisible by $35$, so the proportion is $\frac1{60}=0.0167$. Lemma~\ref{lem:compare} bounds the difference of the second-moment averages by $\frac{(2z)^k}N$. For $z=7$ and $k=2$ that bound is $\frac{196}{60}$, which is larger than $1$. The lemma is crude, and it is still strong enough once $z^k$ is small beside $N$.

The lemma is the point at which independence of small primes becomes a theorem. As long as $z^k$ is small compared with $N$, the integers up to $N$ cannot tell the difference between divisibility by the primes up to $z$ and independent coin tosses. This is Kubilius's model. It works for the small primes and fails for primes near $\sqrt N$. That failure is where the factor $2e^{-\gamma}$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens} came from.

\section{From moments to the Gaussian law}

One principle of probability finishes the argument. If every moment of $Y_N$ converges to the corresponding Gaussian moment $m_k$, then $Y_N$ converges in law to the standard Gaussian. Volume~4 states Lévy's continuity theorem and leaves its proof to Volume~11. The special case used here is short, and it is proved from Volume~4's inversion formula. Let $\Phi$ be the distribution function of $N(0,1)$. The characteristic function of a standard normal is $e^{-t^2/2}$ (Volume~4, Proposition~6.7). If $Z$ is standard, $sZ$ has characteristic function $e^{-s^2t^2/2}$, which is the transform of $N(0,s^2)$.

The variable $Y_N$ itself may have jumps, so its distribution function need not have a density. The inversion below is applied only after adding an independent Gaussian $\varepsilon G$. That sum has an integrable characteristic function. Volume~4's inversion formula then supplies a continuous density. Volume~5's Fourier inversion asks for a continuous integrable function whose transform is integrable. It is not used here, and it is not applied to a jump.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:momentslaw}
Let $Y_N$ be real random variables, and let $m_k$ be the Gaussian moments defined above ($m_k=0$ for odd $k$, and $m_{2j}=(2j)!/(2^j j!)$). If $\mathbb E[Y_N^k]\to m_k$ for every $k\ge1$, then $P(Y_N\le a)\to\Phi(a)$ for every real $a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Characteristic functions converge.} For real $u$ and $K\ge1$, Taylor's formula with integral remainder gives $\bigl|e^{iu}-\sum_{k<K}\frac{(iu)^k}{k!}\bigr|\le\frac{|u|^K}{K!}$. Apply it with $u=tY_N$, take expectations,
and choose $K$ even, so that $\mathbb E|Y_N|^K=\mathbb E[Y_N^K]$:
\[
\Bigl|\varphi_{Y_N}(t)-\sum_{k<K}\frac{(it)^k}{k!}\mathbb E[Y_N^k]\Bigr|\le\frac{|t|^K}{K!}\mathbb E[Y_N^K].
\]
As $N\to\infty$ the sum tends to $\sum_{k<K}\frac{(it)^km_k}{k!}=\sum_{j<K/2}\frac{(-t^2/2)^j}{j!}$, because $\frac{m_{2j}}{(2j)!}=\frac1{2^jj!}$, and the right side tends to $\frac{|t|^K}{2^{K/2}(K/2)!}$. Both
$\bigl|\sum_{j<K/2}\frac{(-t^2/2)^j}{j!}-e^{-t^2/2}\bigr|$ and $\frac{|t|^K}{2^{K/2}(K/2)!}$ tend to $0$ as $K\to\infty$. So $\varphi_{Y_N}(t)\to e^{-t^2/2}$ for every $t$.

\emph{Smoothing.} Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and let $G$ be $N(0,1)$, independent of $Y_N$. The characteristic function of $Y_N+\varepsilon G$ is $\varphi_{Y_N}(t)e^{-\varepsilon^2t^2/2}$, which is integrable, so by the inversion formula
(Volume~4, Theorem~6.8(b)) $Y_N+\varepsilon G$ has the continuous density $q_N(y)=\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-ity}\varphi_{Y_N}(t)e^{-\varepsilon^2t^2/2}\dd t$. The Taylor remainder in the first paragraph is uniform for $|t|\le A$: the bound $\frac{|t|^K}{K!}\mathbb E[Y_N^K]$ depends on $t$ only through $|t|$, and the moments converge, so $\varphi_{Y_N}(t)\to e^{-t^2/2}$ uniformly on each bounded interval. Volume~4's dominated-convergence theorem therefore applies. The integrand is bounded by $e^{-\varepsilon^2t^2/2}$,
so $q_N(y)\to q(y)$ for every $y$, where $q$ is the density of $N(0,1+\varepsilon^2)$. The same domination gives $\int|q_N-q|\to0$ (Scheffé's lemma: $\int|q-q_N|=2\int(q-q_N)^+$ since both integrate to $1$, and $0\le(q-q_N)^+\le q$). Hence $P(Y_N+\varepsilon G\le b)\to\Phi\bigl(\frac b{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}}\bigr)$ for every $b$.

\emph{Removing the smoothing.} For $\eta>0$, $\{Y_N\le a\}\subset\{Y_N+\varepsilon G\le a+\eta\}\cup\{\varepsilon G>\eta\}$ and $\{Y_N+\varepsilon G\le a-\eta\}\subset\{Y_N\le a\}\cup\{\varepsilon G<-\eta\}$. So
\[
\begin{aligned}\liminf P(Y_N\le a)&\ge\Phi\Bigl(\frac{a-\eta}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}}\Bigr)-\Phi\Bigl(-\frac\eta\varepsilon\Bigr),\\\limsup P(Y_N\le a)&\le\Phi\Bigl(\frac{a+\eta}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}}\Bigr)+\Phi\Bigl(-\frac\eta\varepsilon\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
(by symmetry, $P(\varepsilon G>\eta)=P(\varepsilon G<-\eta)=\Phi(-\frac\eta\varepsilon)$). Let $\varepsilon\to0$ with $\eta$ fixed, then $\eta\to0$.
\end{proof}

\section{The theorem}

The cut-off has to be chosen before the limit is taken. The primes up to $z$ must be few enough for Lemma~\ref{lem:compare}, and numerous enough for $\sigma_z$ to be almost $\sqrt{\log\log x}$. The choice below is $z=x^{1/\beta}$ with $\beta=(\log\log x)^{1/4}$.

\begin{theorem}[Erd\H{o}s and Kac, 1940]\label{thm:erdoskac}
For every real $a$, the proportion of integers $n\le x$ such that
\[
\frac{\omega(n)-\log\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\le a
\]
tends to $\Phi(a)=\int_{-\infty}^a\frac{e^{-t^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\dd t$ as $x\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$, $L=\log\log x$, and choose the cut-off $z=x^{1/\beta}$ with $\beta=L^{1/4}$, so that $\beta\to\infty$ but $\beta=o(\sqrt L)$.

\emph{The truncated function is Gaussian.} Let $Y_N(n)=\frac{\omega_z(n)-\mu_z}{\sigma_z}$ for $n$ uniform in $\{1,\dots,N\}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:compare}, for each fixed $k$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\Bigl|\mathbb E_N[Y_N^k]-\mathbb E\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{S_z}{\sigma_z}\Bigr)^k\Bigr]\Bigr|&\le\frac{(2z)^k}{N\sigma_z^k}\eqbr\le\frac{2^{k+1}x^{k/\beta}}x\to0,\end{aligned}
\]
since $\frac k\beta\to0$. With Lemma~\ref{lem:modelmoments}, $\mathbb E_N[Y_N^k]\to m_k$ for every $k$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:momentslaw} gives $P_N(Y_N\le b)\to\Phi(b)$ for every $b$.

\emph{Back to $\omega$.} For $n\le x$, the primes $p>z$ dividing $n$ number at most $\frac{\log x}{\log z}=\beta$, so $0\le\omega(n)-\omega_z(n)\le\beta$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2}, $\mu_z=\log\log z+M+o(1)=L-\log\beta+M+o(1)$, and
$\sigma_z^2=\mu_z+O(1)$, so $c_x=\frac{\sigma_z}{\sqrt L}\to1$. Hence
\[
\begin{gathered}\frac{\omega(n)-L}{\sqrt L}=c_xY_N(n)+\rho_x(n),\qq|\rho_x(n)|\le\eta_x=\frac{\beta+\log\beta+O(1)}{\sqrt L},\end{gathered}
\]
uniformly in $n\le x$, and $\eta_x\to0$. Since $c_x>0$, if $Y_N(n)\le\frac{a-\eta_x}{c_x}$ then $\frac{\omega(n)-L}{\sqrt L}\le a$, and if $\frac{\omega(n)-L}{\sqrt L}\le a$ then $Y_N(n)\le\frac{a+\eta_x}{c_x}$. Both bounds tend to $a$. So for every $\delta>0$
and $x$ large, the proportion in the theorem lies between $P_N(Y_N\le a-\delta)$ and $P_N(Y_N\le a+\delta)$, which tend to $\Phi(a-\delta)$ and $\Phi(a+\delta)$. Let $\delta\to0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[how fast, and beyond]
Rényi and Turán (1958) showed that the error in the theorem is $O\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt{\log\log x}}\bigr)$, and this is the true order. At $x=10^7$, $\frac1{\sqrt{\log\log x}}$ is still $0.6$: the proportion of $n\le10^7$ with $\omega(n)\le\log\log x$ is $0.32$, against the limit $0.5$, and Figure~\ref{fig:omega} looks nothing like a
bell curve. A sharper description is the Sathe--Selberg formula (1954): for fixed $k\ge1$, the number of $n\le x$ with $\omega(n)=k$ is
\[
\frac x{\log x}\frac{(\log\log x)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\Bigl(G\Bigl(\frac{k-1}{\log\log x}\Bigr)+o(1)\Bigr),
\]
for an explicit function $G$ with $G(0)=1$. We quote it without proof. Up to the factor $G$, this is the Poisson law of mean $\log\log x$, shifted by one, which is what the model predicts if one adds up rare independent
events; for fixed $k$, $G(\frac{k-1}{\log\log x})\to G(0)=1$, and this is Landau's theorem of 1900, whose case $k=2$ is Exercise~\ref{ex:semiprimes}. The Poisson law of large mean is close to Gaussian, which is the Erd\H{o}s--Kac theorem again. The same theorem holds for every
strongly additive $f$ with bounded values on the primes and $B(x)\to\infty$, with mean $A(x)$ and variance $B(x)^2$ (Kubilius, Shapiro): the proof above goes through with minor changes.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
Take $n$ at random up to a large $x$. The primes that divide $n$ behave like the coins of Figure~\ref{fig:coincount}, with heads probabilities $\frac12,\frac13,\frac15,\frac17,\dots$ The integer $n$ is fixed by arithmetic. Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac} says that the count of its distinct prime factors, once centred and scaled, follows the Gaussian law. Kac called the phenomenon statistical independence.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The Gaussian law is Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac}. Three earlier statements carry the proof. Turán's variance, Theorem~\ref{thm:tk}, shows that a strongly additive function concentrates. The coin model has Gaussian moments, by Lemma~\ref{lem:modelmoments}. The integers up to $N$ have the same moments as the model when only primes up to a small $z$ are used, by Lemma~\ref{lem:compare}. Those moments determine the distribution function, by Lemma~\ref{lem:momentslaw}, where $m_k=0$ for odd $k$ and $m_{2j}=(2j)!/(2^j j!)$. Along the way, Corollary~\ref{cor:taunormal} says that a typical integer has about $(\log n)^{0.693}$ divisors.

The model fails for primes above $\sqrt x$. The events ``$p\mid n$'' are then disjoint, as Exercise~\ref{ex:largestprime} shows. The convergence is slow enough that at $10^7$ the Gaussian curve in Figure~\ref{fig:omega} is still far from the bars.

The coins in this chapter are attached to the divisors of a typical integer. Chapter~\ref{ch:random} attaches a coin to each integer $n\ge 3$, with heads probability $\frac1{\log n}$, and calls heads ``prime''. That model predicts the gaps between primes, the count of twin primes, and the size of $\pi(x)-\operatorname{Li}(x)$. A theorem in that chapter shows where the prediction fails.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:omegaOmega}
Show that $\sum_{n\le x}(\Omega(n)-\omega(n))=O(x)$. Deduce that $\Omega(n)$ has normal order $\log\log n$, and that Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac} holds with $\Omega$ in place of $\omega$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:taumean}
The average of $2^{\omega(n)}$ over $n\le x$ is about $\frac6{\pi^2}\log x$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:twoomega}), while a typical value is about $(\log x)^{0.69}$. Show that the $n\le x$ with $\omega(n)\le1.4\log\log x$
contribute $o(x\log x)$ to $\sum_{n\le x}2^{\omega(n)}$. So the sum is carried by a set of integers of density $0$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:skew}
Compute the third moment of $S_z$ and show that the skewness $\mathbb E[S_z^3]/\sigma_z^3$ is $\sim\frac1{\sqrt{\log\log z}}$. What does this say about the speed of convergence in Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:largestprime}
Let $P(n)$ be the largest prime factor of $n$. Show that the proportion of $n\le x$ with $P(n)>\sqrt x$ tends to $\log2$. (The model of independent primes would answer $1-\prod_{\sqrt x<p\le x}(1-\frac1p)\to\frac12$. Why is the model wrong here?)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$, the signature exercise]\label{ex:multtable}
Let $D(N)$ be the number of distinct integers in the $N\times N$ multiplication table, that is, of the form $ab$ with $1\le a,b\le N$. Show that $D(N)=o(N^2)$. (Erd\H{o}s, 1955.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:omega1mod4}
Let $\omega_1(n)$ be the number of primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$ dividing $n$. Show that $\omega_1(n)$ has normal order $\frac12\log\log n$, and that $\frac{\omega_1(n)-\frac12\log\log x}{\sqrt{\frac12\log\log x}}$ is asymptotically $N(0,1)$
for $n\le x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:omega60}
Figure~\ref{fig:omega60} plots $\omega(n)$ for $1\le n\le 60$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Count the integers in this range with $\omega(n)=0$, $1$, $2$ and $3$.
\item Compute the mean of $\omega(n)$ over $1\le n\le 60$, and compare it with $\log\log 60$.
\item List the three integers with $\omega(n)=3$. Why is there no $n\le 60$ with $\omega(n)=4$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:momentcheck}
The moments in Lemma~\ref{lem:momentslaw} satisfy $m_k=0$ for odd $k$ and $m_{2j}=(2j)!/(2^j j!)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $m_2$, $m_4$ and $m_6$ from the factorial formula.
\item Check the recurrence $m_{2j}=(2j-1)m_{2j-2}$ for $j\ge1$, with $m_0=1$.
\item Let $Z$ be standard normal. Why is $\mathbb E[Z^k]=0$ for every odd $k$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:cutoff16}
In the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac}, $\beta=L^{1/4}$ with $L=\log\log x$, and the truncation error satisfies $|\rho_x(n)|\le\eta_x=(\beta+\log\beta+O(1))/\sqrt L$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Take $L=16$. Compute $\beta$, $\log\beta$ and $\sqrt L$.
\item Compute the main piece $(\beta+\log\beta)/\sqrt L$.
\item Find the smallest integer $\beta\ge 2$ for which $(\beta+\log\beta)/\beta^2\le\frac12$. What is the corresponding $L$, and what does the size of that $L$ say about the estimate $\eta_x$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:fourthmodel}
Prove that $\mathbb E[S_z^4]=3\sigma_z^4+O(\sigma_z^2)$ as $z\to\infty$. Deduce $\mathbb E[(S_z/\sigma_z)^4]\to 3$. This is the case $k=4$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:modelmoments}. The fourth Gaussian moment is $m_4=3$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:omegaOmega}
\idea Repeated prime factors are rare. The event $p^2\mid n$ has probability $\frac1{p^2}$, and $\sum\frac1{p^2}$ converges. So $\Omega$ and $\omega$ differ by a bounded amount on average, and by a small amount for almost every $n$.
\writeup If $p^a\|n$ then $a-1$ is the number of $j\ge2$ with $p^j\mid n$. So $\sum_{n\le x}(\Omega(n)-\omega(n))\le\sum_{p^j\le x,\,j\ge2}\frac x{p^j}\le x\sum_p\frac1{p(p-1)}=O(x)$. By Markov's inequality, for $\psi(x)\to\infty$, the
$n\le x$ with $\Omega(n)-\omega(n)>\psi(x)$ number $O(\frac x{\psi(x)})=o(x)$. Taking $\psi=\varepsilon\log\log x$ gives the normal order. For Erd\H{o}s--Kac, take $\psi(x)=(\log\log x)^{1/4}$. Outside a set of density $0$,
$\frac{\Omega(n)-\log\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}$ and $\frac{\omega(n)-\log\log x}{\sqrt{\log\log x}}$ differ by at most $(\log\log x)^{-1/4}\to0$, and the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac} applies.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:taumean}
\idea A sum of exponentials is dominated by the largest exponents, even when those exponents are rare. A typical $n$ has $2^{\omega(n)}$ about $(\log x)^{0.69}$, far below the average, so the average has to come from atypical $n$.
\writeup If $\omega(n)\le1.4\log\log x$, then $2^{\omega(n)}\le2^{1.4\log\log x}=(\log x)^{1.4\log2}=(\log x)^{0.9704}$. So these $n$ contribute at most $x(\log x)^{0.9704}=o(x\log x)$, while the whole sum is
$\frac6{\pi^2}x\log x+O(x)$. The rest of the sum comes from the $n$ with $\omega(n)>1.4\log\log x$, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:hr} (with $\psi(x)=0.4\sqrt{\log\log x}$) they have density $0$. The same argument with
$1.4$ replaced by any $\lambda<\frac1{\log2}=1.4427$ shows that the mean of $2^{\omega(n)}$ is made by integers with at least about $1.44\log\log x$ prime factors, far in the tail of the Erd\H{o}s--Kac law.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:skew}
\idea The odd moments of the model come from blocks of size at least $3$. For the third moment, the only surviving blocks are triples of equal primes.
\writeup By independence and $\mathbb E Y_p=0$, $\mathbb E[S_z^3]=\sum_p\mathbb E[Y_p^3]$, and $\mathbb E[Y_p^3]=\frac1p(1-\frac1p)^3-(1-\frac1p)\frac1{p^3}=\frac1p(1-\frac1p)(1-\frac2p)$. So $\mathbb E[S_z^3]=\log\log z+O(1)$, like $\sigma_z^2$,
and the skewness is $\frac{\log\log z+O(1)}{(\log\log z+O(1))^{3/2}}\sim\frac1{\sqrt{\log\log z}}$. For a sum of independent variables, the Berry--Esseen theorem (proved in Volume~11, Chapter~4), which we quote here, bounds the distance to the Gaussian law by a constant times $\sum_p\mathbb E|Y_p|^3/\sigma_z^3$, which is of the same order $\frac1{\sqrt{\log\log z}}$. Rényi and Turán proved the same rate for $\omega$ itself. With all primes up to $10^7$
the model has skewness $0.49$, and $\log\log z$ would have to exceed $10^4$, so $z>e^{e^{10^4}}$, to bring it below $0.01$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:largestprime}
\idea At most one prime factor exceeds $\sqrt x$, so the events ``$p\mid n$'' for $p>\sqrt x$ are disjoint. Their probabilities add, and the sum is $\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}\frac1p\to\log2$. Independent events would combine differently.
\writeup If $n\le x$ has a prime factor $p>\sqrt x$, it has exactly one, since the product of two would exceed $x$. So the number of such $n$ is $\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}\lfloor\frac xp\rfloor=x\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}\frac1p+O(\pi(x))$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2}, $\sum_{\sqrt x<p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x-\log\log\sqrt x+O(\frac1{\log x})=\log2+O(\frac1{\log x})$, and $\pi(x)=O(\frac x{\log x})$. The proportion tends to $\log2=0.693$. The model treats ``$p\mid n$'' and ``$q\mid n$'' as independent for $p,q>\sqrt x$, but they are
incompatible. Its answer is $1-\exp\bigl(-\sum\frac1p+O(\frac1{\sqrt x})\bigr)\to1-\frac12=\frac12$. This is the same failure of the model for large primes as the factor $2e^{-\gamma}$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens}, and it is why Lemma~\ref{lem:compare} needs $z$ small.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:multtable}
\idea A product remembers how many prime factors its factors had. A typical product $ab$ has about $2\log\log N$ prime factors, counted with multiplicity. A typical integer up to $N^2$ has about $\log\log(N^2)=\log\log N+\log2$ of them. The typical products are atypical integers, and atypical integers are few.
\writeup Fix $\varepsilon\in\left]0,\frac14\right[$ and let $L=\log\log N$. By Exercise~\ref{ex:omegaOmega}, all but $o(N)$ integers $a\le N$ have $|\Omega(a)-L|\le\varepsilon L$. So all but $o(N^2)$ pairs $(a,b)$ have
$\Omega(ab)=\Omega(a)+\Omega(b)\ge(2-2\varepsilon)L$. On the other hand, $\log\log N^2=L+\log2$, so by the normal order of $\Omega$ (with $x=N^2$), all but $o(N^2)$ integers $m\le N^2$ have $\Omega(m)\le(1+\varepsilon)(L+\log2)<(2-2\varepsilon)L$ for large $N$, since $1+\varepsilon<2-2\varepsilon$.
Every product $ab$ is either the product of an exceptional pair, or an integer $m\le N^2$ with $\Omega(m)\ge(2-2\varepsilon)L$, an exceptional integer. So $D(N)\le o(N^2)+o(N^2)$.
Counting gives $D(N)/N^2=0.29$, $0.25$, $0.24$ for $N=100$, $1000$, $2000$. The decrease is very slow. Ford (2008) found the true order, $D(N)\asymp\frac{N^2}{(\log N)^c(\log\log N)^{3/2}}$ with $c=1-\frac{1+\log\log2}{\log2}=0.086\ldots$
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:omega1mod4}
\idea The proofs use the sum of $\frac1p$ over the primes that are counted. For the primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$ that sum is $\frac12\log\log x+O(1)$.
\writeup Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:tk} to $f(p)=1$ for $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $f(p)=0$ otherwise: $A(x)=B(x)^2=\sum_{p\le x,\,p\equiv1\,(4)}\frac1p=\frac12\log\log x+O(1)$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:recipprog}. As in Theorem~\ref{thm:hr},
$\sum_{n\le x}(\omega_1(n)-\frac12\log\log x)^2=O(x\log\log x)$, which gives the normal order. For the Gaussian law, repeat Lemmas~\ref{lem:modelmoments} and~\ref{lem:compare} with the primes $p\le z$, $p\equiv1\pmod4$ only:
the model has variance $\sigma_z^2=\sum_{p\le z,\,p\equiv1}\frac1p(1-\frac1p)=\frac12\log\log z+O(1)\to\infty$, which is all that Lemma~\ref{lem:modelmoments} used, and Lemma~\ref{lem:compare} holds verbatim. The end of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac} goes through with $L$ replaced by $\frac12\log\log x$: the truncation error is still at most $\beta$, and $\mu_z=\frac12\log\log x-\frac12\log\beta+O(1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:omega60}
\idea Count the dots in Figure~\ref{fig:omega60} by height, then add. Four distinct prime factors would require a multiple of $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$.
\writeup (a) The value $0$ occurs once, at $n=1$. The value $1$ occurs $25$ times, the value $2$ occurs $31$ times, and the value $3$ occurs $3$ times. The four counts add to $1+25+31+3=60$.

(b) The sum of the values is $0\cdot1+1\cdot25+2\cdot31+3\cdot3=25+62+9=96$. The mean is $\frac{96}{60}=1.6$. Also $\log\log 60=\log(4.0943\ldots)=1.410$. The mean sits a little above $\log\log 60$.

(c) The three integers are $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$, $42=2\cdot3\cdot7$ and $60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5$. The product of the first four primes is $210>60$, so no integer up to $60$ has four distinct prime factors.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:momentcheck}
\idea The factorial formula is the closed form of the recurrence $\mathbb E[Z^k]=(k-1)\mathbb E[Z^{k-2}]$. Odd moments vanish because the standard normal law is symmetric about $0$.
\writeup (a) For $j=1$, $m_2=2!/(2\cdot1!)=1$. For $j=2$, $m_4=24/(4\cdot2!)=3$. For $j=3$, $m_6=720/(8\cdot6)=15$.

(b) The identity $(2j)!=(2j)(2j-1)(2j-2)!$ and $2^j j!=2\cdot 2^{j-1}\cdot j\cdot(j-1)!$ give
\[
m_{2j}=\frac{(2j)(2j-1)}{2\cdot j}\,m_{2j-2}=(2j-1)m_{2j-2}.
\]
With $m_0=1$ this produces $1$, $3$ and $15$ again.

(c) The variables $Z$ and $-Z$ have the same law, so $\mathbb E[Z^k]=\mathbb E[(-Z)^k]$. For odd $k$ the right side is $-\mathbb E[Z^k]$. A finite number equal to its negative is $0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:cutoff16}
\idea The cut-off in the proof is $\beta=L^{1/4}$, so $\sqrt L=\beta^2$. The fraction $(\beta+\log\beta)/\sqrt L$ is the main piece of $\eta_x$. It becomes small only when $\beta$ is large, which forces $L=\beta^4$ to be huge.
\writeup (a) If $L=16$, then $\beta=16^{1/4}=2$, $\log\beta=\log2=0.693$ and $\sqrt L=4$.

(b) The main piece is $(2+\log2)/4=0.673$.

(c) Since $\sqrt L=\beta^2$, the inequality is $(\beta+\log\beta)/\beta^2\le\frac12$. For $\beta=2$ the value is $0.673>\frac12$. For $\beta=3$, $\log3=1.099$ and $(3+1.099)/9=0.455\le\frac12$. So the smallest such integer is $\beta=3$, and $L=\beta^4=81$. At that height $\log\log x=81$, so $\log x=e^{81}$ and $x=\exp(e^{81})$. The $O(1)$ in $\eta_x$ is still present, and even the main piece of this particular estimate stays above $\frac12$ until $L$ is at least $81$. The estimate proves $\eta_x\to0$, and it does so very slowly. That is consistent with the error $O(1/\sqrt{\log\log x})$ quoted after Theorem~\ref{thm:erdoskac}, which at $x=10^7$ is still $0.6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:fourthmodel}
\idea Expand $\mathbb E[S_z^4]$ and group the terms by which primes are equal. A singleton block has mean zero and drops out. Three pairings survive, and so do the terms in which one prime is repeated four times.
\writeup Write $Y_p=X_p-\frac1p$ and $v_p=\mathbb E[Y_p^2]$, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:modelmoments}. Then
\[
\mathbb E[S_z^4]=\sum_{p,q,r,s\le z}\mathbb E[Y_p Y_q Y_r Y_s].
\]
If the equality pattern on $(p,q,r,s)$ has a prime that appears once, independence and $\mathbb E Y_p=0$ kill the term. The surviving patterns are the three pairings of four indices and the pattern in which all four indices agree.

There are three pairings: positions $(1,2)$ with $(3,4)$, positions $(1,3)$ with $(2,4)$, and positions $(1,4)$ with $(2,3)$. For the first, the sum over $p\ne q$ is $\sum_{p\ne q}v_pv_q$. The same holds for the other two. Hence the pairings contribute
\[
3\sum_{p\ne q}v_pv_q=3\Bigl(\sigma_z^4-\sum_pv_p^2\Bigr).
\]
Since $v_p\le\frac1p$, one has $\sum_pv_p^2\le\sum_p\frac1{p^2}<1$, and the subtracted term is $O(1)$.

For the diagonal, $|Y_p|\le1$, so $Y_p^4\le Y_p^2$ and $\mathbb E[Y_p^4]\le v_p$. The diagonal contributes at most $\sum_pv_p=\sigma_z^2$. Therefore
\[
\mathbb E[S_z^4]=3\sigma_z^4+O(\sigma_z^2).
\]
Divide by $\sigma_z^4$. As $z\to\infty$ one has $\sigma_z^2\to\infty$, so the ratio tends to $3$. By the formula in Lemma~\ref{lem:momentslaw}, $m_4=24/(4\cdot2)=3$.
\end{solution}
