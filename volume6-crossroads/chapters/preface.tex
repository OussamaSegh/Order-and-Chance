\chapter*{Preface}
No formula produces the next prime. Twins keep appearing, and so do long gaps. The sequence thins out, and the thinning is uneven. In bulk, three laws hold anyway. The number of primes up to $x$ is asymptotic to $\frac x{\log x}$. The classes modulo $q$ that can contain primes share those primes equally. The number of distinct prime factors of a typical integer follows a Gaussian law. This volume of \emph{Order and Chance} is the last of Part~II. It proves these laws. The proof uses the integers of Volume~2, the characters of Volume~3, the probability of Volume~4 and the analysis of Volume~5 together.

Chapters~1 and~2 are elementary. They prove Mertens's theorems on sums and products over primes, and the average size of arithmetic functions. Chapters~3 and~4 build Dirichlet's $L$-functions and prove that every progression $a+nq$ with $\gcd(a,q)=1$ contains infinitely many primes. Chapter~5 proves the prime number theorem by Newman's short analytic argument. Chapters~6 and~7 study the zeros of the zeta function, give the theorem an error term, and derive Riemann's explicit formula: the primes appear as a smooth main term plus a wave for each zero. Chapters~8 to~12 are sieves, the Erd\H{o}s--Kac theorem, random models of the primes, exponential sums, and a first taste of modular forms. The book stops at the classical explicit formula, Selberg's sieve, the Erd\H{o}s--Kac theorem and the shape of Cramér's model. A stronger zero-free input, the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem, the circle method and a positive proportion of zeros on the critical line are Volume~14.

Throughout, $\log$ is the natural logarithm, and $p$ always denotes a prime. The logarithmic integral is $\mathrm{Li}(x)=\int_2^x\frac{\mathrm dt}{\log t}$.

Every theorem, proposition and lemma is proved. A result quoted only for its interest is a remark, and the remark says where the proof lives. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a good afternoon and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} for a hard argument. Each one has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its chapter. The solution begins with \emph{How to find it}, then the write-up. A ``Pause and think'' box asks you to try a case before the general statement. Two facts are used without proof. Each use is labelled \emph{admitted}, and it names the place where the fact is proved. The functional equation of a primitive Dirichlet $L$-function is proved in Volume~13, Chapter~12. One remark in Chapter~7 uses it, to read the race between the primes $4n+1$ and $4n+3$ as a sum of waves. No theorem depends on that remark. Tonelli's theorem for improper integrals is proved in Volume~7. One solution in Chapter~12 uses it.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random while they obey laws. This volume gives the first precise answer it can give. In bulk the primes obey the prime number theorem, the explicit formula and the Gaussian law of $\omega(n)$. A model that treats them as coin tosses reproduces those laws, until Maier's theorem. That theorem is quoted in Chapter~10 and proved in Volume~15. It is the first place the coin model fails. The next volume begins to pay the debts this one ran up, starting with the measure theory behind ``almost every'' and behind the interchanges of limits that Tonelli's theorem justifies.
