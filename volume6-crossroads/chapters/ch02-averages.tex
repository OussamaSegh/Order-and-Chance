Take $12$. Its divisors are $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, $6$ and $12$, so $\tau(12)=6$. Take $13$. Its divisors are $1$ and $13$, so $\tau(13)=2$. Neighbouring integers need not look alike. Every prime has $\tau(p)=2$. The number $720720=2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13$ has $\tau(720720)=240$.

In this chapter $\tau$, and later $\tau_3$ and $\tau_k$, means that divisor function. Chapter~\ref{ch:modular} uses the same letter for Ramanujan's function. That is a different function.

Euler's function $\varphi$, the sum-of-divisors function $\sigma$, and the squarefree indicator $\mu^2$ jump in the same way. Their averages do not. Volume~2, Theorem~4.6, shows that $\tau(n)$ is $\log n$ on average, by counting lattice points under a hyperbola. Volume~2, Exercise~4.8, and Volume~5, Exercise~10.2, both find the density $\frac6{\pi^2}$: once for coprime pairs, once for squarefree integers.

Divisibility by different primes behaves, on average, like independent coin tosses. Wintner's theorem makes the comparison exact: if $f=\mathbf1*h$ and $\sum|h(d)|/d$ converges, the mean of $f$ is an Euler product, one factor for each prime. Keeping the error in the same argument gives rates for $\varphi$, for $\sigma$, and for the squarefree integers. One more pass of the hyperbola sums $\tau_3$. The size of the error in the lattice-point count has been open since 1849.

\end{think}

\section{Mean values}

We keep the notation of Volume~2, Chapter~4. The Dirichlet convolution is $(f*g)(n)=\sum_{dm=n}f(d)g(m)$. The function $\mathbf1$ is the constant function $1$, $\operatorname{id}(n)=n$, and $\varepsilon$ is the unit: $\varepsilon(1)=1$ and $\varepsilon(n)=0$ for $n>1$. Möbius inversion says $\mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon$. Thus $\tau=\mathbf1*\mathbf1$, $\sigma=\operatorname{id}*\mathbf1$ and $\varphi=\mu*\operatorname{id}$.

An arithmetic function $f$ has \emph{mean value} $m(f)$ when
\[
\frac1x\sum_{n\le x}f(n)\to m(f)\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty.
\]
So $m(f)$ is the limit of the averages, when that limit exists.

Both sides of the next lemma count the pairs $(d,m)$ with $dm\le x$. For a check at $x=10$: $\sum_{n\le10}\tau(n)$ equals $\sum_{d\le10}\lfloor10/d\rfloor$, because $\tau=\mathbf1*\mathbf1$ and each side counts those pairs.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:convsum}
Let $g,h$ be arithmetic functions and $G(y)=\sum_{m\le y}g(m)$. Then for $x\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}(h*g)(n)=\sum_{d\le x}h(d)\,G\Bigl(\frac xd\Bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Both sides equal $\sum h(d)g(m)$ over the pairs $(d,m)$ of positive integers with $dm\le x$. On the left, group the pairs by the product $n=dm$. On the right, group them by $d$. The condition $dm\le x$ is the same as $m\le x/d$.
\end{proof}

With $g=\mathbf1$ one has $G(y)=\lfloor y\rfloor$, and $\lfloor x/d\rfloor=x/d-\{x/d\}$. If $h$ is small, the main term $x\sum_d h(d)/d$ wins, and the fractional parts are the error. Try the smallest $h$ that is not zero.

Let $h(1)=1$, $h(2)=-1$, and $h(n)=0$ for every $n>2$. Set $f=\mathbf1*h$, so $f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}h(d)$. If $n$ is odd, the only divisor in the support of $h$ is $1$, and $f(n)=1$. If $n$ is even, both $1$ and $2$ divide $n$, and $f(n)=1+(-1)=0$. The series $\sum h(d)/d$ has two nonzero terms, $1-1/2=1/2$. The average of $f$ up to $x$ is the proportion of odd integers up to $x$, and that proportion tends to $1/2$. Exercise~\ref{ex:avg6a} writes the Euler product for this $h$ and checks the average up to $10$. The theorem is the same argument for every $h$ with $\sum|h(d)|/d<\infty$.

\begin{theorem}[Wintner, 1943]\label{thm:wintner}
Let $f=\mathbf1*h$, that is $f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}h(d)$, and assume that $\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{|h(d)|}d<\infty$. Then $f$ has a mean value, and
\[
m(f)=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{h(d)}d.
\]
If moreover $h$ is multiplicative, $m(f)=\prod_p\bigl(1+\frac{h(p)}p+\frac{h(p^2)}{p^2}+\cdots\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum}, with $g=\mathbf1$, gives
\[
\sum_{n\le x}f(n)=x\sum_{d\le x}\frac{h(d)}d-\sum_{d\le x}h(d)\Bigl\{\frac xd\Bigr\}.
\]
Let $S=\sum_{d\ge1}h(d)/d$. Absolute convergence gives ordinary convergence, so the tail $\sum_{d>x}h(d)/d$ tends to $0$. The first term on the right is $xS-x\sum_{d>x}h(d)/d$, hence $xS+o(x)$.

The second term has absolute value at most $\sum_{d\le x}|h(d)|$. We claim this sum is $o(x)$. Fix $D$ with $1\le D\le x$. For $d\le x$ one has $1/x\le1/d$, so $|h(d)|/x\le|h(d)|/d$. Split the sum at $D$:
\[
\begin{aligned}\frac1x\sum_{d\le x}|h(d)|&\le\frac1x\sum_{d\le D}|h(d)|+\sum_{D<d\le x}\frac{|h(d)|}d\\&\le\frac1x\sum_{d\le D}|h(d)|+\sum_{d>D}\frac{|h(d)|}d.\end{aligned}
\]
Let $x\to\infty$ with $D$ held fixed. The first piece on the right tends to $0$, so the $\limsup$ of the left side is at most $\sum_{d>D}|h(d)|/d$. That tail tends to $0$ as $D\to\infty$. Hence $\frac1x\sum_{d\le x}|h(d)|\to0$, and $\frac1x\sum_{n\le x}f(n)\to S$.

If $h$ is multiplicative, then so is $h(d)/d$. The sum $S$ is the Euler product in the statement, by Volume~5, Theorem~10.3, applied at $s=1$ to the absolutely convergent series $\sum h(d)/d$.
\end{proof}

The argument needs $\sum|h(d)|/d<\infty$. For $\tau=\mathbf1*\mathbf1$ the choice $h=\mathbf1$ makes $\sum 1/d$ diverge, so the mean of $\tau$ is infinite. The partial sums grow like $x\log x$ (Volume~2, Theorem~4.6). The hyperbola section keeps an error in that formula.

To reach the squarefree integers, write $\mu^2$ itself as a convolution. Check $n=12$ before the lemma. The square divisors of $12$ are $1$ and $4$. The sum $\sum_{k^2\mid12}\mu(k)$ is $\mu(1)+\mu(2)=1-1=0$. And $12$ is not squarefree, so $\mu^2(12)=0$. For $n=6$ the only square divisor is $1$, the sum is $\mu(1)=1$, and $6$ is squarefree.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mu2}
For every $n\ge1$, $\mu^2(n)=\sum_{k^2\mid n}\mu(k)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $n=a^2b$ with $b$ squarefree. There is exactly one such writing: the factor $a^2$ takes, from each prime, the largest even power that divides $n$. Then $k^2\mid n$ if and only if $k\mid a$. Indeed, if $p^j\,\|\,k$ and $p^e\,\|\,n$, the condition $2j\le e$ means $j\le\lfloor e/2\rfloor$, and $\lfloor e/2\rfloor$ is the exponent of $p$ in $a$. The sum is therefore $\sum_{k\mid a}\mu(k)$. By Volume~2, Theorem~4.4, that sum equals $1$ when $a=1$ and equals $0$ otherwise. And $a=1$ exactly when $n$ is squarefree.
\end{proof}

Wintner's theorem applies to three functions at once. The Euler product for the squarefree density starts $\bigl(1-\frac14\bigr)\bigl(1-\frac19\bigr)\bigl(1-\frac1{25}\bigr)=\frac{16}{25}=0.640$, and the full product $\prod_p\bigl(1-1/p^2\bigr)$ equals $6/\pi^2=0.6079\ldots$. Three primes already carry most of the value. The tail is small because $\sum 1/n^2$ converges.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:means}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The squarefree integers have density $m(\mu^2)=\sum_k\frac{\mu(k)}{k^2}=\prod_p\bigl(1-\frac1{p^2}\bigr)=\frac1{\zeta(2)}=\frac6{\pi^2}$.
\item $m\bigl(\frac{\varphi(n)}n\bigr)=\prod_p\bigl(1-\frac1{p^2}\bigr)=\frac6{\pi^2}$.
\item $m\bigl(\frac{\sigma(n)}n\bigr)=\sum_d\frac1{d^2}=\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}6$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(a) By Lemma~\ref{lem:mu2}, $\mu^2=\mathbf1*h$ with $h(k^2)=\mu(k)$ and $h(d)=0$ when $d$ is not a square. Then $\sum_d|h(d)|/d\le\sum_k 1/k^2<\infty$, and $\sum_d h(d)/d=\sum_k\mu(k)/k^2=1/\zeta(2)$ (Volume~5, Corollary~10.4).

(b) From $\varphi=\mu*\operatorname{id}$ one has $\varphi(n)/n=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)/d$. Take $h(d)=\mu(d)/d$. Then $\sum_d h(d)/d=\sum_d\mu(d)/d^2$.

(c) And $\sigma(n)/n=\sum_{d\mid n}(n/d)/n=\sum_{d\mid n}1/d$, so $h(d)=1/d$.

The Euler products are Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}, or the Euler product for $\zeta(2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
Read Corollary~\ref{cor:means}(b) as a coin toss. Choose $n\le x$ at random. For distinct primes $p$, the events ``$p\mid n$'' have probabilities near $1/p$, and those events are nearly independent (Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens}, Exercise~\ref{ex:sieveprob}).

The quotient $\varphi(n)/n=\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)$ contributes the factor $1-1/p$ when $p\mid n$, and the factor $1$ otherwise. The expectation of that factor is
\[
\frac1p\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)+\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)=\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)\Bigl(1+\frac1p\Bigr)=1-\frac1{p^2}.
\]
For independent factors, the expectation of a product is the product of the expectations (Volume~4). The mean would then be $\prod_p(1-1/p^2)$. That is the true mean. Wintner's theorem says the coin-toss picture is exact whenever $h$ is small. Only the small primes contribute to the tail of $\sum|h(d)|/d$, and for those primes the independence becomes exact as $x\to\infty$.

Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens} met a different range of primes. The factor $2e^{-\gamma}$ there comes from the primes up to $\sqrt x$, and the product of the probabilities overshoots the density of the primes. Chapter~\ref{ch:erdoskac} turns the coin-toss picture, the model of Kubilius, into a tool.
\end{beauty}

\section{Error terms}

A mean value says where the averages go. It does not say how fast they get there. The next three theorems need a speed, and the speed comes from three elementary bounds.

Two short counts show the size of the gap before the bounds are written down. The squarefree integers up to $10$ are $1,2,3,5,6,7,10$: seven of them. The main term $(6/\pi^2)\cdot10$ equals $6.079$. The gap is under $1$, while $\sqrt{10}=3.162$, so an error $O(\sqrt x)$ has room. The sum $\sum_{n\le10}\varphi(n)$ equals $32$, and $(3/\pi^2)\cdot10^2$ equals $30.40$. The gap is about $1.6$. The error $O(x\log x)$ proved below is much larger than $1.6$ at $x=10$. The bound is an upper bound, and at this range it is loose.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:elemsums}
For real $x\ge1$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\sum_{n\le x}\frac1n=\log x+\gamma+O\bigl(\frac1x\bigr)$;
\item for $s>1$, $0\le\sum_{n>x}\frac1{n^s}\le\frac{2^{s-1}}{s-1}x^{1-s}$;
\item $\bigl|\sum_{n\le x}n-\frac{x^2}2\bigr|\le\frac x2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) Let $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$. Volume~5, Corollary~11.6, gives $\sum_{n\le N}1/n=\log N+\gamma+O(1/N)$. The gap between $\log x$ and $\log N$ satisfies $0\le\log x-\log N\le(x-N)/N\le1/N$. For $x\ge1$ one has $N\ge x/2$, so $1/N\le2/x$. The two errors together are $O(1/x)$.

(b) Let $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$, so the sum runs over $n\ge N+1$. The map $t\mapsto t^{-s}$ decreases, and on the interval $[n-1,n]$ it is at least $n^{-s}$. Thus $n^{-s}\le\int_{n-1}^n t^{-s}\dd t$, and the sum is at most $\int_N^\infty t^{-s}\dd t=N^{1-s}/(s-1)$. Since $N\ge x/2$ and $1-s<0$, one has $N^{1-s}\le(x/2)^{1-s}$. That is the bound in the statement.

(c) Let $N=\lfloor x\rfloor$. The sum equals $N(N+1)/2$. The inequalities $N\ge x-1$ and $N\le x$ give $(x-1)x\le N(N+1)\le x(x+1)$. Dividing by $2$, the sum sits between $x(x-1)/2$ and $x(x+1)/2$. Each of those is within $x/2$ of $x^2/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:errorterms}
For $x\ge2$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Mertens, 1874) $\displaystyle\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)=\frac3{\pi^2}x^2+O(x\log x)$;
\item $\displaystyle\sum_{n\le x}\sigma(n)=\frac{\pi^2}{12}x^2+O(x\log x)$;
\item the number $Q(x)$ of squarefree integers $n\le x$ is $\frac6{\pi^2}x+O(\sqrt x)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $S(y)=\sum_{m\le y}m=y^2/2+O(y)$ for $y\ge1$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(c).

(a) Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum}, with $\varphi=\mu*\operatorname{id}$, gives
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)&=\sum_{d\le x}\mu(d)S\Bigl(\frac xd\Bigr)\eqbr=\frac{x^2}2\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}+O\Bigl(x\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) makes the error $O(x\log x)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(b) at $s=2$ gives $\sum_{d\le x}\mu(d)/d^2=6/\pi^2+O(1/x)$, since $1/\zeta(2)=6/\pi^2$ and the tail of $\sum 1/d^2$ is $O(1/x)$. Multiplying the tail by $x^2/2$ contributes $O(x)$, which is absorbed in $O(x\log x)$.

(b) Here $\sigma=\mathbf1*\operatorname{id}$, so $\sum_{n\le x}\sigma(n)=\sum_{d\le x}S(x/d)=\frac{x^2}2\sum_{d\le x}1/d^2+O(x\log x)$. And $\sum_{d\le x}1/d^2=\pi^2/6+O(1/x)$.

(c) Lemma~\ref{lem:mu2} and Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum} apply with the function $h$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:means}(a), which vanishes off the squares:
\[
\begin{aligned}Q(x)&=\sum_{k\le\sqrt x}\mu(k)\Bigl\lfloor\frac x{k^2}\Bigr\rfloor\eqbr=x\sum_{k\le\sqrt x}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^2}+O(\sqrt x).\end{aligned}
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(b) gives $\sum_{k\le\sqrt x}\mu(k)/k^2=6/\pi^2+O(1/\sqrt x)$. Multiplying by $x$ turns that tail into $O(\sqrt x)$.
\end{proof}

Coprime pairs reduce to $\varphi$. Figure~\ref{fig:coprime} draws them as the lattice points you can see from the origin.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:coprimepairs}
The number $C(x)$ of pairs $(a,b)\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}^2$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$ satisfies $C(x)=\frac6{\pi^2}x^2+O(x\log x)$ for $x\ge2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $b\ge2$, the integers $a$ with $1\le a<b$ and $\gcd(a,b)=1$ are counted by $\varphi(b)$. The pair $(b,b)$ is not coprime. For $b=1$ the only pair is $(1,1)$, and $\varphi(1)=1$. Symmetry $(a,b)\mapsto(b,a)$ therefore gives $C(x)=2\sum_{b\le x}\varphi(b)-1$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}(a).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The errors in Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms} are larger than the truth, and shrinking them is hard. For $\sum\varphi$, the best bound on record is $O\bigl(x(\log x)^{2/3}(\log\log x)^{4/3}\bigr)$ (Walfisz, 1963). For $Q(x)$, no bound $O(x^\theta)$ with $\theta<1/2$ is known unconditionally. Under the Riemann hypothesis, Montgomery and Vaughan (1981) obtained $O(x^{9/28+\varepsilon})$. In both problems the error is governed by the sums $\sum_{n\le y}\mu(n)$, and those sums are governed by the zeros of $\zeta$ (Chapters~\ref{ch:pnt} and~\ref{ch:explicit}). These bounds are quoted. The proofs are not in this chapter. The sources are listed at the end.
\end{remark}

\section{The hyperbola method}

The sum of $\tau$ is a picture. Each pair of positive integers $(m,n)$ with $mn\le x$ contributes one to $\sum_{k\le x}\tau(k)$, because $k=mn$ has the ordered factorisation $(m,n)$. Figure~\ref{fig:hyperbola30} draws the picture for $x=30$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch02-newhyperbola}
\caption{Lattice points $(m,n)$ of positive integers with $mn\le30$. There are $111$ points, and $\sum_{k\le30}\tau(k)=111$. The coral lines are the split at $\sqrt{30}=5.477\ldots$. The $25$ ringed points lie in the square and would be counted twice if the two sides of the split were added, so the count subtracts them once.}
\label{fig:hyperbola30}
\end{figure}

The coral lines sit at $\sqrt{30}=5.477\ldots$. The positive integers on the near side of either line are $1,2,3,4,5$. They form a $5\times5$ square, and every point of that square satisfies $mn\le25\le30$, so the whole square lies under the hyperbola. That square is the overlap: a point there has both $m\le\sqrt{30}$ and $n\le\sqrt{30}$. Exercise~\ref{ex:avg6c} counts the three pieces. The lemma is the same cut for a general convolution, at a cut $y$ you are free to choose.

\begin{lemma}[Dirichlet's hyperbola method]\label{lem:hyperbola}
Let $f=g*h$, with $G(y)=\sum_{m\le y}g(m)$ and $H(y)=\sum_{m\le y}h(m)$. For $1\le y\le x$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}f(n)&=\sum_{d\le y}g(d)H\Bigl(\frac xd\Bigr)+\sum_{m\le x/y}h(m)G\Bigl(\frac xm\Bigr)\eqbr-G(y)H\Bigl(\frac xy\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The left side sums $g(d)h(m)$ over the pairs with $dm\le x$. A pair cannot have both $d>y$ and $m>x/y$, because then $dm>x$. Every pair therefore has $d\le y$, or $m\le x/y$, or both. The first sum on the right counts the pairs with $d\le y$. The second counts the pairs with $m\le x/y$. The pairs with both are counted twice, and they contribute $G(y)H(x/y)$. Subtract them once.
\end{proof}

For $g=h=\mathbf1$ and $y=\sqrt x$, the two sums are equal and the identity becomes Volume~2's formula
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)=2\sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor-\bigl\lfloor\sqrt x\bigr\rfloor^2.
\]
That count gives
\[
\begin{aligned}D(x)&=\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)\eqbr=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+\Delta(x),\end{aligned}
\]
with $|\Delta(x)|\le C\sqrt x$ for $x\ge1$ and an absolute constant $C$. Exercise~\ref{ex:avg6b} derives the same main terms from Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola}, and shows why the cut $y=\sqrt x$ is the one that balances the error.

The same cut, used twice, reaches the three-fold divisor function. Set $\tau_3=\mathbf1*\mathbf1*\mathbf1=\tau*\mathbf1$. Then $\tau_3(n)$ is the number of ordered triples of positive integers with product $n$. For $n=12$ you can list them. Three of the triples are $(2,2,3)$, $(2,3,2)$ and $(3,2,2)$, and the full list has $18$ triples, so $\tau_3(12)=18$. One more elementary sum is needed before the average of $\tau_3$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:logsum}
$\displaystyle\sum_{m\le x}\frac{\log m}m=\frac12\log^2x+O(1)$ for $x\ge1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:abelint} with $a_m=1$, $A(t)=\lfloor t\rfloor$, $f(t)=(\log t)/t$ and $y=1$. Note that $f(1)=0$, so the $m=1$ term of the sum is $0$ and the lower limit does not contribute. Write $\lfloor t\rfloor=t-\{t\}$ and integrate $t f'(t)$ by parts:
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{m\le x}\frac{\log m}m&=\lfloor x\rfloor f(x)-xf(x)+\int_1^xf(t)\dd t\eqbr+\int_1^x\{t\}f'(t)\dd t.\end{aligned}
\]
The integral of $f$ is $\frac12\log^2 x$. The boundary piece satisfies $|\lfloor x\rfloor f(x)-xf(x)|\le f(x)\le1/e$, since the maximum of $(\log t)/t$ on $[1,\infty[$ is $1/e$. And $|f'(t)|=|1-\log t|/t^2$ is integrable on $[1,\infty[$, so the integral against the bounded function $\{t\}$ stays bounded as $x$ grows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tau3}
For $x\ge2$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau_3(n)=\frac12x\log^2x+(3\gamma-1)x\log x+O(x).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum}, with $\tau_3=\mathbf1*\tau$, gives $\sum_{n\le x}\tau_3(n)=\sum_{m\le x}D(x/m)$. For $m\le x$ one has $x/m\ge1$, so the formula for $D$ applies at $x/m$:
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{m\le x}D\Bigl(\frac xm\Bigr)&=x\sum_{m\le x}\frac{\log x-\log m}m\eqbr+(2\gamma-1)x\sum_{m\le x}\frac1m+\sum_{m\le x}\Delta\Bigl(\frac xm\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) and Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum} handle the first two sums. The first term equals $x\log x(\log x+\gamma)-\frac12 x\log^2 x+O(x)$: the harmonic sum contributes an error $O(\log x)$, and $O(\log x)$ is absorbed in $O(x)$. The second term equals $(2\gamma-1)x\log x+O(x)$. The coefficient of $x\log x$ is $\gamma$ from the first term and $2\gamma-1$ from the second, and $\gamma+(2\gamma-1)=3\gamma-1$.

For the third term, $|\Delta(x/m)|\le C\sqrt{x/m}$, so the sum is at most $C\sqrt x\sum_{m\le x}m^{-1/2}$. The map $t\mapsto t^{-1/2}$ decreases on $[1,\infty[$, and
\[
\sum_{m\le x}m^{-1/2}\le1+\int_1^x t^{-1/2}\dd t=2\sqrt x-1\le2\sqrt x.
\]
The third term is therefore at most $2Cx$. Adding the three terms gives $\frac12 x\log^2 x+(3\gamma-1)x\log x+O(x)$.
\end{proof}

At $x=30$ the sum $\sum_{n\le30}\tau_3(n)$ equals $276$. The two main terms equal $248.18$. The gap is $27.82$, about the size of $x$, so at this range the $O(x)$ is still visible beside the main terms.

The same shape continues for $\tau_k=\mathbf1^{*k}$, the number of ordered factorisations of $n$ into $k$ factors. The error $O(x^{1-1/k}\log^{k-2}x)$ in the next display is quoted, and it is not proved here. Exercise~\ref{ex:tauk} proves the main term, with the weaker error $O_k(x\log^{k-2}x)$.
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau_k(n)=xP_k(\log x)+O(x^{1-1/k}\log^{k-2}x),
\]
where $P_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k-1$ with leading coefficient $1/(k-1)!$. The constant term of $P_3$ is $3\gamma^2-3\gamma-3\gamma_1+1=0.4863\ldots$, where $\gamma_1=-0.0728\ldots$ is the Stieltjes constant, the coefficient after $\gamma$ in the expansion of $\zeta$ at $1$. We quote this value from Titchmarsh, Chapter~XII, and we do not use it. The display is not a theorem of this chapter.

\section{The divisor problem}

The formula for $D(x)$ leaves the error $\Delta(x)$ inside an $O(\sqrt x)$. Figure~\ref{fig:divisorerr} plots $\Delta(x)/x^{1/4}$ on a short range. The curve oscillates and stays far below the size Dirichlet's bound allows.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch02-divisorerr}
\caption{$\Delta(x)/x^{1/4}$, where $\Delta(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)-x\log x-(2\gamma-1)x$, plotted against $\sqrt x$ for $400\le x\le1600$. It oscillates
with a root mean square close to $1$, as Cram\'er's mean-square theorem predicts; Dirichlet's bound only says that the curve stays below a multiple of
$\sqrt x/x^{1/4}=x^{1/4}$, which is between $4.5$ and $6.3$ here.}
\label{fig:divisorerr}
\end{figure}

\begin{remark}
Dirichlet read a memoir on mean values to the Berlin Academy on 9 August 1849. In it he proved $D(x)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt x)$, by the hyperbola count. The question he left open is the true order of $\Delta(x)$. That question is Dirichlet's divisor problem, and it is still open.

Vorono\"\i{} (1903) proved $\Delta(x)=O(x^{1/3}\log x)$, by a finer study of the lattice points that sit near the hyperbola. Huxley (2003) reached the exponent $131/416=0.3149\ldots$. In the other direction, Hardy (1916) proved that $\Delta(x)$ is not $O(x^{1/4})$, and Cram\'er (1922) proved
\[
\begin{gathered}\int_1^X\Delta(x)^2\dd x\sim cX^{3/2},\qq c=\frac1{6\pi^2}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\tau(n)^2}{n^{3/2}}=0.6543\ldots\end{gathered}
\]
so that $\Delta(x)$ is of size $x^{1/4}$ on average. The conjecture is $\Delta(x)=O(x^{1/4+\varepsilon})$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. Gauss's circle problem, Exercise~\ref{ex:circle}, has the same shape and the same exponents. Sierpi\'nski proved the circle error $O(x^{1/3})$ in 1906.

Hou\"el's translation of Dirichlet's memoir is in the Journal de math\'ematiques pures et appliqu\'ees, 2e s\'erie, 1 (1856), 353--370. Vorono\"\i, Hardy, Cram\'er, Huxley and Sierpi\'nski are cited at the end of the chapter. The proofs we do not give are in Titchmarsh, Chapter~XII.
\end{remark}

The average of $\tau$ hides a second surprise, which the think box was asking for. For $n\le10^6$ the mean of the values $\tau(n)$ is $13.970$, and $\log10^6+2\gamma-1$ equals $13.970$ as well. The median of those values is $8$. Only $34.3\%$ of the integers up to $10^6$ have $14$ or more divisors. A few integers with many divisors pull the average up. Chapter~\ref{ch:erdoskac} shows that for almost all $n$, $\tau(n)=(\log n)^{\log2+o(1)}$. At $n=10^6$ that size is $(\log10^6)^{\log2}=6.172$. A typical integer has far fewer divisors than the average one. No integer has very many, compared with its size: that is Exercise~\ref{ex:taubound}.

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner} gives the mean of $f=\mathbf1*h$ whenever $\sum|h(d)|/d$ converges. The mean is $\sum h(d)/d$. When $h$ is multiplicative, the sum is an Euler product, one factor for each prime. Corollary~\ref{cor:means} reads three values off that product: $6/\pi^2$ for the squarefree integers, $6/\pi^2$ for $\varphi(n)/n$, and $\pi^2/6$ for $\sigma(n)/n$.

Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms} puts a rate on the same three sums. The count of squarefree integers has error $O(\sqrt x)$. The sums of $\varphi$ and of $\sigma$ have error $O(x\log x)$. Corollary~\ref{cor:coprimepairs} turns the sum of $\varphi$ into the number of coprime pairs in a square. The counting step under all of this is Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum}. The rates use Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}, and the squarefree indicator is Lemma~\ref{lem:mu2}.

Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola} is the split of a convolution at a cut $y$. Fed with $D(x)$, and with the logarithmic sum in Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum}, it gives Theorem~\ref{thm:tau3}: the sum of $\tau_3$ equals $\frac12 x\log^2 x+(3\gamma-1)x\log x+O(x)$. The sharper error for $\tau_k$ stays a quotation. The true order of $\Delta(x)$ stays open.

Every average in this chapter runs over all integers $n\le x$. The argument cannot separate the integers that end in $7$ from the integers that end in $3$.

That separation is the next question. Volume~2, Chapter~11, introduced Dirichlet characters: functions on the residue classes modulo $q$ that still multiply. Chapter~\ref{ch:characters} builds them for every modulus, and attaches to each character $\chi$ a series $L(s,\chi)$. That series does, for one class modulo $q$, the job $\zeta$ did for all the integers in Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens}.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:sigmaovern}
Prove the two averages with an error, for $x\ge2$:
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\sigma(n)}n=\frac{\pi^2}6x+O(\log x),\qquad\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\varphi(n)}n=\frac6{\pi^2}x+O(\log x).
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:nphi}
Show that $\frac n{\varphi(n)}=\sum_{d\mid n}\frac{\mu^2(d)}{\varphi(d)}$. Deduce that $\frac n{\varphi(n)}$ has mean value
\[
\prod_p\Bigl(1+\frac1{p(p-1)}\Bigr)=\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{\zeta(6)}=1.9435\ldots.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:coprimek}
\emph{Signature exercise.} Let $k\ge2$ and let $C_k(x)$ be the number of $k$-tuples $(a_1,\dots,a_k)\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}^k$ with $\gcd(a_1,\dots,a_k)=1$. Show that $C_k(x)=\sum_{d\le x}\mu(d)\lfloor x/d\rfloor^k$, and that
\[
\frac{C_k(x)}{x^k}=\frac1{\zeta(k)}+\begin{cases}O\bigl(\frac{\log x}x\bigr)&k=2,\\O\bigl(\frac1x\bigr)&k\ge3.\end{cases}
\]
So three random integers are coprime with probability $1/\zeta(3)=0.8319\ldots$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:circle}
\emph{Gauss's circle problem.} Let $N(r)$ be the number of $(a,b)\in\Z^2$ with $a^2+b^2\le r^2$. Place a unit square centred at each of these points, and show that $|N(r)-\pi r^2|\le\pi(\sqrt2\,r+1/2)$ for $r\ge1$. Deduce that $\sum_{0\le n\le x}r_2(n)=\pi x+O(\sqrt x)$, where $r_2(n)$ is the number of $(a,b)\in\Z^2$ with $a^2+b^2=n$. In particular $r_2$ has mean value $\pi$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:twoomega}
Show that $\sum_{n\le x}2^{\omega(n)}=\frac6{\pi^2}x\log x+O(x)$ for $x\ge2$. Here $\omega(n)$ is the number of distinct prime factors of $n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:taubound}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\tau(n)\le\sqrt{3n}$ for every $n\ge1$, with equality only for $n=12$.
\item Show that for every $\varepsilon\in\left]0,1\right]$ and every $n\ge1$, $\tau(n)\le(\varepsilon\log2)^{-2^{1/\varepsilon}}n^\varepsilon$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:tauk}
Let $\tau_k=\mathbf1^{*k}$ be the number of ordered factorisations $n=a_1\cdots a_k$. Show by induction on $k\ge1$ that for $x\ge3$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau_k(n)=\frac1{(k-1)!}x\log^{k-1}x+O_k(x\log^{k-2}x),
\]
where for $k=1$ the error term is read as $O(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:phiovern}
\emph{The law of $\varphi(n)/n$.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that for every integer $k\ge1$,
\[
\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}\Bigl(\frac{\varphi(n)}n\Bigr)^k\longrightarrow\prod_p\Bigl(1-\frac1p+\frac1p\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)^k\Bigr).
\]
\item Deduce that $\frac1N\sum_{n\le N}g\bigl(\varphi(n)/n\bigr)$ has a limit for every continuous $g:[0,1]\to\R$. The values $\varphi(n)/n$ have a limiting distribution.
\item Show that at least half of all integers, asymptotically, have $\varphi(n)\le n/2$, although the mean of $\varphi(n)/n$ is $6/\pi^2>1/2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:visible}
\emph{Visible points.} A point $(a,b)\in\Z^2\setminus\{0\}$ is \emph{visible} from the origin when no other lattice point lies on the segment joining them.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $(a,b)$ is visible if and only if $\gcd(a,b)=1$.
\item Let $V(R)$ be the number of visible points in the disc of radius $R$. Show that $V(R)=\frac6\pi R^2+O(R\log R)$.
\item Show that the visible points are equidistributed in angle: the proportion of them in a sector of angle $\theta$ tends to $\theta/(2\pi)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:farey}
\emph{Farey fractions.} The Farey sequence $\mathcal F_N$ lists the fractions $h/k$ in $[0,1]$ in lowest terms with $1\le k\le N$, in increasing order.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $|\mathcal F_N|=1+\sum_{k\le N}\varphi(k)=\frac3{\pi^2}N^2+O(N\log N)$.
\item Show that two consecutive fractions $h/k<h'/k'$ of $\mathcal F_N$ satisfy $h'k-hk'=1$ and $k+k'>N$.
\item Deduce that $\sum 1/(kk')=1$, the sum running over consecutive pairs, and that the gaps of $\mathcal F_N$ all lie between $1/(N(N-1))$ and $1/N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:orderedfact}
\emph{Ordered factorisations (Kalm\'ar, 1931).} Let $H(1)=1$ and, for $n\ge2$, let $H(n)$ be the number of ways of writing $n$ as an ordered product of factors $>1$ (so $H(12)=8$). Let $\rho>1$ be the solution of $\zeta(\rho)=2$; numerically $\rho=1.7286\ldots$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\sum_n H(n)n^{-s}=1/(2-\zeta(s))$ for real $s>\rho$.
\item Compute $H(p^a)$ for a prime $p$, and $H(p_1\cdots p_k)$ for distinct primes, for $k\le4$.
\item Show that $H(n)\le n^\rho$ for every $n\ge1$.
\item Show that $\sum_{n\le x}H(n)\le x^\sigma/(2-\zeta(\sigma))$ for every $\sigma>\rho$ and $x\ge1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:fracmean}
\emph{Remainders on average.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\le x}\bigl\{x/n\bigr\}=(1-\gamma)x+O(\sqrt x)$ for $x\ge1$.
\item For an integer $N\ge2$, let $N\bmod n$ be the remainder when $N$ is divided by $n$. Show that
\[
\sum_{n=1}^N(N\bmod n)=\Bigl(1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}\Bigr)N^2+O(N\log N).
\]
\item Explain both constants as integrals: $1-\gamma=\int_0^1\{1/t\}\dd t$ and $2-\pi^2/6=\int_0^1 2t\{1/t\}\dd t$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:radical}
\emph{The average radical.} The radical $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ is the product of the distinct primes dividing $n$, so $\operatorname{rad}(72)=6$. It is the quantity in the $abc$ conjecture of Masser and Oesterl\'e. Let $C=\prod_p\bigl(1-1/(p(p+1))\bigr)=0.70444\ldots$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Write $\operatorname{rad}(n)/n=\sum_{d\mid n}g(d)$ and compute $g$. Show that $g(d)=0$ unless $d$ is \emph{squarefull} (every prime factor of $d$ appears at least squared), and that $|g(d)|\le1$.
\item Show that the number of squarefull $d\le y$ is at most $\zeta(3/2)\sqrt y$.
\item Deduce that $\sum_{n\le x}\operatorname{rad}(n)=\frac C2 x^2+O(x^{3/2})$, and that the mean value of $\operatorname{rad}(n)/n$ is $C$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:avg6a}
Let $h(1)=1$, $h(2)=-1$, and $h(n)=0$ for every $n>2$. Set $f=\mathbf1*h$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $f(n)=1$ when $n$ is odd and $f(n)=0$ when $n$ is even.
\item Check that $\sum|h(d)|/d$ converges, and deduce from Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner} that $m(f)=1/2$. Write the mean as an Euler product.
\item Check by hand that the average of $f(n)$ for $n\le10$ equals $1/2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:avg6b}
Let $x\ge1$ and let $y$ be real with $1\le y\le x$. Using Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola} with $g=h=\mathbf1$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a), show that
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O\Bigl(y+\frac xy\Bigr),
\]
with an implied constant that does not depend on $x$ or on $y$. Deduce that the cut $y=\sqrt x$ gives
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt x).
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:avg6c}
\emph{A short count.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Compute $\sum_{n\le24}\tau(n)$ by listing $\tau(n)$, or by reading the bars in Figure~\ref{fig:taubars}. Compare the sum with $24\log24+(2\gamma-1)\cdot24$, and compare the absolute difference with $\sqrt{24}$.
\item For $x=30$ and $y=\sqrt{30}$, count the lattice points $(m,n)$ in the three pieces of Figure~\ref{fig:hyperbola30}: both coordinates at most $\sqrt{30}$; $m\le\sqrt{30}$ but $n>\sqrt{30}$; $n\le\sqrt{30}$ but $m>\sqrt{30}$. Check that Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola} at this $x$ and $y$ recovers $\sum_{k\le30}\tau(k)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:avg6d}
Show that for $x\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\tau(n)}n=\frac12\log^2 x+2\gamma\log x+O(1).
\]
The tools are the double count $\tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1$, Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) and Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:sigmaovern}
\idea Each quotient is $\mathbf1*h$, with $h(d)=1/d$ or $h(d)=\mu(d)/d$. Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum} produces the main term $x\sum h(d)/d$ and an error $\sum_{d\le x}|h(d)|$, and here that error is only $O(\log x)$.
\writeup By Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum},
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\sigma(n)}n=\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor=x\sum_{d\le x}\frac1{d^2}-\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d\Bigl\{\frac xd\Bigr\}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums} makes the first term $\frac{\pi^2}6 x+O(1)$. The second term lies between $0$ and $\log x+1$. The same computation with $\mu(d)/d$ in place of $1/d$ gives
\[
x\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}+O(\log x)=\frac6{\pi^2}x+O(\log x).
\]
These are Corollary~\ref{cor:means}(b) and~(c), now with error terms.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:nphi}
\idea The quotient $n/\varphi(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}p/(p-1)$ depends only on the distinct primes dividing $n$. A sum over squarefree divisors has that shape. The function $h=\mu^2/\varphi$ is small enough for Wintner.
\writeup Both sides are multiplicative functions of $n$. The right side is $\mathbf1*h$ with $h=\mu^2/\varphi$, and $h$ is multiplicative (Volume~2, Proposition~4.3). At $n=p^k$ with $k\ge1$, the left side equals $p^k/(p^{k-1}(p-1))=p/(p-1)$. The right side equals $1+1/(p-1)=p/(p-1)$, because $\mu^2(p^j)=0$ for $j\ge2$. The two sides agree at prime powers, hence for every $n$.

Next, $h(d)/d\ge0$ is multiplicative, with $h(p)/p=1/(p(p-1))$ and $h(p^j)=0$ for $j\ge2$. Every $d\le x$ with $h(d)\ne0$ is a squarefree product of primes at most $x$, so
\[
\sum_{d\le x}\frac{h(d)}d\le\prod_{p\le x}\Bigl(1+\frac1{p(p-1)}\Bigr)\le\exp\sum_p\frac1{p(p-1)}<\infty.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner} applies and gives the mean $\prod_p\bigl(1+1/(p(p-1))\bigr)$. The Euler factor of $\zeta(2)\zeta(3)/\zeta(6)$ at $p$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}\frac{1-p^{-6}}{(1-p^{-2})(1-p^{-3})}&=\frac{1+p^{-3}}{1-p^{-2}}\eqbr=\frac{(p+1)(p^2-p+1)}{p(p-1)(p+1)}\eqbr=1+\frac1{p(p-1)}.\end{aligned}
\]
The product equals $1.94359\ldots$. The average of $n/\varphi(n)$ over $n\le10^6$ equals $1.94358\ldots$. So $n/\varphi(n)$ is about $2$ on average, while Corollary~\ref{cor:means}(b) gave $0.608$ for $\varphi(n)/n$. The mean of a reciprocal and the reciprocal of the mean are different numbers.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:coprimek}
\idea As in Volume~2, Exercise~4.8, the condition $\gcd=1$ becomes a sum of $\mu(d)$ over common divisors $d$. The $k$-tuples of multiples of $d$ are then easy to count. The new point is the error, which is smaller for $k\ge3$ because $\sum d^{1-k}$ converges.
\writeup Since $\sum_{d\mid g}\mu(d)$ equals $1$ when $g=1$ and equals $0$ otherwise,
\[
C_k(x)=\sum_{a_1,\dots,a_k}\sum_{d\mid a_1,\dots,d\mid a_k}\mu(d)=\sum_{d\le x}\mu(d)\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor^k.
\]
Each $a_i$ runs through the $\lfloor x/d\rfloor$ multiples of $d$ up to $x$. For $y\ge1$ the mean value theorem gives $0\le y^k-\lfloor y\rfloor^k\le ky^{k-1}(y-\lfloor y\rfloor)\le ky^{k-1}$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}C_k(x)&=x^k\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^k}+O\Bigl(kx^{k-1}\sum_{d\le x}\frac1{d^{k-1}}\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(b) and Volume~5, Corollary~10.4, give $\sum_{d\le x}\mu(d)/d^k=1/\zeta(k)+O(x^{1-k})$, and that tail contributes $O(x)$ after multiplication by $x^k$. The sum $\sum_{d\le x}d^{1-k}$ is $O(\log x)$ for $k=2$, and it is at most $\zeta(2)$ for $k\ge3$. Dividing by $x^k$ gives the claim. For $x=1000$ the proportions are $0.60838$, $0.83205$ and $0.92399$ for $k=2,3,4$, against $0.60793$, $0.83191$ and $0.92394$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:circle}
\idea Area counts lattice points up to a boundary layer. Each lattice point owns a unit square. The squares of the points in the disc fill the disc up to a strip of width $\sqrt2/2$, the half-diagonal of a square, along the circle.
\writeup Let $K(a,b)=\bigl[a-\frac12,a+\frac12\bigr]\times\bigl[b-\frac12,b+\frac12\bigr]$. These squares have area $1$ and disjoint interiors, and every point of $K(a,b)$ lies within $\sqrt2/2$ of $(a,b)$. If $a^2+b^2\le r^2$, then $K(a,b)$ lies in the disc of radius $r+\sqrt2/2$, so
\[
N(r)\le\pi\Bigl(r+\frac{\sqrt2}2\Bigr)^2=\pi r^2+\pi\Bigl(\sqrt2\,r+\frac12\Bigr).
\]
Conversely, take $|z|\le r-\sqrt2/2$ and let $(a,b)$ be a lattice point nearest to $z$. Then $z\in K(a,b)$ and $|(a,b)|\le|z|+\sqrt2/2\le r$, so the $N(r)$ squares cover the disc of radius $r-\sqrt2/2$. Thus $N(r)\ge\pi(r-\sqrt2/2)^2\ge\pi r^2-\pi\sqrt2\,r$. For the sum, $\sum_{0\le n\le x}r_2(n)=N(\sqrt x)$. For $r=1000$ the count gives $N(r)-\pi r^2=-43.65$, far inside the bound $4444$. The true error is believed to be $O(r^{1/2+\varepsilon})$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twoomega}
\idea The value $2^{\omega(n)}$ counts the squarefree divisors of $n$, so the sum counts pairs (squarefree $d$, multiple of $d$). The squarefree integers have density $6/\pi^2$, and summing $x/d$ over them produces $(6/\pi^2)x\log x$.
\writeup By Volume~2, Exercise~4.2, $2^{\omega(n)}=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu^2(d)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum} gives
\[
\sum_{n\le x}2^{\omega(n)}=\sum_{d\le x}\mu^2(d)\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor=x\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\mu^2(d)}d+O(x).
\]
Abel summation (Lemma~\ref{lem:abelint} with $a_d=\mu^2(d)$, $A=Q$ and $f(t)=1/t$) gives
\[
\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\mu^2(d)}d=\frac{Q(x)}x+\int_1^x\frac{Q(t)}{t^2}\dd t.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}(c) holds for $1\le t<2$ as well, because $Q(t)=1$ there. So $Q(t)=(6/\pi^2)t+O(\sqrt t)$, the integral equals $(6/\pi^2)\log x+O\bigl(\int_1^\infty t^{-3/2}\dd t\bigr)=(6/\pi^2)\log x+O(1)$, and $Q(x)/x\le1$. Multiplying by $x$ gives the result. The constant in the next term can be named: it is $Cx$ with
\[
C=\frac6{\pi^2}\Bigl(2\gamma-1-2\frac{\zeta'(2)}{\zeta(2)}\Bigr)=0.7869\ldots.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:taubound}
\idea Both $\tau$ and $n^\varepsilon$ are multiplicative, so compare them prime by prime. A large prime contributes a factor at most $1$. Only finitely many small primes can make $\tau(n)/n^\varepsilon$ large, and each of them contributes a bounded factor.
\writeup (a) One has $\tau(n)/\sqrt n=\prod_{p^k\|n}(k+1)/p^{k/2}$. For $p\ge5$ and $k\ge1$, $p^{k/2}\ge5^{k/2}>k+1$ (true for $k=1$, and the left side grows faster), so these factors are $<1$. For $p=3$ the factors are $2/\sqrt3=1.155$, then $3/3=1$, then less than $1$. For $p=2$ they are $2/\sqrt2=1.414$, $3/2=1.5$, $4/(2\sqrt2)=1.414$, and then they decrease. So
\[
\frac{\tau(n)}{\sqrt n}\le\frac32\cdot\frac2{\sqrt3}=\sqrt3,
\]
with equality only when $2^2\|n$, $3\|n$, and no other prime divides $n$. That is $n=12$, where $\tau(12)=6=\sqrt{36}$.

(b) Write $\tau(n)/n^\varepsilon=\prod_{p^k\|n}(k+1)/p^{k\varepsilon}$. If $p\ge2^{1/\varepsilon}$, then $p^{k\varepsilon}\ge2^k\ge k+1$, so the factor is at most $1$. If $p<2^{1/\varepsilon}$, then
\[
p^{k\varepsilon}\ge2^{k\varepsilon}=e^{k\varepsilon\log2}\ge1+k\varepsilon\log2\ge(k+1)\varepsilon\log2,
\]
using $\varepsilon\log2\le1$. The factor is at most $1/(\varepsilon\log2)$. There are fewer than $2^{1/\varepsilon}$ such primes, and $1/(\varepsilon\log2)>1$, so $\tau(n)/n^\varepsilon\le(\varepsilon\log2)^{-2^{1/\varepsilon}}$. The constant is enormous for small $\varepsilon$. The point is that $\tau(n)=O_\varepsilon(n^\varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. Optimising $\varepsilon$ as a function of $n$ gives Wigert's theorem $\tau(n)\le2^{(1+o(1))\log n/\log\log n}$, attained along products of the first primes, such as $720720=2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:tauk}
\idea Each convolution with $\mathbf1$ is a sum $\sum_{m\le x}T(x/m)$. Summing $(x/m)\log^j(x/m)$ over $m$ behaves like the integral $x\int_1^x\log^j(x/t)\,dt/t=x\log^{j+1}x/(j+1)$, which raises the power of the logarithm by one and divides by $j+1$.
\writeup Let $T_k(y)=\sum_{n\le y}\tau_k(n)$, $j=k-1$ and $L=\log x$. We prove a slightly sharper statement: for all $y\ge1$,
\[
T_k(y)=\frac{y\log^j y}{j!}+E_k(y),
\]
with $|E_k(y)|\le C_k y(1+\log y)^{j-1}$ if $k\ge2$, and $|E_1(y)|\le1$. For $y\ge3$ this is the claim, since $1+\log y\le2\log y$. For $k=1$, $T_1(y)=\lfloor y\rfloor$.

Suppose the statement holds for $k$. Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum} gives $T_{k+1}(x)=\sum_{m\le x}T_k(x/m)$. The errors add up to at most $\sum_{m\le x}C_k(x/m)(1+L)^{j-1}\le C_k x(1+L)^j$ (and for $k=1$, at most $x$), which has the shape required at step $k+1$.

For the main term, let $\phi(t)=t^{-1}\log^j(x/t)$ on $[1,x]$. For $j=0$ this is $1/t$, which decreases. For $j\ge1$,
\[
\phi'(t)=-\frac1{t^2}\log^{j-1}\frac xt\Bigl(\log\frac xt+j\Bigr)\le0,
\]
so $\phi$ decreases as well. For a decreasing function, $\int_1^{\lfloor x\rfloor+1}\phi\le\sum_{m\le x}\phi(m)\le\phi(1)+\int_1^x\phi$, with $\phi$ extended by $0$ past $x$. The sum is therefore $\int_1^x\phi+O(\phi(1))=L^{j+1}/(j+1)+O(L^j)$. Multiplying by $x/j!$ gives
\[
T_{k+1}(x)=\frac{x L^k}{k!}+O(x L^{k-1}),
\]
which is the claim for $k+1$. For $k=3$ the main term is the main term of Theorem~\ref{thm:tau3}. The error here is the weaker one, $O(x\log x)$, which is what this exercise proves. The exponent $1-1/k$ in the quoted display after that theorem is not proved in this chapter.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:phiovern}
\idea The power $(\varphi(n)/n)^k$ is multiplicative, and it equals $\sum_{d\mid n}h(d)$ for a function $h$ that vanishes off squarefree integers and is $O(k/p)$ at primes. Wintner's theorem gives the mean. Polynomials then approximate every continuous function on $[0,1]$.

\writeup (a) Let $f_k(n)=(\varphi(n)/n)^k=\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)^k$, which is multiplicative. Define $h$ to be multiplicative by $h(p)=(1-1/p)^k-1$ and $h(p^j)=0$ for $j\ge2$. Then $\sum_{d\mid n}h(d)=\prod_{p\mid n}(1+h(p))=f_k(n)$. Since $|h(p)|\le k/p$,
\[
\sum_d\frac{|h(d)|}d\le\prod_p\Bigl(1+\frac k{p^2}\Bigr)<\infty.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner} gives $f_k$ the mean $\sum_d h(d)/d=\prod_p\bigl(1+h(p)/p\bigr)$, which is the product in the statement.

(b) By (a) and linearity, the averages of $g(\varphi(n)/n)$ converge for every polynomial $g$. For $g$ continuous and $\varepsilon>0$, Weierstrass's theorem (Volume~4, Theorem~4.6, via Bernstein polynomials) supplies a polynomial $q$ with $|g-q|\le\varepsilon$ on $[0,1]$. The averages of $g$ and of $q$ differ by at most $\varepsilon$, and the averages of $q$ form a Cauchy sequence. The averages of $g$ do too, so they converge.

(c) Every even $n$ has $\varphi(n)/n\le1/2$, and the even integers have density $1/2$. For $k=1$ the product in (a) is $\prod_p(1-1/p^2)=6/\pi^2$. The mean exceeds $1/2$ because the odd integers mostly have $\varphi(n)/n$ close to $1$.

\tools Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}; Volume~4, Theorem~4.6; Volume~2, Chapter~4.
\leads Schoenberg (1928) proved that the limiting distribution function exists and is continuous, and Erd\H os (1939) proved that it is purely singular: it has no density, although it increases strictly on $[0,1]$. Among the $n\le10^6$, the proportion with $\varphi(n)\le n/2$ is $0.511$. That proportion counts the even integers, together with a few odd integers that have many small prime factors, such as $105=3\cdot5\cdot7$, for which $\varphi(105)/105=48/105$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:visible}
\idea Each nonzero lattice point is $d$ times a visible point, with $d$ the gcd of its coordinates. The count of all lattice points is a sum of rescaled counts of visible points, and Möbius inversion returns the visible count.

\writeup (a) If $d=\gcd(a,b)>1$, the point $(a/d,b/d)$ lies strictly between $0$ and $(a,b)$. Conversely, a lattice point on the segment is $t(a,b)$ with $0<t<1$. Write $t=u/v$ in lowest terms, so $\gcd(u,v)=1$ and $v\mid ua$, $v\mid ub$. Euclid's lemma, from Bézout's identity in Volume~2, Chapter~1, gives $v\mid a$ and $v\mid b$, hence $v\mid\gcd(a,b)$, and $v>1$.

(b) Let $N(R)$ be the number of lattice points in the closed disc of radius $R$. Exercise~\ref{ex:circle} gives $|N(R)-\pi R^2|\le C(R+1)$. Writing each nonzero point as $d\cdot v$ with $v$ visible, one has $N(R)-1=\sum_{d\ge1}V(R/d)$, and $V(R/d)=0$ for $d>R$. Möbius inversion, on a finite sum, gives
\[
V(R)=\sum_{d\le R}\mu(d)\bigl(N(R/d)-1\bigr)=\pi R^2\sum_{d\le R}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}+O\Bigl(\sum_{d\le R}\Bigl(\frac Rd+1\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
The error is $O(R\log R)$, and $\sum_{d\le R}\mu(d)/d^2=6/\pi^2+O(1/R)$. So $V(R)=(6/\pi)R^2+O(R\log R)$.

(c) Repeat (b) with a sector $\Sigma$ of angle $\theta$ and radius $R$ in place of the disc. The unit squares centred at the lattice points of $\Sigma$ cover $\Sigma$ up to a band of width $\sqrt2/2$ along its boundary, and they lie in $\Sigma$ enlarged by such a band. The boundary has length $2R+\theta R$, so the number of lattice points in $\Sigma$ is $(\theta/2)R^2+O(R+1)$. The Möbius step is unchanged, because $v\mapsto dv$ preserves the sector, and it produces $(6/\pi^2)(\theta/2)R^2+O(R\log R)$ visible points. Divide by $V(R)$.

\tools Exercise~\ref{ex:circle}; Volume~2, Chapter~4.
\leads The visible points have holes of every size. By the Chinese remainder theorem there are $k\times k$ blocks of invisible points for every $k$. In dimension $n$ the density of visible points is $1/\zeta(n)$. The gaps between the angles of visible points have a limiting distribution, computed by Boca, Cobeli and Zaharescu (2000). The angles are equidistributed, and the gaps are not exponential.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:farey}
\idea Neighbours in the Farey sequence span a parallelogram of area $1$. Bézout produces the candidate neighbour, and a short inequality shows that nothing fits between them.

\writeup (a) The fractions of $\mathcal F_N$ with denominator $k$ are $h/k$ with $0\le h\le k$ and $\gcd(h,k)=1$. There are $\varphi(k)$ of them for $k\ge2$, and two of them, $0/1$ and $1/1$, for $k=1$. So $|\mathcal F_N|=1+\sum_{k\le N}\varphi(k)$. Mertens's estimate $\sum_{k\le N}\varphi(k)=(3/\pi^2)N^2+O(N\log N)$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}(a), finishes the count.

(b) Let $h/k<1$ lie in $\mathcal F_N$. Since $\gcd(h,k)=1$, the solutions $(x,y)$ of $kx-hy=1$ form one family $y\equiv y_0\pmod k$, so there is one with $N-k<y\le N$. Then $\gcd(x,y)=1$ and $x/y=h/k+1/(ky)$. Also $0<x/y\le1$, because $h\le k-1$ gives $x/y\le(k-1)/k+1/(ky)\le1$. So $x/y\in\mathcal F_N$. Suppose some $h'/k'\in\mathcal F_N$ lies strictly between them. Positive fractions of these denominators differ by at least the reciprocal of the product of the denominators, so $x/y-h'/k'\ge1/(yk')$ and $h'/k'-h/k\ge1/(kk')$. Adding these gaps,
\[
\frac1{ky}=\frac xy-\frac hk\ge\frac1{yk'}+\frac1{kk'}=\frac{k+y}{kyk'}>\frac N{kyk'}\ge\frac1{ky},
\]
because $k+y>N\ge k'$. This is absurd. So $x/y$ is the successor of $h/k$: one has $h'=x$, $k'=y$, $h'k-hk'=1$, and $k+k'>N$.

(c) The gap between consecutive fractions is $h'/k'-h/k=1/(kk')$, and the gaps add up to $1$. Since $k,k'\le N$ and $k\ne k'$ unless $k=k'=1$ (two consecutive fractions with the same denominator $k\ge2$ would differ by $1/k^2$, while $h'k-hk'=1$ forces the difference of the fractions to have numerator $1$), each gap for $N\ge2$ satisfies $1/(kk')\ge1/(N(N-1))$. And $k+k'\ge N+1$ gives $1/(kk')\le1/(k(N+1-k))\le1/N$.

\tools Volume~2, Chapter~1; Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}(a).
\leads Franel (1924) and Landau showed that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to a strong spacing statement for the Farey sequence: $\sum_j\bigl|f_j-j/n\bigr|=O(N^{1/2+\varepsilon})$, where $f_1<\dots<f_n$ is $\mathcal F_N$. The mediant $(h+h')/(k+k')$ of two neighbours is the first fraction that appears between them. Inserting mediants again and again, with no bound on the denominator, builds the Stern--Brocot tree of all positive fractions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:orderedfact}
\idea An ordered factorisation into $k$ factors greater than $1$ is counted by the Dirichlet series $(\zeta(s)-1)^k$. The geometric series in $\zeta-1$ converges when $\zeta(s)<2$. The number $\rho$ where $\zeta(\rho)=2$ is the natural growth exponent, and a direct induction gives $H(n)\le n^\rho$.

\writeup (a) For real $s>1$, $\zeta(s)-1=\sum_{n\ge2}n^{-s}$, so $(\zeta(s)-1)^k=\sum_n H_k(n)n^{-s}$, where $H_k(n)$ counts the ordered factorisations of $n$ into exactly $k$ factors $>1$. Since $H=\sum_k H_k$ (with $H_0$ the indicator of $n=1$) and every term is nonnegative,
\[
\sum_n H(n)n^{-s}=\sum_k\bigl(\zeta(s)-1\bigr)^k,
\]
which sums to $1/(2-\zeta(s))$ when $\zeta(s)<2$, that is, when $s>\rho$. The function $\zeta$ decreases on $]1,\infty[$.

(b) An ordered factorisation of $p^a$ is a composition $a=a_1+\dots+a_k$ with each $a_i\ge1$, and there are $2^{a-1}$ compositions of $a$. For $n=p_1\cdots p_k$, an ordered factorisation is an ordered partition of the set of $k$ primes into nonempty blocks. The counts are $1,3,13,75$ for $k=1,2,3,4$. For $k=3$ the breakdown is one factorisation with one factor, six with two factors, and six with three factors.

(c) The first factor of an ordered factorisation of $n\ge2$ is a divisor $e>1$, and the rest is an ordered factorisation of $n/e$, so $H(n)=\sum_{d\mid n,\,d<n}H(d)$. Assume $H(d)\le d^\rho$ for every $d<n$, the case $H(1)=1$ being the start. Then
\[
H(n)\le\sum_{d\mid n,\,d<n}d^\rho=n^\rho\sum_{e\mid n,\,e>1}e^{-\rho}<n^\rho\sum_{e\ge2}e^{-\rho}=n^\rho\bigl(\zeta(\rho)-1\bigr)=n^\rho.
\]

(d) For $\sigma>\rho$ and $n\le x$ one has $1\le(x/n)^\sigma$, so $\sum_{n\le x}H(n)\le x^\sigma\sum_n H(n)n^{-\sigma}=x^\sigma/(2-\zeta(\sigma))$.

\tools Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum}; Volume~5, Chapter~10.
\leads Kalm\'ar proved in 1931 that $\sum_{n\le x}H(n)\sim -x^\rho/(\rho\zeta'(\rho))=0.3181\ldots x^\rho$, by a Tauberian theorem at the simple pole of $1/(2-\zeta(s))$ at $s=\rho$. We quote the asymptotic and do not prove it. The bound (c) is due to Chor, Lemke and Mador (2000). The exponent $\rho$ cannot be lowered: Hille showed in 1936 that $H(n)\ge n^{\rho-\varepsilon}$ for infinitely many $n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:fracmean}
\idea One has $\{x/n\}=x/n-\lfloor x/n\rfloor$. The sum of $x/n$ is the harmonic sum, and the sum of the floors is the divisor sum. Their difference keeps the constant terms.

\writeup (a) Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) gives $\sum_{n\le x}x/n=x\log x+\gamma x+O(1)$. Counting the pairs $(n,m)$ with $nm\le x$ in two ways, $\sum_{n\le x}\lfloor x/n\rfloor=\sum_{m\le x}\tau(m)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt x)$, by the formula for $D(x)$ that follows from Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola}. Subtract:
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\Bigl\{\frac xn\Bigr\}=(1-\gamma)x+O(\sqrt x).
\]

(b) The remainder is $N\bmod n=N-n\lfloor N/n\rfloor$, and
\[
\sum_{n\le N}n\Bigl\lfloor\frac Nn\Bigr\rfloor=\sum_{nm\le N}n=\sum_{k\le N}\sigma(k)=\frac{\pi^2}{12}N^2+O(N\log N)
\]
by Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}(b). The sum of the remainders is therefore $N^2-(\pi^2/12)N^2+O(N\log N)$.

(c) With $t=n/x$, the average $\frac1x\sum_{n\le x}\{x/n\}$ is a Riemann sum for $\{1/t\}$ on $]0,1]$. The change of variables $u=1/t$ turns the integral into $\int_1^\infty\{u\}u^{-2}\dd u=\sum_{n\ge1}\bigl(\log((n+1)/n)-1/(n+1)\bigr)$. The partial sums $\log(N+1)-\sum_{2\le m\le N+1}1/m$ tend to $1-\gamma$. Likewise $\frac2{N^2}\sum_n n\{N/n\}$ is a Riemann sum for $2t\{1/t\}$, and by (b) it tends to $2-\pi^2/6$. The same integral can be computed directly:
\[
2\int_1^\infty\{u\}u^{-3}\dd u=\sum_{n\ge1}\Bigl(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{(n+1)^2}\Bigr)=1-\bigl(\zeta(2)-1\bigr)=2-\zeta(2).
\]
These Riemann-sum readings are a guide. The function $\{1/t\}$ jumps infinitely often near $0$, and the error terms come from (a) and (b).

\tools Lemmas~\ref{lem:elemsums} and~\ref{lem:hyperbola}, Theorem~\ref{thm:errorterms}.
\leads The error in (a) is $-\Delta(x)+O(1)$, where $\Delta$ is the error in Dirichlet's divisor problem. The question of how regular the remainders of $x$ modulo $n$ are is the divisor problem itself, open since 1849.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:radical}
\idea The radical forgets repeated prime factors. The quotient $\operatorname{rad}(n)/n$ differs from $1$ only when $n$ has a repeated factor. Squarefull integers are rare, about $\sqrt x$ of them up to $x$, so the convolution of Lemma~\ref{lem:convsum} saves a power of $x$.

\writeup (a) The function $f(n)=\operatorname{rad}(n)/n$ is multiplicative, with $f(p^k)=p^{1-k}$. Möbius inversion supplies a multiplicative $g=f*\mu$ with $g(p^k)=f(p^k)-f(p^{k-1})$:
\[
g(p)=1-1=0,\qq g(p^k)=p^{1-k}-p^{2-k}=-(p-1)p^{1-k}\quad(k\ge2).
\]
So $g(d)=0$ unless $d$ is squarefull. When $d$ is squarefull, $|g(d)|=\prod_{p^k\parallel d}(p-1)p^{1-k}\le1$, because $p-1\le p^{k-1}$ for $k\ge2$.

(b) Every squarefull $d$ can be written $d=a^2b^3$: place one or three copies of each prime of odd exponent into $b^3$. For fixed $b$ there are at most $\sqrt{y/b^3}$ choices of $a$, so the count is at most $\sqrt y\sum_b b^{-3/2}=\zeta(3/2)\sqrt y$.

(c) Since $\operatorname{rad}(n)=n\sum_{d\mid n}g(d)$, the change of variables $n=dm$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{n\le x}\operatorname{rad}(n)&=\sum_{d\le x}g(d)\,d\sum_{m\le x/d}m=\sum_{d\le x}g(d)\,d\Bigl(\frac{x^2}{2d^2}+O\Bigl(\frac xd\Bigr)\Bigr)\\&=\frac{x^2}2\sum_{d\le x}\frac{g(d)}d+O\Bigl(x\sum_{d\le x}|g(d)|\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
By (a) and (b), $\sum_{d\le x}|g(d)|\le\zeta(3/2)\sqrt x$. Partial summation gives $\sum_{d>x}|g(d)|/d=O(x^{-1/2})$, the sum running over squarefull $d$. Hence $\sum_{n\le x}\operatorname{rad}(n)=(x^2/2)G+O(x^{3/2})$ with $G=\sum_d g(d)/d$. The series converges absolutely, so it is an Euler product:
\[
G=\prod_p\Bigl(1-(p-1)\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{p^{1-k}}{p^k}\Bigr)=\prod_p\Bigl(1-\frac{(p-1)p^{-3}}{1-p^{-2}}\Bigr)=\prod_p\Bigl(1-\frac1{p(p+1)}\Bigr)=C.
\]
The mean of $f=\mathbf1*g$ is $\sum_d g(d)/d=C$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}. For $x=10^7$, one has $\frac2{x^2}\sum_{n\le x}\operatorname{rad}(n)=0.704446$, against $C=0.704442$.

\tools Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}; Euler products (Volume~5, Theorem~10.3).
\leads The mean of $\operatorname{rad}(n)/n$ and the mean of $\operatorname{rad}(n)$ relative to $x/2$ are the same constant $C$. The weight $n$ only rescales the sum by $1/2$. A typical radical is a fixed fraction of $n$, and the integers with a small radical, the squarefull ones, are rare. The $abc$ conjecture asserts that when $a+b=c$ with $a$ and $b$ coprime, the radical of $abc$ can be much smaller than $c$ only in a weak sense: $c\le K_\varepsilon\operatorname{rad}(abc)^{1+\varepsilon}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:avg6a}
\idea This is the case of Wintner's theorem in which $h$ is supported on $\{1,2\}$. The function $f$ is the indicator of the odd integers, and the mean $1/2$ is their density.
\writeup (a) By definition $f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}h(d)$. If $n$ is odd, then $2\nmid n$, so the only nonzero term is $h(1)=1$. If $n$ is even, then both $1$ and $2$ divide $n$, and $f(n)=h(1)+h(2)=1-1=0$.

(b) The series $\sum|h(d)|/d$ equals $1+1/2$, a finite sum, so it converges. Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner} gives $m(f)=\sum h(d)/d=1-1/2=1/2$. The function $h$ is multiplicative (it vanishes at every product of two coprime integers greater than $1$, and the values at prime powers multiply). The Euler product is
\[
\Bigl(1+\frac{h(2)}2\Bigr)\prod_{p>2}\bigl(1+0+0+\cdots\bigr)=1-\frac12=\frac12,
\]
since $h(2^k)=0$ for $k\ge2$ and $h(p^k)=0$ for every odd prime.

(c) The odd integers up to $10$ are $1,3,5,7,9$: five of them. The average is $5/10=1/2$.
\tools Theorem~\ref{thm:wintner}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:avg6b}
\idea Cut the pairs $dm\le x$ at $d=y$. Each floor is a quotient minus a fractional part, the harmonic sums are Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a), and the product of the two floors is $x$ plus an error of size $y+x/y$. The two error sizes are equal when $y=\sqrt x$.
\writeup Let $z=x/y$, so $z\ge1$, and let $D(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola} with $g=h=\mathbf1$ gives
\[
D(x)=\sum_{d\le y}\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor+\sum_{m\le z}\Bigl\lfloor\frac xm\Bigr\rfloor-\lfloor y\rfloor\lfloor z\rfloor.
\]
Write $\lfloor x/d\rfloor=x/d-\{x/d\}$. There are $\lfloor y\rfloor\le y$ terms in the first sum, and each fractional part is $<1$, so
\[
\sum_{d\le y}\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor=x\sum_{d\le y}\frac1d+\theta_1,\qquad |\theta_1|\le y.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) gives $\sum_{d\le y}1/d=\log y+\gamma+O(1/y)$, with an absolute implied constant. Multiplying by $x$, and keeping the fractional-part error $\theta_1=O(y)$, the first sum equals $x\log y+\gamma x+O(y+x/y)$. The same argument with $z$ in place of $y$ gives
\[
\sum_{m\le z}\Bigl\lfloor\frac xm\Bigr\rfloor=x\log z+\gamma x+O(y)+O\Bigl(\frac xy\Bigr),
\]
because the fractional-part error is $O(z)=O(x/y)$ and the harmonic error $x\cdot O(1/z)$ equals $O(y)$.

For the product, $|\lfloor y\rfloor-y|<1$ and $|\lfloor z\rfloor-z|<1$, so
\[
\bigl|\lfloor y\rfloor\lfloor z\rfloor-yz\bigr|<z+y+1.
\]
Since $y\ge1$ and $z\ge1$, one has $y+z+1\le2(y+z)$, and $yz=x$. Thus $\lfloor y\rfloor\lfloor z\rfloor=x+O(y+x/y)$.

Put the three pieces together:
\begin{align*}
D(x)&=(x\log y+\gamma x)+(x\log z+\gamma x)-x+O\Bigl(y+\frac xy\Bigr)\\
&=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O\Bigl(y+\frac xy\Bigr).
\end{align*}
The implied constant is absolute, because every $O$ above came from Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) or from a count of fractional parts. At the cut $y=\sqrt x$ one has $y+x/y=2\sqrt x$, and the error is $O(\sqrt x)$.
\tools Lemmas~\ref{lem:hyperbola} and~\ref{lem:elemsums}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:avg6c}
\idea The bars are the values $\tau(n)$. The lattice points are the ordered factorisations. Both counts are finite, so they can be added by hand and set against the formulae in the chapter.
\writeup (a) The values $\tau(n)$ for $n=1,\ldots,24$ are
\[
1,2,2,3,2,4,2,4,3,4,2,6,2,4,4,5,2,6,2,6,4,4,2,8.
\]
Their sum is $84$. With $\log24=3.17805383$ and $\gamma=0.57721566$,
\begin{align*}
24\log24&=76.27329,\\
(2\gamma-1)\cdot24&=3.70635,\\
24\log24+(2\gamma-1)\cdot24&=79.980.
\end{align*}
The difference is $\Delta(24)=84-79.980=4.020$. And $\sqrt{24}=4.899$. On this one value the error is smaller than $\sqrt x$. Volume~2, Theorem~4.6, only promises a constant times $\sqrt x$. Dirichlet's divisor problem asks whether a smaller power of $x$ will do.

(b) Since $\sqrt{30}=5.477\ldots$, the positive integers at most $\sqrt{30}$ are $1,2,3,4,5$. Every pair from this set has product at most $25\le30$, so the overlap contains $5\times5=25$ points. The points with $m\le5$ and $n\ge6$ are: $25$ points for $m=1$ ($n$ from $6$ to $30$), $10$ for $m=2$ ($n$ from $6$ to $15$), $5$ for $m=3$ ($n$ from $6$ to $10$), $2$ for $m=4$ ($n=6,7$), and $1$ for $m=5$ ($n=6$). That is $43$ points. The region $mn\le30$ is symmetric in $m$ and $n$, and the cut $y=\sqrt{30}$ equals $x/y$, so the third piece also contains $43$ points. The total is $25+43+43=111$.

Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola} adds the points with $m\le\sqrt{30}$ to the points with $n\le\sqrt{30}$ and subtracts the overlap: $(25+43)+(25+43)-25=111$. Each point $(m,n)$ is one ordered factorisation of $k=mn$, so the same count is $\sum_{k\le30}\tau(k)=111$.
\tools Figure~\ref{fig:taubars}, Figure~\ref{fig:hyperbola30}, Lemma~\ref{lem:hyperbola}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:avg6d}
\idea Divide the divisor sum by $n$ and write $n=dm$. The inner sum over $m$ is a harmonic number, of size $\log(x/d)$. Summing $(\log(x/d))/d$ produces a square of a logarithm, and Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum} supplies the cross term.
\writeup Since $\tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1$,
\[
\sum_{n\le x}\frac{\tau(n)}n=\sum_{dm\le x}\frac1{dm}=\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d\sum_{m\le x/d}\frac1m.
\]
For each $d\le x$ one has $x/d\ge1$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) gives $\sum_{m\le x/d}1/m=\log(x/d)+\gamma+O(d/x)$, with an absolute implied constant. The error contributes
\[
\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d\cdot O\Bigl(\frac dx\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\frac1x\sum_{d\le x}1\Bigr)=O(1).
\]
The main piece is
\[
\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\log(x/d)+\gamma}d=(\log x+\gamma)\sum_{d\le x}\frac1d-\sum_{d\le x}\frac{\log d}d.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:elemsums}(a) turns the first sum into $\log x+\gamma+O(1/x)$, and
\[
(\log x+\gamma)\bigl(\log x+\gamma+O(1/x)\bigr)=(\log x+\gamma)^2+O\Bigl(\frac{\log x}x\Bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum} turns the second sum into $\frac12\log^2 x+O(1)$. Expanding the square,
\[
(\log x+\gamma)^2-\frac12\log^2 x=\frac12\log^2 x+2\gamma\log x+\gamma^2.
\]
The constant $\gamma^2$ and the $O(1)$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum} are together $O(1)$. This is the claim.

At $x=20$ the sum equals $8.559$, and $\frac12\log^2 20+2\gamma\log20$ equals $7.946$. The difference is $0.614$. Part of it is the constant $\gamma^2=0.333$ coming from the square, and the rest sits in the $O(1)$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:logsum}. The lemmas name no sharper constant, so the error term stops at $O(1)$.
\tools Lemmas~\ref{lem:elemsums} and~\ref{lem:logsum}.
\end{solution}
