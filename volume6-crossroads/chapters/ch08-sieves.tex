\chapter{Sieves}\label{ch:sieves}

\begin{center}\itshape
The method for the production of these is called by Eratosthenes the sieve, because we take the odd numbers mingled together and indiscriminate
and out of them, by this method of production, separate, as by a kind of instrument or sieve, the prime and incomposite numbers by themselves.\\
\upshape --- Nicomachus of Gerasa, \emph{Introduction to Arithmetic} (about 100 AD), Book~I, Chapter~13
\end{center}

Around 240 BC, Eratosthenes of Cyrene described how to list the primes. Write the integers in a row. Cross out the multiples of $2$, then of $3$, then of $5$, and so on. What survives every prime up to $\sqrt x$ is $1$ together with the primes up to $x$. The description is an algorithm. It does not by itself give a formula for $\pi(x)$.

In 1808 Legendre turned the algorithm into an exact formula, an inclusion--exclusion over the primes up to $z$. The formula looks as if it should give the prime number theorem at once. It has $2^{\pi(z)}$ terms. When $z$ is as large as $\sqrt x$, those terms are too numerous for the error to be useful, and the main term is the wrong size.

For another century, sieve upper bounds stayed out of reach. Between 1915 and 1919 Viggo Brun stopped the inclusion--exclusion early, with an error he could bound. He applied the truncated sum to twin primes, pairs such as $(11,13)$ and $(101,103)$. He did not prove that infinitely many such pairs exist. He proved that they are sparse enough for a series of reciprocals to converge:

\portrait{brun}{Viggo Brun (1885--1978). Photograph by Peder O.~Aune, 25 May 1927. NTNU University Library. CC BY-SA 4.0.}
\portrait{selberg}{Atle Selberg (1917--2007). Photograph by Konrad Jacobs. Mathematisches Forschungsinstitut Oberwolfach. CC BY-SA 2.0 de.}
\[
\begin{aligned}\Bigl(\frac13+\frac15\Bigr)&+\Bigl(\frac15+\frac17\Bigr)\eqbr+\Bigl(\frac1{11}+\frac1{13}\Bigr)+\cdots=B_2<\infty,\end{aligned}
\]
while $\sum 1/p$ diverges (Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens}). A prime that sits in two twin pairs, such as $5$, appears twice. The value $B_2=1.9021605\ldots$ has a place in the history of computation as well. In 1994 Thomas Nicely, evaluating the constant, found that a new Pentium processor divided incorrectly. Intel's announced charge for replacing the processors was about $475$ million dollars. The note is in the sources.

In 1947 Atle Selberg replaced Brun's bookkeeping by one idea. An upper bound for a count is a sum of squares, and the best sum of squares is the minimum of a quadratic form. Six years earlier, Yuri Linnik had found a sieve of a different shape, the large sieve, which removes many residue classes for each prime. Its modern form is an inequality for trigonometric polynomials. This chapter proves both sieves, at the strength written below, and gives the first applications: primes in short intervals, twin primes, and sets that look like the squares modulo every small prime. The same weights are the starting point of the proofs of Zhang and of Maynard (2013) that infinitely many pairs of primes lie at a bounded distance.

\begin{think}
If divisibility by different primes were independent, the proportion of $n\le x$ with no prime factor $\le z$ would be $\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)$. For $z=\sqrt x$, the survivors are exactly $1$ and the primes in $]\sqrt x,x]$. Compare that count with the product, using Mertens's theorem and the prime number theorem. Which of the two is larger, and by what factor?
\end{think}

\section{Legendre's sieve, and why it fails}

Let $P(z)=\prod_{p\le z}p$, and let $\Phi(x,z)$ be the number of integers $1\le n\le x$ with no prime factor $\le z$. Since $\sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)$ equals $1$ if $m=1$ and equals $0$ otherwise (Volume~2, Theorem~4.4),
\[
\Phi(x,z)=\sum_{n\le x}\sum_{d\mid(n,P(z))}\mu(d)=\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\mu(d)\Bigl\lfloor\frac xd\Bigr\rfloor.
\]
This is Legendre's identity: inclusion--exclusion over the primes $\le z$. Writing $\lfloor x/d\rfloor=x/d-\{x/d\}$ separates a main term from an error.

\begin{proposition}[Legendre, 1808]\label{prop:legendre}
For $x\ge1$ and $z\ge2$,
\[
\Phi(x,z)=x\prod_{p\le z}\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)+E,\qquad|E|\le2^{\pi(z)}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding the product gives $\sum_{d\mid P(z)}\mu(d)\,x/d=x\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)$. Each of the $2^{\pi(z)}$ fractional parts lies in $[0,1[$.
\end{proof}

The main term is useful when the error is smaller than it is, so $z$ has to be small. The inequality $2^{\pi(z)}\le\sqrt x$ forces $z$ to be about $\log x$. With $z=\log x$, Mertens's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens3}) gives $\pi(x)\le z+\Phi(x,z)=O(x/\log\log x)$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:legendrepi}). The bound is true, and it is weak.

On a small range the formula can be exact. Take $x=30$ and $z=5$. The integers up to $30$ with no prime factor $\le5$ are
\[
1,7,11,13,17,19,23,29,
\]
eight numbers. The main term is $30\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac45=8$. The error bound is $2^{\pi(5)}=8$. Here the error happens to be $0$, and the allowance of $8$ is as large as the count itself.

At $z=\sqrt x$ the main term has the wrong constant. There $\Phi(x,\sqrt x)=\pi(x)-\pi(\sqrt x)+1\sim x/\log x$, while
\[
x\prod_{p\le\sqrt x}\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)\sim\frac{2e^{-\gamma}x}{\log x}=\frac{1.1229\,x}{\log x}.
\]
The difference is about $12.3\%$ of $x/\log x$, which is $10.9\%$ of the main term (Exercise~\ref{ex:legendrefail}). The fractional parts, each in $[0,1[$, add up to a positive proportion of the main term. The same factor already appeared in Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens}. A sieve that only knows how many integers lie in each residue class modulo small numbers counts a product of two large primes in the same way that it counts a prime. Selberg called this the parity problem: sieve methods of this shape do not separate integers with an even number of prime factors from integers with an odd number. That is why the sieves below give upper bounds of the right order, and lower bounds for numbers with a bounded number of prime factors.

\section{Selberg's sieve}

\subsection*{The setting}

Let $\mathcal A$ be a finite list of positive integers, repetitions allowed. Let $\mathcal P$ be a set of primes, let $z\ge2$, and let $P(z)=\prod_{p\in\mathcal P,\,p\le z}p$. The count to bound from above is
\[
S(\mathcal A,\mathcal P,z)=\#\{a\in\mathcal A:\gcd(a,P(z))=1\}.
\]
For $d\mid P(z)$ let $\mathcal A_d$ be the sublist of multiples of $d$. The only information used is that $|\mathcal A_d|$ is roughly a proportion $g(d)$ of a total $X$:
\[
|\mathcal A_d|=g(d)X+r_d\qquad(d\mid P(z)),
\]
where $g$ is multiplicative, $0<g(p)<1$ for $p\in\mathcal P$, and $r_d$ is a remainder. For the integers in an interval of length $X$, one has $g(d)=1/d$ and $|r_d|\le1$. Let
\[
h(p)=\frac{g(p)}{1-g(p)},\qquad G(y)=\sum_{\substack{d\le y\\d\mid P(z)}}\mu^2(d)h(d),
\]
with $h$ extended multiplicatively to squarefree $d$.

\begin{theorem}[Selberg, 1947]\label{thm:selberg}
For every $y\ge1$,
\[
S(\mathcal A,\mathcal P,z)\le\frac X{G(y)}+\sum_{\substack{d_1,d_2\le y\\d_1,d_2\mid P(z)}}|r_{[d_1,d_2]}|,
\]
where $[d_1,d_2]$ is the least common multiple.
\end{theorem}

The idea is one line. If $\lambda_1=1$, then for every positive integer $m$ the square $\bigl(\sum_{d\mid m}\lambda_d\bigr)^2$ equals $1$ when $m=1$, and is at least $0$ otherwise. Any real weights with $\lambda_1=1$ give an upper bound. The weights are then chosen to make the bound as small as the quadratic form allows. Brun had to arrange signs. Selberg's squares are non-negative for every $m$.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Squares.} Let $(\lambda_d)$ be real numbers indexed by the $d\mid P(z)$ with $d\le y$, and let $\lambda_1=1$. For $a\in\mathcal A$, the square $\bigl(\sum_{d\mid(a,P(z))}\lambda_d\bigr)^2$ equals $1$ if $\gcd(a,P(z))=1$, and is at least $0$ otherwise. So
\[
\begin{aligned}S&\le\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}\Bigl(\sum_{d\mid(a,P(z))}\lambda_d\Bigr)^2=\sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}|\mathcal A_{[d_1,d_2]}|\\&=XQ+\sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}r_{[d_1,d_2]},\end{aligned}
\]
since $d_1\mid a$ and $d_2\mid a$ together mean $[d_1,d_2]\mid a$, and where $Q=\sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}g([d_1,d_2])$. The quadratic form $Q$ is minimised first. The minimiser satisfies $|\lambda_d|\le1$, which bounds the second sum by the remainder in the theorem.

\emph{Diagonalising $Q$.} For squarefree $d_1,d_2$, multiplicativity gives $g([d_1,d_2])=g(d_1)g(d_2)/g((d_1,d_2))$. Since $1/g(p)=1+1/h(p)$, for squarefree $m$ one has
\[
\frac1{g(m)}=\prod_{p\mid m}\Bigl(1+\frac1{h(p)}\Bigr)=\sum_{e\mid m}\frac1{h(e)}.
\]
Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}Q&=\sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}g(d_1)\lambda_{d_2}g(d_2)\eqbr\quad\times\sum_{e\mid(d_1,d_2)}\frac1{h(e)}=\sum_e\frac{u_e^2}{h(e)},\end{aligned}
\]
where $u_e=\sum_{e\mid d}\lambda_dg(d)$, and $e$ and $d$ run over the divisors of $P(z)$ up to $y$. The change of variables is invertible: $\lambda_dg(d)=\sum_{d\mid e}\mu(e/d)u_e$. Substituting the definition of $u_e$ and using that $\sum_{d\mid e\mid k}\mu(e/d)$ equals $1$ if $k=d$ and equals $0$ otherwise checks the inversion. The constraint $\lambda_1=1$ becomes $\sum_e\mu(e)u_e=1$.

\emph{The minimum.} By Cauchy--Schwarz,
\[
\begin{aligned}1=\Bigl(\sum_e\mu(e)u_e\Bigr)^2&\le\Bigl(\sum_e\frac{u_e^2}{h(e)}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_eh(e)\Bigr)\eqbr=Q\cdot G(y),\end{aligned}
\]
with equality when $u_e=\mu(e)h(e)/G(y)$. With this choice, $Q=1/G(y)$.

\emph{The weights are at most $1$.} Write $e=dm$ in the inversion formula. Then $m$ is squarefree and coprime to $d$, $\mu(m)\mu(dm)=\mu(d)\mu(m)^2=\mu(d)$, and $h(dm)=h(d)h(m)$. So
\[
\lambda_d=\frac{\mu(d)h(d)}{g(d)G(y)}\sum_{\substack{m\le y/d,\ m\mid P(z)\\(m,d)=1}}\mu^2(m)h(m).
\]
Now $h(d)/g(d)=\prod_{p\mid d}1/(1-g(p))=\prod_{p\mid d}(1+h(p))=\sum_{k\mid d}h(k)$. Each product $km$ with $k\mid d$ and $m$ in the sum is a squarefree divisor of $P(z)$, at most $y$, and different pairs give different products, because $k=(km,d)$. So
\[
\Bigl(\sum_{k\mid d}h(k)\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_m\mu^2(m)h(m)\Bigr)\le G(y),
\]
which is $|\lambda_d|\le1$. Then $|\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}r_{[d_1,d_2]}|\le|r_{[d_1,d_2]}|$.
\end{proof}

To use the theorem one needs a lower bound for $G(y)$. For the integers the bound is a comparison with the harmonic series.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Glog}
For $y\ge1$, $\displaystyle\sum_{d\le y}\frac{\mu^2(d)}{\varphi(d)}\ge\log y$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For squarefree $d$,
\[
\frac1{\varphi(d)}=\frac1d\prod_{p\mid d}\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)^{-1}=\frac1d\prod_{p\mid d}\sum_{k\ge0}p^{-k}=\sum_{\operatorname{rad}(m)=d}\frac1m,
\]
where $\operatorname{rad}(m)$ is the product of the distinct primes dividing $m$. Every $m\le y$ has $\operatorname{rad}(m)\le m\le y$, so the sum is at least $\sum_{m\le y}1/m\ge\log y$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Primes in short intervals}

The first application counts primes in an interval of length $N$, wherever the interval sits. The prime number theorem says nothing about $[x,x+N]$ when $N$ is much smaller than $x$. The sieve does not use the starting point, only the length.

\begin{theorem}[Brun--Titchmarsh]\label{thm:bt}
Uniformly for $x\ge0$ and $N\ge3$,
\[
\pi(x+N)-\pi(x)\le\frac{2N}{\log N}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal A$ be the integers in $]x,x+N]$, let $\mathcal P$ be all primes, and let $X=N$. For $d\ge1$, one has $|\mathcal A_d|=N/d+r_d$ with $|r_d|\le1$, so $g(d)=1/d$, $h(p)=1/(p-1)$ and $h(d)=1/\varphi(d)$. Take $y=z=\sqrt N/\log N$ for $N$ large; small $N$ go into the $O$. Every squarefree $d\le y$ divides $P(z)$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:Glog} gives $G(y)\ge\log y=\frac12\log N-\log\log N$. The remainder is at most the number of pairs $(d_1,d_2)$, which is at most $y^2=N/\log^2N$. A prime in $]x,x+N]$ is either $\le z$ or coprime to $P(z)$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}\pi(x+N)-\pi(x)&\le z+\frac N{\frac12\log N-\log\log N}\eqbr+\frac N{\log^2N},\end{aligned}
\]
which is the claim.
\end{proof}

By the prime number theorem, an interval $]x,x+N]$ with $N$ about $x$ contains about $N/\log x$ primes. Theorem~\ref{thm:bt} bounds every interval of length $N$ by $2N/\log N$, wherever the interval sits. If the interval begins at $x=N^k$, then $\log x=k\log N$, so the ratio of $2N/\log N$ to the prime-number-theorem scale $N/\log x$ is exactly $2k$, before the secondary factor $1+O(\log\log N/\log N)$. For $k=2$ the interval has length $\sqrt x$ and begins near $x$, and the ratio is $4$. The constant $2$ in the short-interval bound is this leading factor. It is a different $2$ from the one in the next paragraph.

The same sieve, run on a progression, gives
\[
\pi(x;q,a)\le\frac{2x}{\varphi(q)\log(x/q)}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)
\]
when $\gcd(a,q)=1$ and $x/q\to\infty$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:btap}). The leading factor $2$ in that progression bound is the constant tied to Siegel zeros. Lowering it to $2-\varepsilon$, uniformly for moduli $q$ up to a small power of $x$, would rule out the Siegel zeros mentioned in Chapter~\ref{ch:characters}. The argument is quoted from Friedlander and Iwaniec, in the sources, and is not proved here. The parity problem is why the sieve, by itself, does not remove that $\varepsilon$.

\begin{remark}[Montgomery and Vaughan, 1973; quoted]
Montgomery and Vaughan removed the secondary term: $\pi(x+N)-\pi(x)\le 2N/\log N$ for all $N\ge2$. Nothing later uses this sharper form.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Twin primes}

Let $\pi_2(x)$ be the number of primes $p\le x$ such that $p+2$ is also prime. To catch both members of the pair, sieve the products $n(n+2)$. Both factors are prime and larger than $z$ precisely when $n>z$ and $n(n+2)$ has no prime factor $\le z$.

\begin{theorem}[Brun, 1919]\label{thm:twins}
$\pi_2(x)=O\bigl(x/\log^2x\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The data.} Let $\mathcal A=\{n(n+2):1\le n\le x\}$ and let $\mathcal P$ be all primes. For squarefree $d$, the condition $d\mid n(n+2)$ is a condition on $n$ modulo $d$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, the number $\rho(d)$ of solutions modulo $d$ is multiplicative, with $\rho(2)=1$ and $\rho(p)=2$ for odd $p$ (the classes $0$ and $-2$). Counting $n\le x$ in $\rho(d)$ classes modulo $d$ gives $|\mathcal A_d|=\rho(d)x/d+r_d$ with $|r_d|\le\rho(d)$. So $g(d)=\rho(d)/d$, $h(2)=1$, and $h(p)=2/(p-2)$ for odd $p$.

\emph{The main term.} For odd $p$, $h(p)=\frac2p\cdot\frac1{1-2/p}=\sum_{k\ge1}2^k/p^k$. So for odd squarefree $d$,
\[
h(d)=\sum_{\operatorname{rad}(m)=d}\frac{2^{\Omega(m)}}m\ge\sum_{\operatorname{rad}(m)=d}\frac{\tau(m)}m,
\]
where $\Omega(m)$ counts prime factors with multiplicity and $\tau(m)\le2^{\Omega(m)}$. As in Lemma~\ref{lem:Glog}, taking $z\ge y$,
\[
\begin{aligned}G(y)&\ge\sum_{\substack{m\le y\\m\text{ odd}}}\frac{\tau(m)}m=\sum_{\substack{ab\le y\\a,b\text{ odd}}}\frac1{ab}\ge\Bigl(\sum_{\substack{a\le\sqrt y\\a\text{ odd}}}\frac1a\Bigr)^2.\end{aligned}
\]
The harmonic sum up to $Y$ is $\log Y+O(1)$, and the even terms are $\frac12\log(Y/2)+O(1)$, so the odd sum is $\frac12\log Y+O(1)$. With $Y=\sqrt y$ this is at least $\frac12\log(\sqrt y/2)$ for large $y$, and $G(y)\ge\frac1{16}\log^2y\,(1+o(1))$.

\emph{The remainder.} One has $\rho([d_1,d_2])\le2^{\omega([d_1,d_2])}\le2^{\omega(d_1)}2^{\omega(d_2)}\le\tau(d_1)\tau(d_2)$, so the remainder is at most $\bigl(\sum_{d\le y}\tau(d)\bigr)^2$. And $\sum_{d\le y}\tau(d)=\sum_{a\le y}\lfloor y/a\rfloor\le y(1+\log y)$.

\emph{The choice.} Take $y=z=\sqrt x/\log^3x$. Then the remainder is at most $x/\log^4x$ for large $x$, one has $\log y\sim\frac12\log x$, and
\[
\begin{aligned}\pi_2(x)&\le z+\frac{16x}{\log^2y}(1+o(1))+\frac x{\log^4x}\eqbr=O\Bigl(\frac x{\log^2x}\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
Since $\log^2y\sim\frac14\log^2x$, the coefficient $16$ in front of $x/\log^2y$ becomes $64$ in front of $x/\log^2x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Brun, 1919]\label{cor:brun}
The series $\sum 1/p$, summed over the primes $p$ such that $p+2$ is prime, converges.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:abelint} with $a_p=1$ for these primes and $f(t)=1/t$,
\[
\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\frac{\pi_2(x)}x+\int_2^x\frac{\pi_2(t)}{t^2}\dd t,
\]
and $\int_2^\infty\mathrm dt/(t\log^2t)<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Brun, 1919]
Viggo Brun published the convergence in the \emph{Bulletin des Sciences Math\'ematiques}, volume 43 (1919), pages 100--104 and 124--128. The title says the series is ``convergente ou finie'': convergent, or a finite sum. The argument gives an upper bound of the shape in Theorem~\ref{thm:twins}. A finite set of twin primes would have satisfied the title just as well. Corollary~\ref{cor:brun} is that upper bound turned into a convergent series. The paper does not decide whether the set of twin primes is infinite.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[how many twins?]
The constant in the proof above is $64$, up to the factor $1+o(1)$. A more careful evaluation of $G(y)$ gives Selberg's bound $\pi_2(x)\le(16C_2+o(1))x/\log^2x$, where
\[
C_2=\prod_{p>2}\Bigl(1-\frac1{(p-1)^2}\Bigr)=0.6601618\ldots
\]
is the twin prime constant. Hardy and Littlewood conjectured in 1923 that $\pi_2(x)\sim2C_2\,x/\log^2x$, so the bound $16C_2$ is $8$ times the conjectured main term. Sifting the shifted primes $p+2$ in place of the integers $n(n+2)$, and using the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem (Remark~\ref{rem:bv}) to control the remainders, Bombieri and Davenport (1966) brought the factor down to $4$. The best published factor of this shape is about $3.4$ (Wu, 2004). The conjecture is the same probabilistic count as the think box, corrected one prime at a time. Figure~\ref{fig:twins} and Exercise~\ref{ex:twinheuristic} explain the constant, and Chapter~\ref{ch:random} returns to it. A matching lower bound is blocked by the parity problem. The best result in that direction is Chen's theorem (1973), quoted here: there are infinitely many primes $p$ such that $p+2$ is either prime or a product of two primes.
\end{remark}

\begin{beauty}
Zhang (2013) proved that infinitely many pairs of primes differ by at most $70\,000\,000$. Months later Maynard, and independently Tao, found a simpler method that is stronger: for every $m$, some bounded interval contains $m$ primes infinitely often. The tool is a Selberg sieve in several variables. The weights $\lambda_{d_1,\dots,d_k}$ are chosen, by the same Cauchy--Schwarz step as above, to minimise a quadratic form. A collaborative project (Polymath, 2014) brought the gap down to $246$. The twin prime conjecture is the gap $2$.
\end{beauty}

\section{The large sieve}

Selberg's sieve removes one or two residue classes for each prime. Sometimes the set avoids many more classes. The squares, for example, meet only about half of the classes modulo an odd prime. Linnik found in 1941 that a set with that many missing classes has to be small. The proof used here, in the form shaped by R\'enyi, Roth, Bombieri, Davenport, Halberstam, Gallagher and Montgomery, is an $L^2$ inequality. A set that is lopsided modulo $p$ has a large Fourier coefficient at the fractions $a/p$, and a trigonometric polynomial cannot be large at too many well-spaced points.

Write $e(\alpha)=e^{2\pi i\alpha}$, and write $\|\alpha\|$ for the distance from $\alpha$ to the nearest integer. Points $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_R$ are \emph{$\delta$-spaced} if $\|\alpha_r-\alpha_s\|\ge\delta$ for $r\ne s$.

\begin{lemma}[Gallagher]\label{lem:gallagher}
Let $\delta>0$ and let $f$ be of class $C^1$ on $I=[\alpha-\delta/2,\alpha+\delta/2]$, with complex values. Then
\[
|f(\alpha)|\le\frac1\delta\int_I|f|+\frac12\int_I|f'|.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $\beta\in I$, one has $f(\alpha)=f(\beta)-\int_\alpha^\beta f'$. Average over $\beta\in I$:
\[
|f(\alpha)|\le\frac1\delta\int_I|f|+\frac1\delta\int_I\int_{[\alpha,\beta]}|f'(t)|\dd t\dd\beta.
\]
In the double integral, a point $t>\alpha$ is counted for the $\beta\in[t,\alpha+\delta/2]$, a set of length at most $\delta/2$, and the same holds for $t<\alpha$. So the double integral is at most $(\delta/2)\int_I|f'|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the large sieve inequality]\label{thm:largesieve}
Let $a_n$ be complex numbers for the integers $M<n\le M+N$, let $S(\alpha)=\sum_na_ne(n\alpha)$, and let $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_R$ be $\delta$-spaced with $0<\delta\le\frac12$. Then
\[
\sum_{r=1}^R|S(\alpha_r)|^2\le\Bigl(\pi N+\frac1\delta\Bigr)\sum_n|a_n|^2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $c=M+(N+1)/2$, the centre of the range, and set $T(\alpha)=e(-c\alpha)S(\alpha)=\sum_na_ne((n-c)\alpha)$. Then $|T|=|S|$ and $|n-c|\le(N-1)/2$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:gallagher} to $f=T^2$, so that $f'=2TT'$, at each $\alpha_r$. The intervals of length $\delta$ centred at the $\alpha_r$ do not overlap modulo $1$, and $|T|$ and $|T'|$ are $1$-periodic, so
\[
\sum_r|S(\alpha_r)|^2\le\frac1\delta\int_0^1|T|^2+\int_0^1|T||T'|.
\]
By orthogonality, $\int_0^1 e((n-m)\alpha)\dd\alpha$ equals $1$ if $n=m$ and equals $0$ otherwise. So $\int_0^1|T|^2=\sum|a_n|^2$. Since $T'(\alpha)=\sum 2\pi i(n-c)a_ne((n-c)\alpha)$,
\[
\int_0^1|T'|^2=4\pi^2\sum(n-c)^2|a_n|^2\le\pi^2N^2\sum|a_n|^2.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz bounds $\int|T||T'|$ by $\pi N\sum|a_n|^2$.
\end{proof}

The shape is the useful part. The length $N$ counts the coefficients, $1/\delta$ counts how many sample points fit on the circle, and the inequality adds the two contributions.

\begin{remark}[the sharp constant; quoted]
Montgomery and Vaughan (1973), and Selberg, obtained the constant $N-1+1/\delta$, which is sharp. Nothing later uses that constant.
\end{remark}

The fractions $a/q$ with $q\le Q$, $\gcd(a,q)=1$ and $1\le a\le q$ are distinct modulo $1$, and they are $1/Q^2$-spaced: if two such fractions differ, then
\[
\Bigl|\frac aq-\frac{a'}{q'}\Bigr|=\frac{|aq'-a'q|}{qq'}\ge\frac1{qq'}.
\]
So, for $Q\ge2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arithls}
\sum_{q\le Q}\ \sum_{\substack{a=1\\(a,q)=1}}^q\Bigl|S\Bigl(\frac aq\Bigr)\Bigr|^2\le(\pi N+Q^2)\sum_n|a_n|^2.
\end{equation}

A set that misses residue classes modulo $p$ is unevenly spread modulo $p$, and the unevenness appears in the Fourier coefficients.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lsprime}
Let $\mathcal A$ be a finite set of integers, let $c_n$ be complex numbers for $n\in\mathcal A$, and let $S(\alpha)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal A}c_ne(n\alpha)$. Suppose that for each prime $p$ in a set $\mathcal Q$, the set $\mathcal A$ meets at most $p-\omega(p)$ classes modulo $p$, with $0\le\omega(p)<p$. Then for every squarefree $q$ whose prime factors lie in $\mathcal Q$,
\[
\sum_{\substack{a=1\\(a,q)=1}}^q\Bigl|S\Bigl(\frac aq\Bigr)\Bigr|^2\ge|S(0)|^2\prod_{p\mid q}\frac{\omega(p)}{p-\omega(p)}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{A prime $q=p$.} Let $Z_h=\sum_{n\in\mathcal A,\,n\equiv h\,(p)}c_n$, so $S(a/p)=\sum_{h=0}^{p-1}Z_he(ah/p)$. By orthogonality of the characters $h\mapsto e(ah/p)$,
\[
\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\bigl|S(a/p)\bigr|^2=p\sum_h|Z_h|^2.
\]
At most $p-\omega(p)$ of the $Z_h$ are non-zero, so Cauchy--Schwarz gives $|S(0)|^2=|\sum_h Z_h|^2\le(p-\omega(p))\sum_h|Z_h|^2$. Removing the term $a=0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\Bigl|S\Bigl(\frac ap\Bigr)\Bigr|^2&\ge\Bigl(\frac p{p-\omega(p)}-1\Bigr)|S(0)|^2\eqbr=\frac{\omega(p)}{p-\omega(p)}|S(0)|^2.\end{aligned}
\]
\emph{Induction.} Let $q=q_1p$ with $p\nmid q_1$. By the Chinese remainder theorem, as $a_1$ runs over the reduced classes modulo $q_1$ and $a_2$ over those modulo $p$, the sums $a_1/q_1+a_2/p$ run over the reduced fractions $a/q$ modulo $1$. For fixed $a_1$, apply the prime case to the coefficients $c_ne(na_1/q_1)$, which live on the same set $\mathcal A$:
\[
\sum_{a_2}\Bigl|S\Bigl(\frac{a_1}{q_1}+\frac{a_2}p\Bigr)\Bigr|^2\ge\frac{\omega(p)}{p-\omega(p)}\Bigl|S\Bigl(\frac{a_1}{q_1}\Bigr)\Bigr|^2.
\]
Sum over $a_1$ and use the induction hypothesis for $q_1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the arithmetic large sieve, Montgomery 1968]\label{thm:arithls}
Let $\mathcal A$ be a set of integers in $]M,M+N]$, let $Q\ge2$, and suppose that for every prime $p\le Q$ the set $\mathcal A$ meets at most $p-\omega(p)$ classes modulo $p$. Then
\[
\begin{gathered}|\mathcal A|\le\frac{\pi N+Q^2}L,\qq L=\sum_{q\le Q}\mu^2(q)\prod_{p\mid q}\frac{\omega(p)}{p-\omega(p)}.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $c_n=1$ on $\mathcal A$, so $S(0)=|\mathcal A|$. Sum Lemma~\ref{lem:lsprime} over squarefree $q\le Q$ (the term $q=1$ is $|S(0)|^2$ on both sides) and compare with \eqref{eq:arithls}. Then $|\mathcal A|^2 L\le(\pi N+Q^2)|\mathcal A|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:squares}
Let $\mathcal A\subset[1,N]$ be a set of integers such that, for every prime $p\le\sqrt N$, every element of $\mathcal A$ is a square modulo $p$. Then $|\mathcal A|=O(\sqrt N\log N)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For odd $p$ there are $(p+1)/2$ squares modulo $p$, counting $0$ (Volume~2, Chapter~6), so $\mathcal A$ misses $\omega(p)=(p-1)/2$ classes, and
\[
\frac{\omega(p)}{p-\omega(p)}=\frac{p-1}{p+1}\ge\frac12.
\]
Keeping only the prime terms $q=p$ in $L$ gives $L\ge\frac12(\pi(Q)-1)$. With $Q=\sqrt N$ and $\pi(Q)\sim Q/\log Q$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:pnt}), Theorem~\ref{thm:arithls} gives $|\mathcal A|\le(\pi+1)N/L=O(\sqrt N\log N)$.
\end{proof}

The squares themselves satisfy the hypothesis, and there are $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ of them up to $N$, so the bound is sharp up to the logarithm. Gallagher's larger sieve removes the logarithm. Up to $10^6$, the integers that are squares modulo every prime $p\le1000$ are exactly the $1000$ squares $1^2,\dots,1000^2$. Whether a large set that looks like the squares modulo every prime must be essentially the set of values of a quadratic polynomial is open. It is called the inverse sieve problem.

\begin{remark}[Bombieri--Vinogradov, quoted]\label{rem:bv}
The large sieve has a version with Dirichlet characters, obtained from Theorem~\ref{thm:largesieve} through Gauss sums (Chapter~\ref{ch:characters}). Its most famous consequence is the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem (1965). Let $E(x;q)=\max_{(a,q)=1}\bigl|\psi(x;q,a)-x/\varphi(q)\bigr|$. For every $A>0$ there is a $B$ such that
\[
\sum_{q\le\sqrt x/\log^Bx}E(x;q)=O\Bigl(\frac x{\log^Ax}\Bigr).
\]
On average over the moduli, primes in progressions are as well distributed as the generalized Riemann hypothesis predicts for each modulus separately. This average form of the hypothesis is the input to Chen's theorem and to the bounded-gap theorems of Zhang and of Maynard. It is not proved here. Davenport, Chapters~27 and~28, gives a proof.
\end{remark}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

The sieves in this chapter are upper bounds of the right order of magnitude. Theorem~\ref{thm:selberg} minimises a quadratic form by Cauchy--Schwarz. From it, Theorem~\ref{thm:bt} says that any interval of length $N$ contains at most $(2N/\log N)(1+O(\log\log N/\log N))$ primes. If that interval begins at $N^k$, the ratio of this bound to $N/\log(N^k)$ is $2k$. The factor $2$ that bears on Siegel zeros is the leading factor in the progression bound of Exercise~\ref{ex:btap}. Theorem~\ref{thm:twins} says $\pi_2(x)=O(x/\log^2x)$, and Corollary~\ref{cor:brun} turns that bound into a convergent series of reciprocals of twin primes. Theorem~\ref{thm:largesieve} bounds a trigonometric polynomial at well-spaced points, and Theorem~\ref{thm:arithls} concludes that a set which is made of squares modulo every prime $p\le\sqrt N$ has $O(\sqrt N\log N)$ elements up to $N$. Proposition~\ref{prop:legendre} is exact, and it shows why a lower bound of the same strength is harder: at $z=\sqrt x$ the fractional parts remove about $10.9\%$ of the main term.

The model of independent primes, corrected one prime at a time, produces the twin prime constant $2C_2$ and the density $6/\pi^2$ of the squarefree integers. The same model, pushed up to $\sqrt x$, miscounts the primes by the factor $2e^{-\gamma}$.

Problem~\ref{pb:buchstab} computes the proportion of integers up to $x$ with no prime factor below $x^{1/u}$, for $1<u\le3$. The equation that continues the proportion past $u=3$ is taken up in Volume~15. It is not a theorem of this chapter.

Chapter~\ref{ch:erdoskac} asks a different question of the same primes. The question is not whether $n$ is divisible by a small prime. It is how many distinct prime factors $n$ has. The answer is a sum of nearly independent indicators, one for each prime, and the central limit theorem of Volume~4 appears inside the integers.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:legendrepi}
Use Proposition~\ref{prop:legendre} with $z=\log x$ to show that $\pi(x)=O\bigl(x/\log\log x\bigr)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:legendrefail}
Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{d\mid P(\sqrt x)}\mu(d)\bigl\{\tfrac xd\bigr\}\sim(2e^{-\gamma}-1)\dfrac x{\log x}$. So the fractional parts in Legendre's formula add up to about $10.9\%$ of the main term, and to about $12.3\%$ of $x/\log x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:sophie}
A prime $p$ is a Sophie Germain prime if $2p+1$ is also prime. Show that there are $O\bigl(x/\log^2x\bigr)$ of them up to $x$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:btap}
Let $\gcd(a,q)=1$ and $x/q\ge3$. Show that
\[
\pi(x;q,a)\le\frac{2x}{\varphi(q)\log(x/q)}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log(x/q)}{\log(x/q)}\Bigr)\Bigr),
\]
uniformly in such $q$ and $a$. For $1<x/q<e$ the logarithm $\log\log(x/q)$ is negative, and the displayed factor is an asymptotic as $x/q\to\infty$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:goldbachup}
For even $N\ge4$, let $r(N)$ be the number of primes $p\le N$ such that $N-p$ is prime. Show that
\[
r(N)=O\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac N{\varphi(N)}\Bigr)^2\frac N{\log^2N}\Bigr).
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$, the signature exercise]\label{ex:twinheuristic}
(a) Show that the sum of $\frac1p+\frac1{p+2}$ over twin pairs with $p>x$ is $O(1/\log x)$.
(b) Explain the Hardy--Littlewood prediction. For $n$ near $x$, the model in which ``$n$ is prime'' and ``$n+2$ is prime'' are independent gives the chance $1/\log^2x$. Show that correcting for each prime $p$ --- the proportion of $n$ with $p\nmid n(n+2)$ is $1-\rho(p)/p$, in place of $(1-1/p)^2$ --- multiplies this by $2C_2$.
(c) Assuming $\pi_2(x)\sim2C_2\,x/\log^2x$, show that the tail in (a) is $\sim4C_2/\log x$, and use the partial sum $1.7383570$ up to $10^7$ to estimate Brun's constant $B_2$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:lsprimes}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:arithls} to the primes in $]M,M+N]$ to show $\pi(M+N)-\pi(M)\le\frac{2\pi N}{\log N}(1+o(1))$. Where does the constant $\pi$ enter, beside the constant $2$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:bt}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:nsq1}
Show that the number of $n\le x$ such that $n^2+1$ is prime is $O(x/\log x)$. (Whether the count tends to infinity is Landau's fourth problem, open since 1912.)
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:bonferroni}
\emph{Bonferroni's inequalities.} Let $A_1,\dots,A_m$ be events and $S_j=\sum_{i_1<\dots<i_j}P(A_{i_1}\cap\dots\cap A_{i_j})$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $P(A_1\cup\dots\cup A_m)\le\sum_{j=1}^t(-1)^{j-1}S_j$ for $t$ odd, and $\ge$ for $t$ even.
\item Deduce that the probability $D_n/n!$ that a random permutation of $[n]$ has no fixed point differs from $\sum_{j=0}^t(-1)^j/j!$ by at most $1/(t+1)!$, for $0\le t\le n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:buchstab}
\emph{Rough numbers and Buchstab's function, for $u\le3$.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\Phi(x,y)=1+\pi(x)-\pi(y)$ for $\sqrt x\le y\le x$, and that for $x^{1/3}\le y<\sqrt x$,
\[
\Phi(x,y)=1+\pi(x)-\pi(y)+\#\{(p,q):y<p\le q,\ pq\le x\}.
\]
\item Let $1<u\le3$. Show that $\frac{\log y}x\Phi(x,y)\to\omega(u)$ when $y=x^{1/u}$, where $\omega(u)=1/u$ for $1<u\le2$ and $\omega(u)=(1+\log(u-1))/u$ for $2\le u\le3$.
\item Check that $(u\omega(u))'=\omega(u-1)$ for $2<u<3$. This is Buchstab's equation on that interval; the equation for $u>3$ is taken up in Volume~15, and it is not proved in this chapter. Show that $\omega(2)<e^{-\gamma}<\omega(3)$.
\item Independent sieving by the primes up to $y$ would predict $\Phi(x,y)\approx x\prod_{p\le y}(1-1/p)\sim e^{-\gamma}x/\log y$, that is $\omega\equiv e^{-\gamma}$. Where on $]1,3]$ is $\omega$ smallest, and by what factor does the prediction overshoot there? Relate this to Legendre's failure (Exercise~\ref{ex:legendrefail}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:sievehand}
Take $x=30$ and $z=5$. List $\Phi(30,5)$, compute the main term of Proposition~\ref{prop:legendre}, and compare both with the error bound $2^{\pi(5)}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:weightten}
For the integers, and for $y=10$, compute
\[
G(10)=\sum_{d\le10}\frac{\mu^2(d)}{\varphi(d)}
\]
as a fraction. Then compute the Selberg weight $\lambda_2$ from the formula in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:selberg}, and check that it equals $-21/22$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:tworatio}
Let $N\ge3$ and let the interval of length $N$ begin at $x=N^k$, with $k\ge1$ fixed.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that the ratio of $2N/\log N$ to $N/\log x$ equals $2k$.
\item For $k=2$ and $N=100$, compute the two quantities and the ratio.
\item The leading factor $2$ in Exercise~\ref{ex:btap} is the factor which, if it could be lowered to $2-\varepsilon$ for moduli up to a small power of $x$, would rule out Siegel zeros. Say which bound that sentence refers to, the short-interval bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:bt} or the progression bound.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

Using Figure~\ref{fig:sieve60}, list the integers $n\le60$ with no prime factor among $2,3,5$. How many are there, and which of them is not $1$ and not prime? Compare the count with $60\prod_{p\le5}(1-1/p)$, and with the error allowance $2^{\pi(5)}$.

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:legendrepi}
\idea Sieve only by the primes up to a tiny $z$, so that the $2^{\pi(z)}$ error is smaller than the main term you are willing to accept, namely $x/\log z$.
\writeup Every prime $p\le x$ is either $\le z$ or has no prime factor $\le z$, so $\pi(x)\le z+\Phi(x,z)\le z+x\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)+2^z$. With $z=\log x$, one has $2^z=x^{\log2}$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens3} gives $\prod_{p\le z}(1-1/p)=O(1/\log z)=O(1/\log\log x)$. All three terms are $O(x/\log\log x)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:legendrefail}
\idea Legendre's identity is exact, so the error is the gap between the true count and the main term. Both of those have known asymptotics.
\writeup Legendre's identity says that the sum in question equals $x\prod_{p\le\sqrt x}(1-1/p)-\Phi(x,\sqrt x)$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens3}, the first term is
\[
\sim\frac{e^{-\gamma}x}{\log\sqrt x}=\frac{2e^{-\gamma}x}{\log x}.
\]
The survivors are $1$ and the primes in $]\sqrt x,x]$, so $\Phi(x,\sqrt x)=\pi(x)-\pi(\sqrt x)+1\sim x/\log x$ by the prime number theorem. The difference is $\sim(2e^{-\gamma}-1)x/\log x$. Since $2e^{-\gamma}-1=0.1229$, this is $12.3\%$ of $x/\log x$. Dividing by the main term $2e^{-\gamma}=1.1229$ gives $10.9\%$ of the main term. The approach to the limit is slow: the ratio of the main term to the count is $0.998$ at $x=10^4$ and $1.034$ at $x=10^6$, on the way to $1.1229$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sophie}
\idea The same sieve as for the twins, with $2n+1$ in place of $n+2$. Only the number of classes removed by each prime enters the quadratic form.
\writeup Let $\mathcal A=\{n(2n+1):n\le x\}$. For odd $p$, the congruence $p\mid n(2n+1)$ holds for $n\equiv0$ and $n\equiv-\frac12\pmod p$, two classes. For $p=2$, only $n\equiv0$. So $\rho$ is the same function as in Theorem~\ref{thm:twins}, and so are $g$, $h$, the lower bound for $G(y)$ and the remainder. A Sophie Germain prime $p>z$ gives an element $p(2p+1)$ coprime to $P(z)$. The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:twins} applies with the same choices of $y$ and $z$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:btap}
\idea The progression is an arithmetic progression of length about $x/q$, and the primes that divide $q$ are left out of the sieve. Their absence multiplies $G$ by $\varphi(q)/q$.
\writeup Let $\mathcal A=\{a+qm:0\le m\le(x-a)/q\}$, let $X=x/q$, and let $\mathcal P$ be the primes not dividing $q$. For $d$ squarefree and coprime to $q$, the condition $d\mid a+qm$ is one class of $m$ modulo $d$, so $|\mathcal A_d|=X/d+r_d$ with $|r_d|\le1$. Thus $g(d)=1/d$ and $h(d)=1/\varphi(d)$. Every squarefree $d\le y$ factors uniquely as $kd'$ with $k\mid q$ and $(d',q)=1$, and $1/\varphi(d)=1/(\varphi(k)\varphi(d'))$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}\frac q{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\substack{d'\le y\\(d',q)=1}}\frac{\mu^2(d')}{\varphi(d')}&=\sum_{k\mid q}\frac{\mu^2(k)}{\varphi(k)}\sum_{\substack{d'\le y\\(d',q)=1}}\frac{\mu^2(d')}{\varphi(d')}\eqbr\ge\sum_{d\le y}\frac{\mu^2(d)}{\varphi(d)}\ge\log y\end{aligned}
\]
by Lemma~\ref{lem:Glog}, using $\sum_{k\mid q}\mu^2(k)/\varphi(k)=\prod_{p\mid q}(1+1/(p-1))=q/\varphi(q)$. Take $y=z=\sqrt X/\log X$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}\pi(x;q,a)&\le z+\frac{X}{G(y)}+y^2\\&\le z+\frac q{\varphi(q)}\frac{x/q}{\frac12\log X-\log\log X}\eqbr+\frac X{\log^2X},\end{aligned}
\]
which is the claim, since $X/\log^2X$ and $z$ are $O\bigl(x/(\varphi(q)\log^2(x/q))\bigr)$, using $\varphi(q)\le q$. The leading factor is $2$, because $\log y$ is about $\frac12\log(x/q)$. This is the factor discussed after Theorem~\ref{thm:bt}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:goldbachup}
\idea Sieve $n$ and $N-n$ together. A prime that divides $N$ divides one of them whenever it divides the other, so it removes one class in place of two. That is where $N/\varphi(N)$ enters.
\writeup Let $\mathcal A=\{n(N-n):1\le n\le N\}$. For $p\nmid N$, the condition $p\mid n(N-n)$ is two classes of $n$. For $p\mid N$, it is one class. So $g(p)=2/p$ or $1/p$, and $h(p)=2/(p-2)$ or $1/(p-1)$. Restricting $G$ to odd $d$ coprime to $N$, and arguing as in Theorem~\ref{thm:twins},
\[
G(y)\ge\Bigl(\sum_{a\le\sqrt y,\,(a,2N)=1}\frac1a\Bigr)^2.
\]
Every $n\le Y$ is uniquely $kb$ with $k$ composed of primes dividing $2N$ and $(b,2N)=1$, so
\[
\prod_{p\mid2N}\Bigl(1-\frac1p\Bigr)^{-1}\sum_{\substack{b\le Y\\(b,2N)=1}}\frac1b\ge\sum_{n\le Y}\frac1n\ge\log Y.
\]
Since $N$ is even, $\varphi(2N)/(2N)=\varphi(N)/N$, and $G(y)\ge(\varphi(N)/N)^2(\log^2y)/4$. The remainder is bounded as for the twins. With $y=z=\sqrt N/\log^3N$,
\[
r(N)\le z+\frac N{G(y)}+\frac N{\log^4N},
\]
which is the claim. Since $N/\varphi(N)=O(\log\log N)$, the bound is $O\bigl(N(\log\log N)^2/\log^2N\bigr)$. Hardy and Littlewood predicted
\[
r(N)\sim2C_2\prod_{\substack{p\mid N\\p>2}}\frac{p-1}{p-2}\cdot\frac N{\log^2N}.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:twinheuristic}
\idea A sum over a set of density about $1/\log^2t$ converges as fast as $\int_x^\infty\mathrm dt/(t\log^2t)=1/\log x$.
\writeup (a) By Abel summation,
\[
\sum_{p>x}\frac1p\le\int_x^\infty\frac{\pi_2(t)}{t^2}\dd t=O\Bigl(\int_x^\infty\frac{\mathrm dt}{t\log^2t}\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\frac1{\log x}\Bigr).
\]
The boundary term $-\pi_2(x)/x$ is negative, and the same bound holds for $1/(p+2)<1/p$.

(b) For an odd prime $p$, independence would give $\Pr(p\nmid n)\Pr(p\nmid n+2)=(1-1/p)^2$, while the true proportion of $n$ with $p\nmid n(n+2)$ is $1-2/p$. For $p=2$, the independent model gives $(1-1/2)^2=1/4$, and the true proportion is $1/2$. The correction factor is
\[
\frac{1/2}{1/4}\prod_{p>2}\frac{1-2/p}{(1-1/p)^2}=2\prod_{p>2}\frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2}=2\prod_{p>2}\Bigl(1-\frac1{(p-1)^2}\Bigr)=2C_2.
\]
The product converges absolutely. This is the same prime-by-prime correction that Chapter~\ref{ch:mertens} found in the comparison of two sieve problems. The factor $2e^{-\gamma}$, which appears when a single sieve runs all the way to $\sqrt x$, cancels in the ratio.

(c) Abel summation with $\pi_2(t)\sim2C_2 t/\log^2t$ gives
\[
\sum_{p>x}\frac1p\sim\int_x^\infty\frac{2C_2}{t\log^2t}\dd t=\frac{2C_2}{\log x},
\]
and the same for $1/(p+2)$, so the tail of $B_2$ is $4C_2/\log x$. At $x=10^7$, one has $4C_2/\log x=0.16383$, and
\[
1.7383570+0.16383=1.90219,
\]
beside $B_2=1.9021605\ldots$. The same extrapolation from the partial sum $1.67280$ up to $10^5$, adding $4C_2/\log(10^5)=0.22936$, gives $1.90216$. The series of Brun converges slowly. The extrapolation from the conjectured main term is much closer.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:lsprimes}
\idea The primes avoid the single class $0$ modulo every small prime, which is the input the large sieve asks for. The sum $L$ is then the same sum that appears as $G(y)$ in Selberg's sieve.
\writeup Let $Q=\sqrt N/\log N$, and let $\mathcal A$ be the primes in $]M,M+N]$ that are greater than $Q$. They avoid the class $0$ modulo each $p\le Q$, so $\omega(p)=1$, $\omega(p)/(p-\omega(p))=1/(p-1)$, and
\[
L=\sum_{q\le Q}\frac{\mu^2(q)}{\varphi(q)}\ge\log Q.
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:arithls} gives $|\mathcal A|\le(\pi N+Q^2)/\log Q=\pi N(1+o(1))/(\frac12\log N)$, and the primes $\le Q$ add $O(\sqrt N)$. The factor $\pi$ is the constant in Theorem~\ref{thm:largesieve}. With the sharp constant $N-1+Q^2$ in place of $\pi N+Q^2$, the same argument gives $2N/\log N$, the leading factor of Theorem~\ref{thm:bt}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:nsq1}
\idea The polynomial $n^2+1$ has $\rho(p)$ roots modulo $p$, and $\rho(p)$ is $2$ or $0$ according to $p\bmod4$. On average it is $1$, so the sieve removes about one class per prime, and $G(y)$ grows like $\log y$, with constant coming from $L(1,\chi_{-4})=\pi/4$.
\writeup Let $\mathcal A=\{n^2+1:n\le x\}$ and let $\mathcal P=\{2\}\cup\{p\equiv1\ (4)\}$. Primes $p\equiv3\pmod4$ never divide $n^2+1$ (Volume~2, Chapter~6), so sieving by them removes nothing. For $p\in\mathcal P$ let $\rho(p)$ be the number of roots of $n^2+1$ modulo $p$: one has $\rho(2)=1$ and $\rho(p)=2$. Then $|\mathcal A_d|=\rho(d)x/d+r_d$ with $|r_d|\le\rho(d)\le\tau(d)$, and the remainder is handled as in Theorem~\ref{thm:twins}. As there, for squarefree odd $d$ composed of primes $\equiv1\pmod4$,
\[
h(d)\ge\sum_{\operatorname{rad}(m)=d}\frac{\tau(m)}m,
\]
so $G(y)\ge\sum'_{m\le y}\tau(m)/m$, the sum over $m$ composed of primes $\equiv1\pmod4$.

Compare with $f=1*\chi_{-4}$, which is multiplicative, with $f(p^k)=k+1$ for $p\equiv1$, $f(2^k)=1$, and $f(p^k)=1$ or $0$ according as $k$ is even or odd, for $p\equiv3\pmod4$. Every $m\le y$ with $f(m)\ne0$ is $m'k$ with $m'$ composed of primes $\equiv1\pmod4$, $f(m)=\tau(m')$, and $k=2^jl^2$ with $l$ composed of primes $\equiv3\pmod4$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{m\le y}\frac{f(m)}m&\le\sum'_{m'\le y}\frac{\tau(m')}{m'}\sum_{j\ge0}\frac1{2^j}\sum_{l\ge1}\frac1{l^2}\eqbr=2\zeta(2)\sum'_{m'\le y}\frac{\tau(m')}{m'}.\end{aligned}
\]
By the hyperbola method, $\sum_{m\le y}f(m)/m=\sum_{ab\le y}\chi_{-4}(b)/(ab)=L(1,\chi_{-4})\log y+O(1)$, with $L(1,\chi_{-4})=\pi/4$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:characters}). So
\[
G(y)\ge\frac{\pi/4}{2\zeta(2)}\log y\,(1+o(1))=\frac3{4\pi}\log y\,(1+o(1)).
\]
Take $y=z=\sqrt x/\log^2x$. The remainder is at most $\bigl(y(1+\log y)\bigr)^2=O(x/\log^2x)$, and
\[
\#\{n\le x:n^2+1\text{ is prime}\}\le z+\frac x{G(y)}+O\Bigl(\frac x{\log^2x}\Bigr)=O\Bigl(\frac x{\log x}\Bigr).
\]
The conjectured count is $\sim Cx/\log x$ with $C=\frac12\prod_{p>2}\bigl(1-\chi_{-4}(p)/(p-1)\bigr)=0.6864\ldots$, the Hardy--Littlewood constant for $n^2+1$. The sieve gives the order of magnitude. The parity problem stands in the way of an asymptotic formula.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bonferroni}
\idea Look at one outcome at a time. If it lies in $r$ of the events, the truncated alternating sum counts it $1-(-1)^t\binom{r-1}t$ times, which lies above or below $1$ according to the parity of $t$.

\writeup (a) For an outcome in exactly $r\ge1$ events, the indicator of the union is $1$, and the outcome contributes $\binom rj$ to $S_j$, because it lies in $\binom rj$ of the intersections of $j$ events. By induction on $t$ from Pascal's rule,
\[
\sum_{j=1}^t(-1)^{j-1}\binom rj=1-(-1)^t\binom{r-1}t.
\]
This is at least $1$ for $t$ odd and at most $1$ for $t$ even, and for $r=0$ every term is $0$. Taking expectations of these inequalities, which hold at every outcome, gives (a).

(b) Let $A_i$ be the event that $i$ is a fixed point. An intersection of $j$ of them has probability $(n-j)!/n!$, so $S_j=\binom nj(n-j)!/n!=1/j!$. Then $D_n/n!=1-P(\bigcup A_i)$ lies between $\sum_{j=0}^t(-1)^j/j!$ and $\sum_{j=0}^{t+1}(-1)^j/j!$, which differ by $1/(t+1)!$ when $t<n$. For $t=n$ the sum is exact inclusion--exclusion.

\tools Volume~1, Chapter~2 (inclusion--exclusion, derangements).
\leads Brun's sieve of 1915 truncates inclusion--exclusion over the primes and uses a Bonferroni inequality at a chosen $t$. That truncation gave the first upper bound of the shape of Theorem~\ref{thm:twins}. This chapter proved the same shape with Selberg's quadratic form.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:buchstab}
\idea A number below $x$ with no prime factor below $x^{1/3}$ is $1$, a prime, or a product of two primes. Counting the products with the prime number theorem produces $\int\mathrm dt/(t(1-t))$. The case $u\le3$ is the whole of this problem. Volume~15 takes up Buchstab's equation for larger $u$.

\writeup (a) A $y$-rough $n\le x$ with $n>1$ and $y\ge x^{1/3}$ has at most two prime factors, since three would multiply to more than $y^3\ge x$. If $y\ge\sqrt x$ it cannot have two either, so it is a prime in $]y,x]$. If $x^{1/3}\le y<\sqrt x$, it is a prime in $]y,x]$ or a product $pq$ with $y<p\le q$ and $pq\le x$.

(b) For $1<u\le2$, one has $\Phi(x,y)=1+\pi(x)-\pi(y)$, which is $\sim x/\log x$ since $\pi(x^{1/u})=o(\pi(x))$. Multiplying by $\log y/x=(\log x)/(ux)$ gives $1/u$. For $2<u\le3$, the pairs number
\[
\sum_{y<p\le\sqrt x}\Bigl(\pi\Bigl(\frac xp\Bigr)-\pi(p)+1\Bigr),
\]
which is $\sum_{y<p\le\sqrt x}\pi(x/p)+O(\pi(\sqrt x)^2)$. Since $x/p\ge\sqrt x$, one has $\pi(x/p)=(1+o(1))x/(p\log(x/p))$ uniformly, and the sum is
\[
(1+o(1))\frac x{\log x}\sum_{y<p\le\sqrt x}\frac1p\,F\Bigl(\frac{\log p}{\log x}\Bigr)\qquad\text{with }F(t)=\frac1{1-t}.
\]
By Mertens's second theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:mertens2}), $\sum_{x^a<p\le x^b}1/p\to\log(b/a)$ for fixed $0<a<b$. Cutting $[1/u,1/2]$ into small intervals and using the continuity of $F$, Riemann sums give
\[
\sum_{x^{1/u}<p\le x^{1/2}}\frac1p\,F\Bigl(\frac{\log p}{\log x}\Bigr)\to\int_{1/u}^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm dt}{t(1-t)}=\Bigl[\log\frac t{1-t}\Bigr]_{1/u}^{1/2}=\log(u-1).
\]
So $\Phi(x,y)=(1+\log(u-1)+o(1))x/\log x$ and $(\log y/x)\Phi(x,y)\to(1+\log(u-1))/u$. At $u=2$ both formulas give $1/2$.

(c) For $2<u<3$, one has $(u\omega(u))'=1/(u-1)=\omega(u-1)$, because $u-1\in\left]1,2\right[$. So $\omega(2)=0.5<e^{-\gamma}=0.56146<\omega(3)=(1+\log2)/3=0.56438$. The identity $(u\omega(u))'=\omega(u-1)$ for $u>3$, and the limit of $\omega(u)$, are not proved in this chapter. Volume~15 takes up that range.

(d) The function $\omega$ decreases from $1$ to $1/2$ on $]1,2]$. On $[2,3]$, one has $\omega(u)\ge1/2$, because $2+2\log(u-1)-u$ vanishes at $u=2$ and has derivative $2/(u-1)-1\ge0$. So the minimum on $]1,3]$ is $\omega(2)=1/2$. There the independent-sieve prediction $e^{-\gamma}$ overshoots by the factor $2e^{-\gamma}=1.1229$. This is Legendre's failure at $z=\sqrt x$: sieving the integers up to $x$ by the primes up to $\sqrt x$ leaves the primes of $]\sqrt x,x]$, about $x/\log x$ of them, while $x\prod_{p\le\sqrt x}(1-1/p)\sim2e^{-\gamma}x/\log x$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:legendrefail}). As $u\to1$, the prediction $e^{-\gamma}$ is smaller than $\omega(u)=1/u$ by a factor that tends to infinity.

\tools{Theorems~\ref{thm:mertens2} and~\ref{thm:pnt}, Exercise~\ref{ex:legendrefail}.}
\leads This problem is the case $1<u\le3$. Buchstab (1937) wrote the delay equation $(u\omega(u))'=\omega(u-1)$ for all $u>1$, and de Bruijn (1950) proved that $\omega(u)\to e^{-\gamma}$. Those results, and the range $u>3$, are taken up in Volume~15. They are not theorems of this chapter. Figure~\ref{fig:buchstab} draws the function from the formulas in (b) and continues the curve past $3$ by the delay equation.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:tworatio}
\idea When the interval starts at $N^k$, the logarithm in the prime-number-theorem scale is $k$ times $\log N$. The short-interval bound still has $\log N$ in the denominator, so the ratio of the two expressions is $2k$. The factor tied to Siegel zeros is the leading $2$ in the progression bound.
\writeup (a) If $x=N^k$, then $\log x=k\log N$, and
\[
\frac{2N/\log N}{N/\log x}=\frac{2N}{\log N}\cdot\frac{k\log N}N=2k.
\]
(b) For $k=2$ and $N=100$, one has $x=10^4$, $\log100=4.60517$ and $\log(10^4)=9.21034$. Then $2N/\log N=200/4.60517=43.43$ and $N/\log x=100/9.21034=10.86$. The ratio is $4$, which is $2k$.
(c) The sentence after Theorem~\ref{thm:bt} refers to the progression bound of Exercise~\ref{ex:btap}. That is the factor $2$ which, lowered to $2-\varepsilon$ for moduli up to a small power of $x$, would rule out Siegel zeros. The short-interval ratio in (a) equals $2k$, and for $k>1$ it is larger than $2$.
\end{solution}
