# Description

The integers are defined without probability, and yet their most famous sequences behave, in part, like chance. This volume asks when that resemblance is a theorem.

It begins with the question itself. What should "random-like" mean for a deterministic set? Cramér's model of the primes explains a great deal, and Maier's theorem shows where independence fails. The prime factors of a typical integer are then made precise: the Turán–Kubilius inequality, the Erdős–Kac theorem, and the Kubilius model.

The statistics of arithmetic come next. Random multiplicative functions, in the Steinhaus and Rademacher models, are the setting for the conjectures of Chowla and Sarnak. Primes in short intervals are compared with the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures, Gallagher's Poisson law and the Montgomery–Soundararajan variance. Sieves are read as inclusion–exclusion, up to the parity barrier. Class groups are given the Cohen–Lenstra measure, and ranks of elliptic curves are compared with Goldfeld's conjecture, the Bhargava–Shankar averages and the Poonen–Rains model of Selmer groups.

The spectral part states Montgomery's pair correlation, the Keating–Snaith moments and the recipe for families of L-functions, then the Bohr–Jessen theorem and Selberg's central limit theorem for the logarithm of the zeta function. The last of the four parts is where structure wins: Gowers norms, the Green–Tao theorem, Chebyshev's bias, Maier's irregularities, and the bias in consecutive primes found by Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan.

The volume ends with a workshop. One problem is followed through its whole life. Papers are read with their proofs: card shuffling, Selberg's theorem at many points, random groups, and Harper's theorem on random multiplicative functions. Experiments, heuristics, proof assistants and the writing of a proof each get a chapter, followed by open problems and projects. Twelve appendices are case studies of conjectures, from twin primes and the Riemann hypothesis to Collatz, the abc conjecture and Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer.

Every workshop chapter proves what it uses and says where an earlier volume has already proved the rest. The thirteen opening chapters state the classical theorems that frame the questions.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
