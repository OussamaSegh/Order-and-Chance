\chapter*{Preface}
This book is about questions nobody can answer yet. Every even number greater than $2$ seems to be a sum of two
primes; twin primes seem never to stop; every starting number seems to fall to $1$ under the rule ``halve it if
it is even, triple it and add one if it is odd''; the zeros of the zeta function all seem to lie on one line.
Computers have checked each of these statements for numbers so large that nobody doubts them, and yet nobody
can prove them. An open problem is the best possible guide to the frontier: it shows exactly where our methods
stop, and why.

Each of the twelve chapters takes one problem, or one family of problems, and tells its whole story. Who asked
the question, when and why. What the evidence says, with computations you can repeat. Why we believe the
answer, usually through a model of chance, made precise enough to predict constants that the numbers then
confirm. What is proved, with full proofs of the strongest results within reach. Why the problem is hard, with
the obstruction explained honestly. And how to read one of the research papers that moved it forward. The
journey goes from the strange ways conjectures are born and die, through twin primes, Goldbach and the Riemann
hypothesis, the zeros of zeta seen as the eigenvalues of a random matrix, the Collatz problem, Hadamard
matrices and problems about points and distances in the plane, to the abc conjecture, the conjecture of Birch
and Swinnerton-Dyer, the hidden randomness of the M\"obius function, and four open doors in algebra.

The book never claims that a conjecture is true. It says what is proved, what has been checked and to what
bound, with a date, because these records move, and what is believed and why. Along the way you will meet
some of the most beautiful theorems in mathematics: Erd\H os's small gaps between primes, Schnirelmann's
theorem that every integer is a sum of boundedly many primes, Terras's theorem that almost every Collatz orbit
drops, Paley's matrices built from quadratic residues, Mason's $abc$ theorem for polynomials, Gauss's point
counts on elliptic curves, the Erd\H os discrepancy problem for sequences of length twelve, and the symmetric
groups as Galois groups.

The book is written for readers who know the material of Part~I of the series; the analytic chapters lean on
Tome~5 as well, and results proved in other tomes are quoted with a precise reference. Every theorem stated here
is proved; deeper results are labelled as remarks with a reference. Each chapter opens with a question, and
``Pause and think'' boxes ask you to guess before a key result. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} warm-up,
\textbf{$\star\star$} for a good afternoon and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} gems, each with a hint and a solution
that starts with \emph{How to find it}. Every chapter ends with a list of open doors, small questions to try,
and every picture is drawn from numbers computed for this book. A companion Python notebook for each chapter
repeats the experiments and invites you to push them further: Goldbach comets, zeta zeros, Collatz records,
Paley matrices, the BSD products of elliptic curves, Liouville's coin flips and polynomials with small Mahler
measure.
