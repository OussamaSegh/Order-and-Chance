# Description

Every even number greater than 2 seems to be a sum of two primes. Twin primes seem never to stop. Every starting number seems to fall to 1 under the rule "halve it if it is even, triple it and add one if it is odd". Computers have checked these statements for numbers so large that nobody doubts them, and yet nobody can prove them. This book takes twelve of the most famous open problems in mathematics and tells the whole story of each one.

Every chapter follows the same path. The story: who asked the question, when and why, with every quote and date sourced. The evidence: computations you can repeat, and how far the conjecture has been checked, with the year, because these records move. The heuristic: why we believe the answer, usually through a model of chance precise enough to predict constants that the numbers then confirm. What is proved: the strongest results within reach, with complete proofs. Why it is hard: the obstruction, explained honestly. And a guide to one of the research papers that moved the problem forward.

The twelve chapters: how conjectures are born and die (Polya's conjecture, Mertens, Euler's sums of powers); twin primes and prime gaps (Erdos's small gaps, Rankin's large gaps, the parity problem); Goldbach (Schnirelmann's theorem); the Riemann hypothesis (Gronwall, Robin and Lagarias); zeta and random matrices (the semicircle law, level repulsion, Montgomery and Dyson); the Collatz problem (Terras's theorem, cycles); Hadamard matrices (Sylvester, Paley, Williamson, and the open order 668); points, distances and families of sets (the chromatic number of the plane, unit distances, union-closed families); the abc conjecture (Mason's theorem for polynomials); Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (point counts by Jacobi sums); randomness in the integers (Chowla, Sarnak, the Erdos discrepancy problem, Artin's primitive roots, Cohen-Lenstra); and open doors in algebra (the inverse Galois problem, Lehmer's problem, the Jacobian conjecture, Ax-Grothendieck).

Open problems are the best way into research: they show exactly where the known methods stop. The book never claims that a conjecture is true; it separates what is proved, what is checked and what is believed. Every theorem is proved in full, deeper results are clearly labelled with references, and every chapter ends with exercises (each with a hint and a solution that explains how to find the idea), a list of small open questions to try, and sources for further reading.

The book is written for pleasure, in a storytelling style, for readers with a solid undergraduate background in number theory, algebra and analysis. Every picture is drawn from numbers computed for the book, and a free companion Python notebook for each chapter repeats the experiments and invites you to push them further: Goldbach comets, zeros of zeta, Collatz records, Paley matrices, elliptic curves modulo primes, the coin flips of the Liouville function and polynomials of small Mahler measure.
