# Description

In 1974 a design teacher in Budapest built a wooden cube whose faces could twist. Its plastic descendant has 43 quintillion positions, and every one of them can be solved in at most twenty moves. Nobody found that by twisting: it comes from algebra, the study of what survives a transformation.

This book builds groups, rings, fields and representations from the single idea of symmetry, and climbs to two summits. The first is Galois's answer to a three-hundred-year-old question: there is a formula in radicals for the roots of every equation of degree four, but none for x⁵ − 4x + 2 = 0, because a group of permutations of its roots is too large to be built from abelian pieces. The second is the character table of a finite group, a small square of numbers that holds most of what there is to know about the group.

On the way come group actions and the art of counting orbits, the symmetric group and the 15-puzzle, the Sylow theorems, rings and ideals, modules over principal ideal domains (which classify every finite abelian group and prove the Cayley–Hamilton theorem in one stroke), field extensions and the impossibility of trisecting an angle, finite fields and the codes that let a QR code survive a coffee stain, and the quaternions that describe the rotations of space.

Every result is proved in full. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas and has graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that explains how to find the idea, figures, and sources for further reading.

Every chapter has a Python notebook where groups are built and counted, and algebra meets error-correcting codes and cryptography.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
