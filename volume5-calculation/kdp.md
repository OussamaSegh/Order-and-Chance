# Description

In 1713 Jacob Bernoulli wrote that it had taken him less than half of a quarter of an hour to add the tenth powers of the first thousand integers. In 1735 Euler announced that 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … = π²/6. In 1799 Gauss noticed that the arithmetic–geometric mean of 1 and √2 matched a constant he knew to eleven decimals, and recorded in his diary that a new field of analysis would open. This book is that art of calculation: the exact value of a sum, a product or an integral, and, when no exact value exists, an approximation with an error one can bound.

Its tools are finite calculus and generating functions; classic series and infinite products; integration, including differentiation under the integral sign; the Gamma and Beta functions; Fourier series, and Fourier analysis on finite groups, where it becomes the fast Fourier transform; the Fourier transform and Poisson summation; complex analysis and residues; the zeta function with its functional equation, and the precise sense in which 1 + 2 + 3 + … = −1/12; asymptotics, from Stirling's formula to a saddle point; the Hardy–Ramanujan formula for the number of partitions; and the arithmetic–geometric mean, which doubles the number of correct digits of π at every step.

Every theorem is proved. Quoted results are marked, and nothing depends on them; Rademacher's series for the partition function is one of them. Each chapter opens on the formula it will explain, and has graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution.

Written for a reader who wants the proofs.
