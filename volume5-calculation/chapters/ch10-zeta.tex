The series $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s}$ has already appeared several times: the even values in Chapter~\ref{ch:series}, the Bernoulli numbers in Chapter~\ref{ch:finite}, the theta function in Chapter~\ref{ch:transform}, and the residues in Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}. This chapter treats $\zeta$ as a function of a complex variable. Euler found that it is a product over the primes, so an identity for $\zeta$ is an identity about the integers. Riemann's paper of November 1859, eight pages in the \emph{Monatsberichte der Berliner Akademie}, extends $\zeta$ to the plane with one pole and proves the symmetry $s\leftrightarrow 1-s$. We prove both, compute $\zeta$ at the non-positive integers, and say what $1+2+3+\cdots=-\frac1{12}$ means. The zeros on the line $\operatorname{Re}s=\frac12$ are stated as Riemann stated them: a conjecture, checked by computation, and not proved here.

The symmetry is Poisson summation for the Gaussian, written in a different variable. Chapter~\ref{ch:hr} uses the same summation to count partitions.

The next fact, that $\zeta(1+it)\ne0$, and the reason this implies the prime number theorem, is Volume~6, Chapter~5. One promise of Volume~2, Chapter~4, is kept here: a Dirichlet series turns the convolution of arithmetic functions into a product.

\section{Dirichlet series}

Write $s=\sigma+it$ with $\sigma$ and $t$ real, and $n^{-s}=e^{-s\log n}$, so that $|n^{-s}|=n^{-\sigma}$. A \emph{Dirichlet series} is a series $F(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}a_nn^{-s}$ with complex coefficients.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dirseries}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $\sum|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}<\infty$, then $\sum a_nn^{-s}$ converges absolutely and uniformly on $\operatorname{Re}s\ge\sigma_0$, and its sum is holomorphic on
$\operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_0$, with $F'(s)=-\sum a_n(\log n)n^{-s}$.
\item If the partial sums $A_N=\sum_{n\le N}a_n$ are bounded, then $\sum a_nn^{-s}$ converges on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, uniformly on every closed disc there,
and its sum is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The comparison $|a_nn^{-s}|\le|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}$ is the Weierstrass test. Each term $a_nn^{-s}$ is entire. Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(a) says the sum is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_0$, and that it may be differentiated term by term. Differentiation hits the exponential $e^{-s\log n}$ and brings down a factor $-\log n$.

(b) For $x\ge n$, the difference $n^{-s}-x^{-s}=s\int_n^xu^{-s-1}\dd u$ has modulus at most $|s|(x-n)n^{-\sigma-1}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:abel}, with $|A_n|\le M$, gives
\[
\sum_{n=1}^Na_nn^{-s}=A_NN^{-s}+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}A_n\bigl(n^{-s}-(n+1)^{-s}\bigr).
\]
On a closed disc in $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ one has $|s|\le C$ and $\sigma\ge\sigma_1>0$. The boundary term satisfies $|A_NN^{-s}|\le MN^{-\sigma_1}$, which tends to $0$ as $N\to\infty$. Each term of the remaining sum is at most $MCn^{-\sigma_1-1}$, and $\sum n^{-\sigma_1-1}$ converges. The partial sums of the Dirichlet series therefore converge uniformly on the disc. Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(a) makes the sum holomorphic.
\end{proof}

For instance $\zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. The alternating series $\eta(s)=\sum(-1)^{n-1}n^{-s}$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, because the partial sums of $(-1)^{n-1}$ are $0$ or $1$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:eta}).

\begin{proposition}[products, uniqueness]\label{prop:dirmult}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $\sum a_nn^{-s}$ and $\sum b_nn^{-s}$ converge absolutely, then their product is $\sum_nc_nn^{-s}$, with $c=a*b$ the Dirichlet convolution
$c_n=\sum_{de=n}a_db_e$, and this series converges absolutely.
\item If $\sum|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}<\infty$ and $\sum a_nn^{-\sigma}=0$ for all real $\sigma>\sigma_0$, then $a_n=0$ for every $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The double series $\sum_{d,e}a_db_e(de)^{-s}$ converges absolutely, being the product of two absolutely convergent series. Group the terms by the value of $n=de$.

(b) Otherwise, let $m$ be the least index with $a_m\ne0$. For $\sigma>\sigma_0$,
\[
0=m^\sigma\sum_na_nn^{-\sigma}=a_m+\sum_{n>m}a_n\Bigl(\frac mn\Bigr)^\sigma.
\]
For $n>m$ one has $|a_n(m/n)^\sigma|\le|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}m^{\sigma_0}(m/n)^{\sigma-\sigma_0}$, and $\frac mn\le\frac m{m+1}$. The sum over $n>m$ is at most
\[
\Bigl(\frac m{m+1}\Bigr)^{\sigma-\sigma_0}m^{\sigma_0}\sum_n|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}.
\]
The quantity being bounded is that tail. The series of $|a_n|n^{-\sigma_0}$ is a fixed finite number, and $\bigl(\frac m{m+1}\bigr)^{\sigma-\sigma_0}$ tends to $0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$, because $\frac m{m+1}<1$. Hence $a_m=0$, which contradicts the choice of $m$.
\end{proof}

An absolutely convergent Dirichlet series determines its coefficients. A product of Dirichlet series computes a Dirichlet convolution, just as a product of power series computes a Cauchy product. That is the promise of Volume~2, Chapter~4.

\section{The Euler product}

The pause box asks which powers $n^{-s}$ appear when you multiply the geometric series over the primes. Unique factorisation says each $n$ appears once, as soon as every prime factor of $n$ has been included. The theorem is that sentence written as an equality of functions.

\begin{theorem}[Euler product]\label{thm:eulerproduct}
Let $f$ be a multiplicative arithmetic function with $\sum|f(n)|n^{-\sigma}<\infty$. Then for $\operatorname{Re}s\ge\sigma$,
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{f(n)}{n^s}=\prod_p\Bigl(1+\frac{f(p)}{p^s}+\frac{f(p^2)}{p^{2s}}+\cdots\Bigr),
\]
the product over the primes in increasing order being convergent. In particular, for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$,
\[
\zeta(s)=\prod_p\frac1{1-p^{-s}}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each local factor converges absolutely. Fix a bound $P$. The product of the finitely many factors with $p\le P$ expands, by multiplying absolutely convergent series, into the sum of $f(p_1^{k_1})\cdots f(p_r^{k_r})(p_1^{k_1}\cdots p_r^{k_r})^{-s}$ over all choices of exponents. Unique factorisation and multiplicativity turn that sum into $\sum_{n\in S_P}f(n)n^{-s}$, where $S_P$ is the set of positive integers whose prime factors are all at most $P$. Every $n\le P$ lies in $S_P$. The difference between this partial product and the full series is at most $\sum_{n>P}|f(n)|n^{-\sigma}$. That tail tends to $0$ as $P\to\infty$, because $\sum|f(n)|n^{-\sigma}$ converges. For $\zeta$, take $f=\mathbf 1$. Each local factor is the geometric series $\sum_kp^{-ks}=\frac1{1-p^{-s}}$.
\end{proof}

At $s=2$ the partial products are the numbers in the opening, and in Figure~\ref{fig:rw10-euler}. The product through $p=5$ is $\frac{25}{16}=1.5625$, and $\frac{\pi^2}6-1.5625\approx0.08243$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:mobius}
For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^s}=\frac1{\zeta(s)},\qq\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\varphi(n)}{n^s}=\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}\quad(\operatorname{Re}s>2).\end{gathered}
\]
In particular $\zeta(s)\ne0$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:dirmult}(a), $\zeta(s)\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}=\sum(\mathbf 1*\mu)(n)n^{-s}=1$, since $\mu*\mathbf 1=\varepsilon$ (Volume~2, Chapter~4). The product of $\zeta(s)$ with a holomorphic function is $1$, so $\zeta(s)\ne0$. Similarly $\varphi*\mathbf 1=\operatorname{id}$ (Volume~2, Chapter~3: $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$) gives $\zeta(s)\sum\varphi(n)n^{-s}=\sum n\cdot n^{-s}=\zeta(s-1)$. All three series converge absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}s>2$, because $\varphi(n)\le n$.
\end{proof}

At $s=2$, one has $\sum\frac{\mu(n)}{n^2}=\frac6{\pi^2}$. An exercise of Volume~2, Chapter~4, needed this value: the probability that two integers drawn from $1,\ldots,N$ are coprime tends to $\frac6{\pi^2}\approx0.6079$. The Euler product is the heuristic behind the limit. Two integers are coprime when no prime divides both. The prime $p$ divides both with ``probability'' $\frac1{p^2}$, and different primes are treated as independent, so the probability should be $\prod_p\bigl(1-\frac1{p^2}\bigr)=\frac1{\zeta(2)}$.

\begin{corollary}[Euler, 1737]\label{cor:primesdiverge}
$\displaystyle\sum_p\frac1p=\infty$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $\sigma>1$ be real. By Theorem~\ref{thm:eulerproduct} and Proposition~\ref{prop:products}, $\log\zeta(\sigma)=\sum_p-\log(1-p^{-\sigma})$. For $0\le x\le\frac12$,
\[
-\log(1-x)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{x^k}k\le x+x^2\sum_{k\ge0}x^k\le x+2x^2,
\]
so $\log\zeta(\sigma)\le\sum_pp^{-\sigma}+2\sum_nn^{-2}$. Since $\zeta(\sigma)\ge\int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}\dd x=\frac1{\sigma-1}$, the left side tends to $\infty$ as $\sigma\to1^+$. The comparison forces $\sum_pp^{-\sigma}$ to tend to $\infty$ as well, and $\sum_pp^{-\sigma}\le\sum_p\frac1p$.
\end{proof}

The primes are thicker than the squares: $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ converges, and $\sum\frac1p$ diverges. Exercise~\ref{ex:primesum} sharpens the argument to $\sum_pp^{-\sigma}=\log\frac1{\sigma-1}+O(1)$.

\section{Beyond the line of convergence}

The series $\sum n^{-s}$ diverges at $s=1$ and for real $s<1$. The function $\zeta$ still has a value there, by analytic continuation. Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} says the continuation, if it exists, is unique. The first step stays on the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:zetastrip}
The function $\zeta(s)-\frac1{s-1}$ extends to a holomorphic function on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, whose value at $s=1$ is Euler's constant $\gamma$. So $\zeta$ is
holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ except for a simple pole at $1$ with residue $1$, and $\zeta(s)=\frac1{s-1}+\gamma+O(s-1)$ near $1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, $\frac1{s-1}=\int_1^\infty x^{-s}\dd x=\sum_n\int_n^{n+1}x^{-s}\dd x$, so
\[
\begin{gathered}\zeta(s)-\frac1{s-1}=\sum_{n\ge1}\phi_n(s),\qq\phi_n(s)=\int_n^{n+1}\bigl(n^{-s}-x^{-s}\bigr)\dd x.\end{gathered}
\]
Each $\phi_n$ is entire, by Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(b). The bound in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:dirseries}(b) gives $|\phi_n(s)|\le|s|n^{-\sigma-1}$. On every closed disc in $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ the comparison series $\sum|s|n^{-\sigma-1}$ converges, so $\sum\phi_n$ converges uniformly there and defines a holomorphic function (Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(a)). At $s=1$, the partial sum $\sum_{n\le N}\phi_n(1)=H_N-\log(N+1)$ tends to $\gamma$ (Volume~1, Chapter~3).
\end{proof}

\section{Riemann's functional equation}

Riemann wrote $n^{-s}$ as an integral of the Gaussian $e^{-\pi n^2t}$, summed over $n$, and used the transformation law of Jacobi's theta function from Chapter~\ref{ch:transform}. The memoir is the paper quoted at the head of the chapter: \emph{\"Uber die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Gr\"osse}, November 1859. The argument below is the one in that paper.

\portrait{riemann}{Bernhard Riemann (1826--1866). Photograph, 1863. Public domain.}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mellingauss}
For $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ and $n\ge1$, $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi n^2t}t^{s/2-1}\dd t=\pi^{-s/2}\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac s2\Bigr)\,n^{-s}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Substitute $u=\pi n^2t$. Powers of positive reals multiply, so $t^{s/2-1}=(\pi n^2)^{1-s/2}u^{s/2-1}$. The integral becomes $(\pi n^2)^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:gammac}(a).
\end{proof}

Let $\omega(t)=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\pi n^2t}=\frac{\vartheta(t)-1}2$ for $t>0$, where $\vartheta$ is Jacobi's theta function of Corollary~\ref{cor:theta}. For $t\ge1$, one has $\omega(t)\le\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\pi nt}\le2e^{-\pi t}$, because $e^{-\pi}<\frac12$. For $0<t\le1$, the theta transformation gives $\omega(t)\le\frac12\vartheta(t)=\frac12 t^{-1/2}\vartheta(1/t)\le\frac12\vartheta(1)\,t^{-1/2}$.

Riemann multiplied $\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)$ by the polynomial $\frac12 s(s-1)$, which cancels the poles, and called the product $\xi$. In this series the completed zeta function is $\xi$.

\begin{theorem}[Riemann, 1859]\label{thm:functional}
Let $\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}\xi(s)&=\frac12+\frac{s(s-1)}2\eqbr\quad\cdot\int_1^\infty\omega(t)\bigl(t^{s/2}+t^{(1-s)/2}\bigr)\frac{\mathrm dt}t.\end{aligned}
\]
The integral is an entire function of $s$, so $\xi$ extends to an entire function, and
\[
\xi(1-s)=\xi(s).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\textit{Setup.} Let $\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma>1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:mellingauss} gives $\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)=\sum_n\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi n^2t}t^{s/2-1}\dd t$. The partial sums of $\sum_ne^{-\pi n^2t}t^{s/2-1}$ converge to $\omega(t)t^{s/2-1}$ uniformly on every closed bounded subinterval of $\left]0,\infty\right[$. They are dominated by $\omega(t)t^{\sigma/2-1}$. That dominant is integrable: near $0$ it is at most $\frac12\vartheta(1)\,t^{\sigma/2-3/2}$, and $\sigma>1$ makes the exponent greater than $-1$; near $\infty$ it is at most $2e^{-\pi t}t^{\sigma/2-1}$. Corollary~\ref{cor:dct} lets the sum pass inside the integral:
\[
\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\Bigl(\frac s2\Bigr)\zeta(s)=\int_0^\infty\omega(t)\,t^{s/2-1}\dd t.
\]

\textit{The substitution.} Split the integral at $1$. Call $J$ the part over $\left]0,1\right]$, and substitute $t=\frac1u$. The theta transformation $\vartheta(\frac1u)=\sqrt u\,\vartheta(u)$ reads
\[
\omega\Bigl(\frac1u\Bigr)=\sqrt u\,\omega(u)+\frac{\sqrt u}2-\frac12,
\]
so
\[
\begin{aligned}J&=\int_1^\infty\omega\Bigl(\frac1u\Bigr)u^{-s/2-1}\dd u\\&=\int_1^\infty\omega(u)u^{(1-s)/2-1}\dd u\\&\quad+\frac12\int_1^\infty\bigl(u^{-(s+1)/2}-u^{-s/2-1}\bigr)\dd u.\end{aligned}
\]
In the last line, $\int_1^\infty u^{-(s+1)/2}\dd u=\frac2{s-1}$ and $\int_1^\infty u^{-s/2-1}\dd u=\frac2s$. That term equals $\frac12\bigl(\frac2{s-1}-\frac2s\bigr)=\frac1{s(s-1)}$. Add the integral over $[1,\infty[$:
\[
\begin{gathered}\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma\Bigl(\frac s2\Bigr)\zeta(s)=\frac1{s(s-1)}+I(s),\qq I(s)=\int_1^\infty\omega(t)\bigl(t^{s/2}+t^{(1-s)/2}\bigr)\frac{\mathrm dt}t.\end{gathered}
\]
Multiply by $\frac12 s(s-1)$. The formula in the theorem is
\[
\xi(s)=\frac12+\frac12 s(s-1)\,I(s).
\]

\textit{The integral is entire.} On a closed disc where $|\operatorname{Re}s|\le K$, the integrand of $I(s)$ is dominated by $4e^{-\pi t}t^{K/2+1/2}$, which is integrable on $[1,\infty[$. Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(b) says $I$ is entire. The right-hand side $\frac12+\frac12 s(s-1)I(s)$ is therefore entire, and it equals $\xi$ on $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. That equality defines the extension of $\xi$ to an entire function.

\textit{The symmetry.} Replacing $s$ by $1-s$ leaves both $s(s-1)$ and $I(s)$ unchanged, so $\xi(1-s)=\xi(s)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{beauty}
The functional equation is the theta transformation written in the variable $s$. The theta transformation is Poisson summation for the Gaussian, and the Gaussian is its own Fourier transform. The symmetry $s\leftrightarrow 1-s$ of $\zeta$ is the self-duality of the integers under the Fourier transform (Chapter~\ref{ch:transform}), carried across by the integral of Lemma~\ref{lem:mellingauss}.
\end{beauty}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:zetaplane}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\zeta$ extends to a function holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{1\}$, with a simple pole of residue $1$ at $1$. Moreover $\zeta(\overline s)=\overline{\zeta(s)}$.
\item $\zeta(0)=-\frac12$, and $\zeta(-2k)=0$ for every $k\ge1$: these are simple zeros, the \emph{trivial zeros}.
\item For $\operatorname{Re}s<0$, $\zeta(s)=0$ only at the trivial zeros.
\item (asymmetric form) For every $s$ that is not an integer $\le1$,
\[
\zeta(1-s)=2(2\pi)^{-s}\cos\Bigl(\frac{\pi s}2\Bigr)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
(a) Since $\frac s2\Gamma(\frac s2)=\Gamma(\frac s2+1)$, the definition of $\xi$ reads, for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$,
\[
\zeta(s)=\frac{\pi^{s/2}\,\xi(s)}{(s-1)\,\Gamma(\frac s2+1)}.
\]
Here $\xi$ is entire, and so is $\frac1\Gamma$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:gammac}(c)). The right-hand side is holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{1\}$, and it is the extension. At $s=1$ the theorem gives $\xi(1)=\frac12$, and $\Gamma(\frac32)=\frac12\Gamma(\frac12)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$, so the pole is simple with residue $\sqrt\pi\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac2{\sqrt\pi}=1$. For the conjugation, $s\mapsto\overline{\zeta(\overline s)}$ is holomorphic and equals $\zeta$ on $\left]1,\infty\right[$. Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} extends the equality.

(b) At $s=0$ the formula of (a) gives $\zeta(0)=\frac{\xi(0)}{(-1)\Gamma(1)}=-\frac12$, since $\xi(0)=\xi(1)=\frac12$. At $s=-2k$ with $k\ge1$, the point $\frac s2+1=1-k$ is a pole of $\Gamma$, so $\frac1{\Gamma(s/2+1)}$ has a simple zero there and $\zeta(-2k)=0$. The zero is simple because $\xi(-2k)=\xi(1+2k)\ne0$: for real $s>1$ every factor in the definition of $\xi$ is positive.

(c) If $\operatorname{Re}s<0$, then $\operatorname{Re}(1-s)>1$, so $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)\ne0$. In the definition of $\xi(1-s)$ the polynomial factor is not zero, $\Gamma$ has no zeros, and $\zeta(1-s)\ne0$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:mobius}. By the formula of (a), $\zeta(s)=0$ only where $\frac1{\Gamma(s/2+1)}=0$.

(d) First let $s$ be a non-integer. Dividing $\xi(1-s)=\xi(s)$ by $\frac12 s(s-1)$ gives $\pi^{-(1-s)/2}\Gamma(\frac{1-s}2)\zeta(1-s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)$, that is $\zeta(1-s)=\pi^{1/2-s}R(s)\zeta(s)$ with $R(s)=\frac{\Gamma(s/2)}{\Gamma((1-s)/2)}$. The reflection formula gives $\Gamma(\frac{1-s}2)\Gamma(\frac{1+s}2)=\frac\pi{\cos(\pi s/2)}$, and
the duplication formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:duplication}, extended to $\C$ by the identity theorem) gives $\Gamma(\frac s2)\Gamma(\frac{s+1}2)=2^{1-s}\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(s)$. So
$R(s)=\frac{2^{1-s}\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(s)\cos(\pi s/2)}\pi$, which gives the formula. Both of its sides are holomorphic on $\C$ minus the integers $\le1$, a connected open set, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:identity} the formula also holds at the integers $s\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:zetaneg}
For every $k\ge1$, $\displaystyle\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k}$. Thus $\zeta(-1)=-\frac1{12}$, $\zeta(-3)=\frac1{120}$, $\zeta(-5)=-\frac1{252}$, $\zeta(-7)=\frac1{240}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $s=2k$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:zetaplane}(d), where $\cos(\pi k)=(-1)^k$ and $\Gamma(2k)=(2k-1)!$, and use Euler's formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:zeta2k}):
\[
\begin{aligned}\zeta(1-2k)&=\frac{2(-1)^k(2k-1)!}{(2\pi)^{2k}}\eqbr\quad\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{2k}(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!}\eqbr=-\frac{(2k-1)!}{(2k)!}B_{2k}=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k}.\end{aligned}
\]
The values follow from $B_2=\frac16$, $B_4=-\frac1{30}$, $B_6=\frac1{42}$, $B_8=-\frac1{30}$.
\end{proof}

Check two of them by hand. For $k=1$, $-\frac{B_2}{2}=-\frac{1/6}{2}=-\frac1{12}$. For $k=2$, $-\frac{B_4}{4}=-\frac{-1/30}{4}=\frac1{120}$. Together with $\zeta(0)=-\frac12$ from the corollary, these are the three values the rest of the chapter keeps using.

With $\zeta(0)=-\frac12=-B_1(1)$ and $\zeta(-2k)=0=-\frac{B_{2k+1}}{2k+1}$, the single formula $\zeta(-n)=-\frac{B_{n+1}(1)}{n+1}$ covers all $n\ge0$. Exercise~\ref{ex:hurwitz} proves it directly, for the Hurwitz zeta function, without the functional equation. Euler found the values in 1749 by summing divergent series, and he guessed the functional equation from them.

\section{What $1+2+3+\cdots=-\frac1{12}$ means}

The series $\sum n$ diverges. The equation $\zeta(-1)=-\frac1{12}$ says something else: the analytic continuation of $\sum n^{-s}$ takes the value $-\frac1{12}$ at $s=-1$. A smoothed cut-off makes the same number appear as a constant term.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:smoothed}
As $N\to\infty$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-n/N}=N-\frac12+O\Bigl(\frac1N\Bigr),\qq\sum_{n\ge1}ne^{-n/N}=N^2-\frac1{12}+O\Bigl(\frac1{N^2}\Bigr).\end{gathered}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $h=\frac1N$. The first sum is $\frac1{e^h-1}=\frac1h\cdot\frac h{e^h-1}=\frac1h\bigl(1-\frac h2+O(h^2)\bigr)$. The second is $\sum nx^n=\frac x{(1-x)^2}$ at $x=e^{-h}$, that is
$\frac{e^{-h}}{(1-e^{-h})^2}=\frac1{(e^{h/2}-e^{-h/2})^2}=\frac1{4\sinh^2(h/2)}$. Since $\sinh u=u\bigl(1+\frac{u^2}6+O(u^4)\bigr)$, $4\sinh^2\frac h2=h^2\bigl(1+\frac{h^2}{12}+O(h^4)\bigr)$, whose inverse is
$\frac1{h^2}-\frac1{12}+O(h^2)$.
\end{proof}

The term $N^2$ depends on the cut-off. For this exponential cut-off the constant term is $-\frac1{12}=\zeta(-1)$. A sharp cut-off gives $\sum_{n\le N}n=\frac{N^2}2+\frac N2$, and the constant term is absent: the sharp edge is too rough for that constant to show.

For $N=10$ the closed form $\sum_{n\ge1}ne^{-n/10}=e^{-1/10}/(1-e^{-1/10})^2$ equals $99.916708\ldots$, while $10^2-\frac1{12}=99.916666\ldots$. The two differ by $4.2\times10^{-5}$, which is the size of the $O(1/N^2)$ term.

\paragraph{Every power.} The same constant appears for $\sum n^k$, and the proof is one differentiation. For $h>0$ the sum $\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-nh}=\frac1{e^h-1}$ is a power series in $x=e^{-h}$, so it can be differentiated term by term (Theorem~\ref{thm:powerseries}). Each derivative in $h$ brings down a factor $-n$:
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}n^ke^{-nh}=\Bigl(-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dh}\Bigr)^k\frac1{e^h-1}\qquad(k\ge0).
\]
On the other side, the generating function of the Bernoulli numbers (Chapter~\ref{ch:finite}) gives $\frac1{e^h-1}=\frac1h+\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{B_m}{m!}h^{m-1}$ for $0<|h|<2\pi$, and this power series may also be differentiated term by term. Apply $\bigl(-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dh}\bigr)^k$. The term $\frac1h$ becomes $\frac{k!}{h^{k+1}}$. The terms with $1\le m\le k$ are polynomials of degree less than $k$ and disappear. The term $m=k+1$ becomes the constant $(-1)^k\frac{B_{k+1}}{k+1}$. What is left is a power series without constant term, hence $O(h)$. With $h=\frac1N$:
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}n^ke^{-n/N}=k!\,N^{k+1}+(-1)^k\frac{B_{k+1}}{k+1}+O\Bigl(\frac1N\Bigr).
\]
The constant is $\zeta(-k)$ every time. For odd $k$ it is $-\frac{B_{k+1}}{k+1}$, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:zetaneg}. For even $k\ge2$ it is $0$, because $B_{k+1}=0$, and $\zeta(-k)=0$ is a trivial zero. For $k=0$ it is $B_1=-\frac12=\zeta(0)$. So $1+8+27+\cdots$ hides the constant $\zeta(-3)=\frac1{120}$: for $N=100$ the smoothed sum is $6\cdot10^8+0.0083331\ldots$. This constant $\frac1{120}$ is the one that survives in Casimir's 1948 computation of the force between two conducting plates, where the divergent sum is a sum of cubes.

For other smooth cut-offs $\sum n^k\eta(\frac nN)$ the divergent part changes and the constant $\zeta(-k)$ does not (see Tao's essay in the Sources). That claim is not proved in this chapter, and nothing later in the series uses it.

\begin{think}
Is $1-1+1-1+\cdots=\frac12$? Compute $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^ne^{-n/N}$. Then compare with $\eta(0)$, where $\eta$ is the alternating zeta function of
Exercise~\ref{ex:eta}.
\end{think}

The sum is a geometric series: $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^ne^{-n/N}=\frac1{1+e^{-1/N}}$. For $N=10$ this equals $0.524979$. As $N\to\infty$ it tends to $\frac12$. Exercise~\ref{ex:eta} and $\zeta(0)=-\frac12$ give $\eta(0)=(1-2^{1-0})\zeta(0)=\frac12$. The smoothed alternating sum tends to $\eta(0)$, in the same way that the smoothed sum of the positive integers hides the constant $\zeta(-1)$.

\section{The zeros}

By Corollaries~\ref{cor:mobius} and~\ref{cor:zetaplane}, every zero of $\zeta$ other than the trivial ones lies in the \emph{critical strip} $0\le\operatorname{Re}s\le1$. The zeros are symmetric in the real axis, because $\zeta(\overline s)=\overline{\zeta(s)}$. They are symmetric in the \emph{critical line} $\operatorname{Re}s=\frac12$, because of the functional equation and because $\Gamma$ has no zeros. Exercise~\ref{ex:eta} shows there is no zero on the real segment $\left]0,1\right[$.

Figure~\ref{fig:rw10-strip} marks the trivial zeros at the negative even integers, and the pole at $1$ on the edge of the strip. Figure~\ref{fig:plane} shows the first few zeros inside the strip, as computed points.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch10-rewrite-strip}
\caption{The critical strip $0\le\operatorname{Re}s\le1$, the pole of $\zeta$ at $1$, and the trivial zeros at $-2,-4,-6,-8$.}
\label{fig:rw10-strip}
\end{figure}

On the critical line, the functional equation gives $\xi(\frac12+it)=\xi(\frac12-it)=\overline{\xi(\frac12+it)}$, because $\xi(\overline s)=\overline{\xi(s)}$ in the same way as $\zeta$. So $\xi$ is real-valued on the critical line. Write $\Gamma(\frac14+\frac{it}2)=|\Gamma(\frac14+\frac{it}2)|e^{i\alpha_0(t)}$ with $\alpha_0$ continuous, and set $\alpha(t)=\alpha_0(t)-\frac t2\log\pi$. At $s=\frac12+it$ one has $\frac12 s(s-1)=-\frac12(t^2+\frac14)$, and
\[
\begin{gathered}\xi\Bigl(\frac12+it\Bigr)=-\frac{t^2+\frac14}{2\pi^{1/4}}\Bigl|\Gamma\Bigl(\frac14+\frac{it}2\Bigr)\Bigr|\,Z(t),\qq Z(t)=e^{i\alpha(t)}\zeta\Bigl(\frac12+it\Bigr).\end{gathered}
\]
Hardy's function $Z$ is real-valued, $|Z(t)|=|\zeta(\frac12+it)|$, and every sign change of $Z$ is a zero of $\zeta$ on the critical line (Figure~\ref{fig:hardyz}). Zeros on that line are certified by the intermediate value theorem, once $Z$ has been computed at two points of opposite sign. The phase $\alpha$ is often called the Riemann--Siegel theta function. This series keeps $\theta$ for Chebyshev's function and $\vartheta$ for Jacobi's theta function, so the phase stays $\alpha$.

The ordinates of zeros printed in this chapter are computed values, rounded to two decimals. Riemann computed the first few by hand. Odlyzko's tables, cited in the Sources, give millions of them to many digits. A printed ordinate is not a theorem.

\caption{Hardy's function $Z(t)$ (charcoal), real-valued with $|Z(t)|=|\zeta(\frac12+it)|$ (grey), for $0\le t\le50$. Its sign changes are zeros of $\zeta$ on the
critical line: the ten zeros with $0<t<50$, at $t=14.13$, $21.02$, $25.01$, $30.42$, $32.94$, $37.59$, $40.92$, $43.33$, $48.01$, $49.77$ (rust).}
\label{fig:hardyz}
\end{figure}

\begin{remark}[the Riemann hypothesis]
Riemann conjectured that all non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line. Hardy proved in 1914 that infinitely many do, and
Selberg in 1942 that a positive proportion do. The first $10^{13}$ zeros have been computed and lie on the line (Gourdon, 2004), and the
hypothesis has been verified for all zeros with $0<t\le3\cdot10^{12}$ (Platt and Trudgian, 2021). These are computations, not
theorems about all zeros. The conjecture remains open, and is one of
the Millennium Prize Problems. Its arithmetic meaning, and the proof that there are no zeros on the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ (which gives the
prime number theorem), are the subject of Volume~6.
\end{remark}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Unique factorisation, written with $n^{-s}$, is the Euler product (Theorem~\ref{thm:eulerproduct}). It already shows that $\sum\frac1p$ diverges, and that two integers are coprime with probability $\frac6{\pi^2}$. Poisson summation for the Gaussian, passed through the integral of Lemma~\ref{lem:mellingauss}, is the functional equation. In the form proved here,
\[
\xi(s)=\frac12+\frac12 s(s-1)\,I(s),
\]
the function $\xi$ is entire, and $\xi(1-s)=\xi(s)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:functional}). From that equation came the continuation of $\zeta$ to $\C\setminus\{1\}$, the trivial zeros, the values $\zeta(0)=-\frac12$ and $\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:zetaneg}), and a meaning for $1+2+3+\cdots=-\frac1{12}$ that does not claim the series $\sum n$ converges.

What was not proved is any statement about the zeros inside the critical strip beyond their symmetry. The ordinates drawn in the figures are computed values. Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} continues $\zeta$ a second time, by the Euler--Maclaurin formula, with no theta function in the argument. Chapter~\ref{ch:hr} applies the Poisson symmetry to partitions. Volume~6 then spends the product and the functional equation on the primes: $\zeta(1+it)\ne0$ gives the prime number theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~5), and the zeros of $\xi$ give the error term.
\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:tau}
Show that for $\operatorname{Re}s>2$, $\sum\frac{\tau(n)}{n^s}=\zeta(s)^2$, $\sum\frac{\sigma(n)}{n^s}=\zeta(s)\zeta(s-1)$ and $\sum\frac{|\mu(n)|}{n^s}=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:squarefree}
Let $Q(N)$ be the number of squarefree integers in $\{1,\dots,N\}$. Show that $Q(N)=\sum_{d\le\sqrt N}\mu(d)\lfloor N/d^2\rfloor$ and that $\frac{Q(N)}N\to\frac6{\pi^2}$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:eta}
Let $\eta(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}n^{-s}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\eta$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ and that $\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ with $s\ne1$.
\item Deduce that $\zeta(\sigma)<0$ for $0<\sigma<1$, and recover the residue of $\zeta$ at $1$ from $\eta(1)=\log2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:primesum}
Show that $\log\zeta(\sigma)=\log\frac1{\sigma-1}+O(\sigma-1)$ as $\sigma\to1^+$, and that $\sum_pp^{-\sigma}=\log\frac1{\sigma-1}+O(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:hurwitz}
(The Hurwitz zeta function.) For $0<a\le1$ and $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ let $\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+a)^{-s}$, so that $\zeta(s,1)=\zeta(s)$, and let $g(t)=\frac{e^{-at}}{1-e^{-t}}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,a)=\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}g(t)\dd t$.
\item Show that $tg(t)=\sum_{m\ge0}(-1)^mB_m(a)\frac{t^m}{m!}$ for $|t|<2\pi$.
\item Deduce that $\zeta(s,a)$ extends to a function holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{1\}$, and that $\zeta(-n,a)=-\frac{B_{n+1}(a)}{n+1}$ for every integer $n\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:zeta2fe}
Using only Exercise~\ref{ex:hurwitz} with $a=1$ and the asymmetric functional equation, find $\zeta(2)$ and $\zeta(4)$. Explain why this gives a new proof
of Theorem~\ref{thm:zeta2k}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Journey]\label{pb:gausskuzmin}
\emph{The Gauss--Kuzmin theorem.} For $0<y<1$ let $Ty=\frac1y-\lfloor\frac1y\rfloor$ be the fractional part of $\frac1y$; this is the \emph{Gauss map}. An
irrational $x$ in $]0,1[$ has the continued fraction $x=[0;a_1,a_2,\dots]$ of Volume~2, Chapter~9, and its partial quotients are
$a_{n+1}=\lfloor1/T^nx\rfloor$. Which values do they take, and how often? Let $x$ be uniform in $]0,1[$ (it is irrational with probability $1$), let
$m_n(t)=P(T^nx<t)$ for $0\le t\le1$ and $f_n=m_n'$, so $f_0=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $m_{n+1}(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\bigl(m_n(\frac1k)-m_n(\frac1{k+t})\bigr)$, and deduce
$f_{n+1}(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{(k+t)^2}f_n\bigl(\frac1{k+t}\bigr)$, with every $f_n$ continuously differentiable.
\item Let $\phi_n(t)=(1+t)f_n(t)$. Show that $\phi_{n+1}(t)=\sum_kP_k(t)\phi_n(u_k)$ with $u_k=\frac1{k+t}$ and
$P_k(t)=\frac{1+t}{(k+t)(k+t+1)}$, and that $\sum_kP_k(t)=1$.
\item Show that $\sup|\phi_{n+1}'|\le c\,\sup|\phi_n'|$ with
\[
c=\frac12+2\sum_{k\ge2}\frac1{k^3(k+1)}+\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{k-1}{k(k+1)^2}=2\zeta(3)-\frac32=0.9041\ldots
\]
\item Deduce that $\Bigl|f_n(t)-\frac1{(1+t)\log2}\Bigr|\le\frac{c^n}{1+t}$, and conclude: $|m_n(t)-\log_2(1+t)|\le c^n\log(1+t)$.
\item Deduce that $P(a_n=k)\to\log_2\frac{(k+1)^2}{k(k+2)}$ for $x$ uniform.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:levy}
\emph{How fast the denominators grow.} Let $x$ be uniform in $]0,1[$, $\frac{p_n}{q_n}$ its convergents, and $T$ the Gauss map of Exercise~\ref{pb:gausskuzmin}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $|q_nx-p_n|=x\,Tx\cdots T^nx$, and that this number lies between $\frac1{2q_{n+1}}$ and $\frac1{q_{n+1}}$.
\item Show that $\int_0^1\frac{-\log t}{1+t}\,dt=\frac{\pi^2}{12}$.
\item Using Exercise~\ref{pb:gausskuzmin}, prove that
\[
\mathbb E[\log q_n]=\frac{\pi^2}{12\log2}\,n+O(1),\qquad\frac{\pi^2}{12\log2}=1.18656\ldots
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:rw10a}
Show that the partial Euler product for $\zeta(2)$ through the prime $5$ equals $\frac{25}{16}$. Compute the gap $\frac{\pi^2}6-\frac{25}{16}$ to five decimals, and compare it with the height of the point above $p=5$ in Figure~\ref{fig:rw10-euler}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw10b}
Proposition~\ref{prop:smoothed} says $\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-n/N}=N-\frac12+O(1/N)$. For $N=10$, compute the sum from the closed form $1/(e^{1/10}-1)$ and compare it with $9.5$. The next term in the expansion of $1/(e^h-1)$ is $h/12$, so the difference should be near $1/120$.

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw10c}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:tau}
\idea Write each function as a convolution of functions whose Dirichlet series are known, and multiply.
\writeup All series converge absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}s>2$, since $\tau(n)\le n$ and $\sigma(n)\le n^2$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:dirmult}(a), $\tau=\mathbf 1*\mathbf 1$ gives $\zeta(s)^2$, and
$\sigma=\operatorname{id}*\mathbf 1$ gives $\zeta(s-1)\zeta(s)$, since $\sum n\cdot n^{-s}=\zeta(s-1)$. For $|\mu|$, the Euler product of Theorem~\ref{thm:eulerproduct} has factors $1+p^{-s}=\frac{1-p^{-2s}}{1-p^{-s}}$,
and the product of the numerators is $\frac1{\zeta(2s)}$, that of the denominators' inverses $\zeta(s)$. (This one holds for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:squarefree}
\idea Detect squarefree numbers with M\"obius, exactly as Volume~2, Exercise~4.8, detected coprime pairs, and let Corollary~\ref{cor:mobius} supply the constant.
\writeup Write $n=m^2k$ with $k$ squarefree; then $d^2\mid n$ iff $d\mid m$, so $\sum_{d^2\mid n}\mu(d)=\sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)=[m=1]$ by Volume~2, Chapter~4 ($\mu*\mathbf 1=\varepsilon$), and $m=1$ means $n$ is squarefree.
Summing over $n\le N$ and exchanging the sums, $Q(N)=\sum_{d\le\sqrt N}\mu(d)\lfloor N/d^2\rfloor$. Replacing $\lfloor N/d^2\rfloor$ by $N/d^2$ costs at most $1$ per term, so
\[
\Bigl|Q(N)-N\sum_{d\le\sqrt N}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}\Bigr|\le\sqrt N.
\]
Dividing by $N$, $\frac{Q(N)}N\to\sum_d\frac{\mu(d)}{d^2}=\frac1{\zeta(2)}=\frac6{\pi^2}$. So about $61\%$ of integers are squarefree, the same proportion as coprime pairs.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:eta}
\idea The alternating signs give bounded partial sums, which is all Proposition~\ref{prop:dirseries}(b) needs; and removing the even terms twice turns
$\zeta$ into $\eta$.
\writeup (a) The partial sums of $(-1)^{n-1}$ are $0$ or $1$, so $\eta$ is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$. For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, $\zeta(s)-\eta(s)=2\sum_n(2n)^{-s}=2^{1-s}\zeta(s)$, so
$\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$. Both sides are holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ minus $\{1\}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:zetastrip}), a connected set, so the identity holds there.

(b) For real $0<\sigma<1$, $\eta(\sigma)=(1-2^{-\sigma})+(3^{-\sigma}-4^{-\sigma})+\cdots>0$, a series of positive terms. And $1-2^{1-\sigma}<0$. So $\zeta(\sigma)=\frac{\eta(\sigma)}{1-2^{1-\sigma}}<0$; in
particular $\zeta(\frac12)\approx-1.4604$. Near $1$, $1-2^{1-s}=(s-1)\log2+O((s-1)^2)$, so $(s-1)\zeta(s)=\frac{(s-1)\eta(s)}{1-2^{1-s}}\to\frac{\eta(1)}{\log2}=1$, using
$\eta(1)=1-\frac12+\frac13-\cdots=\log2$ (Volume~1, Chapter~3) and the continuity of $\eta$ at $1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:primesum}
\idea $\zeta(\sigma)$ is squeezed between two integrals, and its logarithm is $\sum_pp^{-\sigma}$ up to a bounded error.
\writeup Comparing the sum with integrals of the decreasing function $x^{-\sigma}$, $\int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}\dd x\le\zeta(\sigma)\le1+\int_1^\infty x^{-\sigma}\dd x$, that is
$\frac1{\sigma-1}\le\zeta(\sigma)\le\frac\sigma{\sigma-1}$. Taking logarithms, $0\le\log\zeta(\sigma)-\log\frac1{\sigma-1}\le\log\sigma\le\sigma-1$. Next, by Corollary~\ref{cor:primesdiverge}'s inequalities,
$\sum_pp^{-\sigma}\le\log\zeta(\sigma)\le\sum_pp^{-\sigma}+2\zeta(2)$. Combining, $\bigl|\sum_pp^{-\sigma}-\log\frac1{\sigma-1}\bigr|\le2\zeta(2)+1$ for $1<\sigma\le2$. Volume~6 (preview) turns this into Mertens's
theorem $\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+O(1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:hurwitz}
\idea Riemann's second method: the Mellin transform turns the sum into an integral of $t^{s-1}$ against a function whose Taylor
expansion at $0$ has Bernoulli coefficients. Each term of that expansion produces one pole of $\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,a)$, and dividing by $\Gamma$
leaves a finite value at each negative integer, read off from the Bernoulli polynomials.
\writeup (a) As in Lemma~\ref{lem:mellingauss}, $\Gamma(s)(n+a)^{-s}=\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}e^{-(n+a)t}\dd t$. Summing over $n$, the partial sums of $\sum_nt^{s-1}e^{-(n+a)t}$ tend to $t^{s-1}g(t)$
uniformly on closed bounded subintervals of $\left]0,\infty\right[$ and are dominated by $t^{\sigma-1}g(t)$, which is integrable for $\sigma>1$ since $g(t)\le\frac{e^{-at}}t\cdot\frac t{1-e^{-t}}=O(t^{-1})$
at $0$ and $g(t)=O(e^{-at})$ at $\infty$. Corollary~\ref{cor:dct} gives the formula.

(b) $tg(t)=\frac{te^{(1-a)t}}{e^t-1}=\sum B_m(1-a)\frac{t^m}{m!}$ by the definition of the Bernoulli polynomials (Chapter~\ref{ch:finite}), and this power series converges for $|t|<2\pi$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:radius}, the only singularities being at $2\pi i\Z\setminus\{0\}$). Replacing $t$ by $-t$ in $\frac{te^{xt}}{e^t-1}$ with $x=1-a$ gives $\frac{-te^{-(1-a)t}}{e^{-t}-1}=\frac{te^{at}}{e^t-1}$,
so $B_m(1-a)(-1)^m=B_m(a)$.

(c) Let $c_m=\frac{(-1)^mB_m(a)}{m!}$ and fix $M\ge1$. Then $r_M(t)=g(t)-\sum_{m=0}^Mc_mt^{m-1}$ is $O(t^M)$ on $\left]0,1\right]$ by (b). For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s,a)&=\sum_{m=0}^M\frac{c_m}{s+m-1}+\int_0^1t^{s-1}r_M(t)\dd t\eqbr\quad+\int_1^\infty t^{s-1}g(t)\dd t,\end{aligned}
\]
using $\int_0^1t^{s+m-2}\dd t=\frac1{s+m-1}$. The last integral is entire and the middle one is holomorphic for $\operatorname{Re}s>-M$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam}(b). So the right
side is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s>-M$ except for simple poles at $s=1-m$, $0\le m\le M$. Multiplying by the entire $\frac1\Gamma$, which vanishes at $0,-1,-2,\dots$, the poles at
$s=1-m$ with $m\ge1$ disappear; only $s=1$ remains. As $M$ is arbitrary, $\zeta(s,a)$ is holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{1\}$. At $s=-n$ the only singular term is $\frac{c_{n+1}}{s+n}$,
and $\frac1{\Gamma(s)}=(-1)^nn!\,(s+n)+O((s+n)^2)$ since $\Gamma$ has residue $\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}$ there (Theorem~\ref{thm:gammac}(b)). Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}\zeta(-n,a)&=(-1)^nn!\,c_{n+1}\eqbr=(-1)^nn!\frac{(-1)^{n+1}B_{n+1}(a)}{(n+1)!}\eqbr=-\frac{B_{n+1}(a)}{n+1}.\end{aligned}
\]
For $a=1$: $\zeta(-n)=-\frac{B_{n+1}(1)}{n+1}$, which is $-\frac12$ for $n=0$ ($B_1(1)=\frac12$), and $-\frac{B_{n+1}}{n+1}$ for $n\ge1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:bernoulli}(c).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:gausskuzmin}
\idea The transfer operator replaces $\phi_n$ by an average of its values at the points $u_k$, and these points are squeezed
together: $|u_k'|=(k+t)^{-2}$. Averaging over squeezed points contracts the Lipschitz constant, and a function with tiny
Lipschitz constant is almost constant; the constant is forced by normalisation.

\writeup (a) Let $0<y<1$ be irrational and $k=\lfloor\frac1y\rfloor\ge1$, so $Ty=\frac1y-k$. Then $Ty<t$ means $k\le\frac1y<k+t$, that is
$y\in\,]\frac1{k+t},\frac1k]$. Taking $y=T^nx$: $T^{n+1}x<t$ if and only if $T^nx$ lies in one of the disjoint intervals $]\frac1{k+t},\frac1k]$, $k\ge1$.
Suppose $m_n$ has a continuous density $f_n$ bounded by $M_n$ on $[0,1]$ (true for $n=0$). Then single points have probability $0$, the
interval $]\frac1{k+t},\frac1k]$ has probability $m_n(\frac1k)-m_n(\frac1{k+t})$, and summing over $k$ gives the formula for $m_{n+1}$. Each term is a $C^1$ function
of $t$ with derivative $\frac{f_n(1/(k+t))}{(k+t)^2}$, bounded by $M_nk^{-2}$. So the series of derivatives converges uniformly on $[0,1]$, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:uniform}(d) $m_{n+1}$ is of class $C^1$ with the stated derivative $f_{n+1}$, continuous and bounded by $M_n\zeta(2)$. This proves the
recursion for every $n$ by induction. The same argument one derivative higher, with the series $\sum_k\bigl(\frac{-2f_n(u_k)}{(k+t)^3}-\frac{f_n'(u_k)}{(k+t)^4}\bigr)$ where $u_k=\frac1{k+t}$,
shows by induction that every $f_n$ is of class $C^1$ with bounded derivative, since $f_0=1$.

(b) $f_n(u_k)=\phi_n(u_k)/(1+u_k)$ and $1+u_k=\frac{k+t+1}{k+t}$, so
\[
\frac{(1+t)f_n(u_k)}{(k+t)^2}=\frac{1+t}{(k+t)(k+t+1)}\phi_n(u_k)=P_k(t)\phi_n(u_k).
\]
Also $\sum_kP_k(t)=(1+t)\sum_k\bigl(\frac1{k+t}-\frac1{k+t+1}\bigr)=1$.

(c) Let $L_n=\sup_{[0,1]}|\phi_n'|$, finite by (a). Differentiating (b) term by term (justified as in (a)),
\[
\phi_{n+1}'(t)=\sum_kP_k'(t)\bigl(\phi_n(u_k)-\phi_n(u_1)\bigr)-\sum_kP_k(t)\frac{\phi_n'(u_k)}{(k+t)^2},
\]
where we subtracted $\phi_n(u_1)\sum_kP_k'=0$. A computation gives
$P_k'(t)=\frac{k^2-k-(1+t)^2}{(k+t)^2(k+t+1)^2}$. For $k=1$ the first term vanishes. For $k\ge2$, $|k^2-k-(1+t)^2|\le k^2$, so
$|P_k'|\le\frac1{(k+1)^2}$, and $|\phi_n(u_k)-\phi_n(u_1)|\le L_n(u_1-u_k)=L_n\frac{k-1}{(1+t)(k+t)}\le L_n\frac{k-1}k$. For the second
sum, $\frac{P_k(t)}{(k+t)^2}=\frac{1+t}{(k+t)^3(k+t+1)}$ is at most $\frac12$ for $k=1$ (it equals $\frac1{(1+t)^2(2+t)}$) and at most
$\frac2{k^3(k+1)}$ for $k\ge2$. Hence $L_{n+1}\le cL_n$ with $c$ as stated. With $\frac1{k^3(k+1)}=\frac1{k^3}-\frac1{k^2}+\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}$
and $\frac{k-1}{k(k+1)^2}=\frac1{(k+1)^2}-\frac1{k(k+1)^2}$, $\frac1{k(k+1)^2}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}-\frac1{(k+1)^2}$, the sums are
$\sum_{k\ge2}\frac1{k^3(k+1)}=\zeta(3)-\zeta(2)+\frac12$ and $\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{k-1}{k(k+1)^2}=2\zeta(2)-3$, so $c=\frac12+2\zeta(3)-2\zeta(2)+1+2\zeta(2)-3=2\zeta(3)-\frac32=0.9041\ldots$

(d) $\phi_0=1+t$ has $L_0=1$, so $L_n\le c^n$ and $\max\phi_n-\min\phi_n\le c^n$. Since $\int_0^1f_n=1$,
$\int_0^1\phi_n(t)\frac{dt}{1+t}=1$: the number $\frac1{\log2}$ is the average of $\phi_n$ for the probability $\frac{dt}{(1+t)\log2}$,
so it lies between $\min\phi_n$ and $\max\phi_n$, and $|\phi_n(t)-\frac1{\log2}|\le c^n$. Divide by $1+t$, and integrate from $0$ to $t$:
$|m_n(t)-\log_2(1+t)|\le c^n\log(1+t)$.

(e) $P(a_{n+1}=k)=P(T^nx\in\,]\frac1{k+1},\frac1k])=m_n(\frac1k)-m_n(\frac1{k+1})$, which by (d) tends to
$\log_2(1+\frac1k)-\log_2(1+\frac1{k+1})$.

\tools Volume~2, Chapter~9 (continued fractions); Lemma~\ref{lem:uniform}(d) (differentiating a series term by term); the values $\zeta(2)$ and $\zeta(3)$ only as names for two convergent sums.
\leads Contraction arguments of this kind go back to Kuzmin (1928) and L\'evy (1929). The constant $c$ here is far from the
truth: Wirsing (1974) found the exact rate $0.3036630029\ldots$, the second eigenvalue of the transfer
operator on a suitable space. Babenko (1978) described the whole spectrum. Mayer later related this operator to the Selberg zeta
function of the modular group.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:levy}
\idea The product $x\,Tx\cdots T^nx$ is both the approximation error of the $n$-th convergent and a product of the values of a
function along the orbit; taking logarithms turns it into a sum, whose terms have, by Gauss--Kuzmin, almost the same law.

\writeup (a) The complete quotient after $a_n$ is $1/T^nx$, so by the formula for $x$ in terms of a complete quotient (Volume~2, Chapter~9), $x=\frac{p_n/T^nx+p_{n-1}}{q_n/T^nx+q_{n-1}}=\frac{p_n+p_{n-1}T^nx}{q_n+q_{n-1}T^nx}$.
Hence, with $p_nq_{n-1}-p_{n-1}q_n=\pm1$,
\[
\beta_n:=|q_nx-p_n|=\frac{T^nx}{q_n+q_{n-1}T^nx}.
\]
Applied at rank $n-1$, and using $T^{n-1}x=\frac1{a_n+T^nx}$ and $q_n=a_nq_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$, this gives $\beta_{n-1}=\frac1{q_n+q_{n-1}T^nx}$;
so $\beta_n=T^nx\cdot\beta_{n-1}$. With $\beta_0=x$ (as $p_0=0$, $q_0=1$), $\beta_n=x\,Tx\cdots T^nx$, and the formula for $\beta_n$ at rank
$n+1$, $\beta_n=\frac1{q_{n+1}+q_nT^{n+1}x}$, puts it between $\frac1{2q_{n+1}}$ and $\frac1{q_{n+1}}$ (as $q_n\le q_{n+1}$).

(b) $\int_0^1(-\log t)t^m\,dt=\frac1{(m+1)^2}$ (integrate by parts). Write $\frac1{1+t}=\sum_{m<M}(-t)^m+\frac{(-t)^M}{1+t}$. The remainder contributes at most
$\int_0^1(-\log t)t^M\,dt=\frac1{(M+1)^2}$, which tends to $0$. So
$\int_0^1\frac{-\log t}{1+t}dt=\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m}{(m+1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:zetavalues}).

(c) By (a), $\log q_{n+1}=\sum_{j=0}^n(-\log T^jx)+\delta$ with $-\log2\le\delta\le0$. By Exercise~\ref{pb:gausskuzmin}(d), $T^jx$ has a density
$f_j$ with $|f_j(t)-\frac1{(1+t)\log2}|\le c^j$, where $c=2\zeta(3)-\frac32<1$, so
\[
\Bigl|\mathbb E[-\log T^jx]-\frac{\pi^2}{12\log2}\Bigr|\le c^j\int_0^1(-\log t)\,dt=c^j,
\]
using (b). Summing over $j\le n$, $\mathbb E[\log q_{n+1}]=(n+1)\frac{\pi^2}{12\log2}+O\bigl(\frac1{1-c}+\log2\bigr)$.

\tools Volume~2, Chapter~9 (convergents); Chapter~\ref{ch:series} (series for $\frac{\pi^2}{12}$); Exercise~\ref{pb:gausskuzmin}.
\leads Khinchin (1935) proved that $\frac1n\log q_n$ has the same limit for almost every $x$, and L\'evy (1936) found its value, $\frac{\pi^2}{12\log2}$. For Euclid's algorithm on rationals,
Heilbronn (1969) and Dixon showed that the mean number of division steps for $\frac ab$ with $a<b$ is $\frac{12\log2}{\pi^2}\log b+O(1)$:
the reciprocal of L\'evy's constant, as (c) suggests, since each step multiplies the denominator by about $e^{1.1866}$. Porter found
the next term.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw10d}
\idea The functional equation carries $\zeta(4)$ to $\zeta(-3)$. The Bernoulli value $\zeta(-3)=\frac1{120}$ is what falls out once $\pi^4$ cancels.
\writeup Put $s=4$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:zetaplane}(d). Then $\cos(2\pi)=1$, $\Gamma(4)=3!=6$, and $\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90$, so
