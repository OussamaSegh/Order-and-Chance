\chapter{Finite Calculus}\label{ch:finite}

\begin{center}\itshape
The sum $1+2+\dots+n$ is $\frac{n(n+1)}2$.\\
What is $1^5+\dots+n^5$, and is there a method rather than a formula to memorise?
\end{center}

Jacob Bernoulli's \emph{Ars Conjectandi} was printed in 1713, eight years after he died. He allowed himself one boast. His method, he wrote, had added the tenth powers of the first thousand integers in ``less than half of a quarter of an hour'':
\[
91\,409\,924\,241\,424\,243\,424\,241\,924\,242\,500.
\]
Every digit checks. The speed was not arithmetic. It was a calculus for sums, as complete as the calculus for areas.

\portrait{jbernoulli}{Jacob Bernoulli (1655--1705). Portrait by his brother Nicolaus Bernoulli, 1687. Public domain.}

An integral $\int_a^bf$ equals $F(b)-F(a)$ once $F'=f$. A sum has the same shape. Write $\Delta F(x)=F(x+1)-F(x)$. If $\Delta F=f$, then $\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}f(k)=F(b)-F(a)$. Three things change with the shift. Ordinary powers $x^m$ difference badly. Falling powers $x(x-1)\cdots(x-m+1)$ difference cleanly. The product rule picks up a shift, $e^x$ becomes $2^x$, and $\ln x$ becomes the harmonic number $H_x$.

The sums this calculus is built for are $\sum k^p$. Stirling numbers turn an ordinary power into falling powers, and the same numbers count set partitions and cycles of permutations. Bernoulli numbers then put every power sum into one formula, Faulhaber's formula. Those numbers are the rational part of $\zeta(2k)$ in Chapter~\ref{ch:series}, and they return in the Euler--Maclaurin formula of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics}.

\begin{think}
The exponential equals its own derivative. Which function $f$ on the integers satisfies $\Delta f=f$ and $f(0)=1$?
\end{think}

\section{The difference operator}

\begin{definition}
For a function $f$ defined on $\R$ (or on $\Z$), its \emph{difference} is $\Delta f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x)$. For integers
$a\le b$ we write $\sum_a^bf=\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}f(k)$, a sum over the half-open range $a\le k<b$ (it is $0$ when $a=b$).
\end{definition}

The half-open range is what makes the pieces fit. $\sum_a^bf+\sum_b^cf=\sum_a^cf$, just as for integrals, and the next theorem has no leftover term.

\begin{theorem}[fundamental theorem of finite calculus]\label{thm:ftfc}
If $\Delta F=f$ on the integers, then $\sum_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)$ for all integers $a\le b$. Conversely, if
$\Delta F=\Delta G$ on $\Z$, then $F-G$ is constant on $\Z$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\sum_{k=a}^{b-1}\bigl(F(k+1)-F(k)\bigr)$ telescopes to $F(b)-F(a)$. If $\Delta(F-G)=0$ then $(F-G)(k+1)=(F-G)(k)$ for
every integer $k$, so $F-G$ takes the same value at all integers.
\end{proof}

Check it on a sum you know. Take $F(x)=\frac{x(x-1)}2$. Then $\Delta F(x)=\frac{(x+1)x-x(x-1)}2=x$, so
$0+1+\dots+(n-1)=F(n)-F(0)=\frac{n(n-1)}2$. No induction, and no pairing.

The question before the section is the same shape of problem. Try $f(x)=2^x$. Then $\Delta 2^x=2^{x+1}-2^x=2^x$, and $f(0)=1$. So $2^x$ equals its own difference. For a general base, $\Delta c^x=(c-1)c^x$.

Every sum is now a search for an antidifference. Ordinary powers are the first guess, and they come out messy: $\Delta x^2=2x+1$ and $\Delta x^3=3x^2+3x+1$, while $(x^m)'=mx^{m-1}$. Figure~\ref{fig:rw01-edge} is the whole table for the squares. The second differences are the constant $2$, and the first differences are the odd numbers $1,3,5,7$, so the extra piece is linear. Change the powers.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{falling powers} are $x^{\underline 0}=1$ and $x^{\underline m}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-m+1)$ for $m\ge1$ ($m$ factors).
The \emph{negative falling powers} are $x^{\underline{-m}}=\dfrac1{(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+m)}$ for $m\ge1$.
\end{definition}

When $n$ is an integer and $0\le m\le n$, the falling power $n^{\underline m}=n!/(n-m)!$ counts ordered choices of $m$ objects from $n$. Dividing by $m!$ gives the polynomial $\binom xm=x^{\underline m}/m!$. The negative powers are built so that the rule $x^{\underline{m+n}}=x^{\underline m}(x-m)^{\underline n}$ still holds for negative exponents: cancel the factors that appear in both the numerator and the denominator.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:falling}
For every integer $m$ (positive, zero or negative), $\Delta x^{\underline m}=m\,x^{\underline{m-1}}$. In particular,
for $m\ge0$ and integers $n\ge0$,
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^{\underline m}=\frac{n^{\underline{m+1}}}{m+1},\qq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom km=\binom n{m+1}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $m\ge1$, $x^{\underline m}=x^{\underline{m-1}}(x-m+1)$ and $(x+1)^{\underline m}=(x+1)x^{\underline{m-1}}$, so
$\Delta x^{\underline m}=x^{\underline{m-1}}\bigl((x+1)-(x-m+1)\bigr)=m\,x^{\underline{m-1}}$. For $m=0$ both sides are
$0$. For $m=-r$ with $r\ge1$, put $P(x)=(x+2)\cdots(x+r)$, so that $x^{\underline{-r}}=\frac1{(x+1)P(x)}$ and
$(x+1)^{\underline{-r}}=\frac1{P(x)(x+r+1)}$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}\Delta x^{\underline{-r}}&=\frac{(x+1)-(x+r+1)}{(x+1)P(x)(x+r+1)}\eqbr=\frac{-r}{(x+1)\cdots(x+r+1)}=-r\,x^{\underline{-r-1}}.\end{aligned}
\]
The sums follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:ftfc} with $F(x)=\frac{x^{\underline{m+1}}}{m+1}$, which vanishes at $0$; the
second is the first divided by $m!$.
\end{proof}

The second sum is the hockey-stick identity in Pascal's triangle. Negative falling powers telescope products in the denominator. With $m=-2$, $\Delta x^{\underline{-1}}=-x^{\underline{-2}}$, so
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{(k+1)(k+2)}=\Bigl[-x^{\underline{-1}}\Bigr]_0^n=1-\frac1{n+1}.
\]
The missing case is $x^{\underline{-1}}$. The rule at $m=0$ only says $\Delta x^{\underline 0}=0$, which is true and useless. So $\frac1{x+1}$ is the one falling power that is not the difference of a falling power, just as $\frac1x$ is the one power that is not a derivative. Its antidifference is the harmonic number. Set $H_0=0$, and $H_n=1+\frac12+\dots+\frac1n$ for $n\ge1$. Then $\Delta H_n=\frac1{n+1}=n^{\underline{-1}}$. The sum in Example~\ref{ex:k2k} starts at $k=0$, where this value is $0$.

We already checked $\Delta 2^x=2^x$. Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} is the dictionary. Figure~\ref{fig:rw01-falling} is the reason the dictionary changes the powers: at each integer the falling square is smaller than the ordinary square by exactly that integer.

\section{Summation by parts}

The product rule picks up a shift. If $Ev(x)=v(x+1)$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}\Delta(uv)(x)&=u(x+1)v(x+1)-u(x)v(x)\eqbr=u(x)\,\Delta v(x)+\Delta u(x)\,v(x+1).\end{aligned}
\]
Expand the right-hand side: $u(x)\bigl(v(x+1)-v(x)\bigr)+\bigl(u(x+1)-u(x)\bigr)v(x+1)$ cancels one copy of $u(x)v(x+1)$ and leaves $u(x+1)v(x+1)-u(x)v(x)$. Sum from $a$ to $b$ and apply Theorem~\ref{thm:ftfc}. That is summation by parts.

\begin{proposition}[summation by parts]\label{prop:byparts}
For functions $u$, $v$ on the integers and integers $a\le b$,
\[
\sum_a^bu\,\Delta v=\bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b-\sum_a^b(\Delta u)\,Ev,
\]
where $[uv]_a^b=u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:k2k}
Sum $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k2^k$ by parts. Take $u(k)=k$ and $\Delta v=2^k$, so $v(k)=2^k$, $\Delta u=1$ and $Ev(k)=2^{k+1}$:
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k2^k&=n2^n-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^{k+1}\eqbr=n2^n-2(2^n-1)\eqbr=(n-2)2^n+2.\end{aligned}
\]
Check $n=3$: $0+2+8=10$, and $(3-2)2^3+2=10$. The same rule with $u=H_k$ and $v(k)=k$ (so $\Delta v=1$) gives
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}H_k=nH_n-n.
\]
\end{example}

Series want the same rule written in partial sums. Put $A_k=a_1+\dots+a_k$ and $A_0=0$, so $a_k=\Delta A_{k-1}$.

\begin{proposition}[Abel summation]\label{prop:abel}
For numbers $a_k$, $b_k$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{k=1}^na_kb_k=A_nb_n-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_k(b_{k+1}-b_k).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\sum_{k=1}^na_kb_k=\sum_{k=1}^n(A_k-A_{k-1})b_k=\sum_{k=1}^nA_kb_k-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A_kb_{k+1}$. Since $A_0=0$, this
is $A_nb_n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_k(b_k-b_{k+1})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dirichlet's test]\label{thm:dirichlet}
Let $(a_k)$ be complex numbers whose partial sums $A_n$ are bounded, $|A_n|\le M$, and let $(b_k)$ be real numbers
that decrease to $0$. Then $\sum a_kb_k$ converges, and $\bigl|\sum_{k\ge1}a_kb_k\bigr|\le Mb_1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In Proposition~\ref{prop:abel}, $|A_nb_n|\le Mb_n\to0$. The series $\sum_kA_k(b_{k+1}-b_k)$ converges absolutely,
since $|A_k(b_{k+1}-b_k)|\le M(b_k-b_{k+1})$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(b_k-b_{k+1})=b_1-b_n\le b_1$. So the partial sums
$\sum_{k=1}^na_kb_k$ converge, and the same bounds give $\bigl|\sum_{k=1}^na_kb_k\bigr|\le Mb_n+M(b_1-b_n)=Mb_1$.
\end{proof}

The choice $a_k=(-1)^{k+1}$ is the alternating series test from Volume~1. The sums that matter next are trigonometric.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sinsum}
For $x\notin2\pi\Z$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{k=1}^n\sin kx=\frac{\sin\frac{nx}2\,\sin\frac{(n+1)x}2}{\sin\frac x2}.
\]
In particular $\bigl|\sum_{k=1}^n\sin kx\bigr|\le\frac1{|\sin(x/2)|}$, and $\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\sin kx}k$ converges for every real $x$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identity $2\sin\frac x2\sin kx=\cos\bigl(k-\frac12\bigr)x-\cos\bigl(k+\frac12\bigr)x$ makes the sum telescope:
\[
\begin{aligned}2\sin\frac x2\sum_{k=1}^n\sin kx&=\cos\frac x2-\cos\Bigl(n+\frac12\Bigr)x\eqbr=2\sin\frac{nx}2\sin\frac{(n+1)x}2,\end{aligned}
\]
the last step by $\cos p-\cos q=2\sin\frac{q+p}2\sin\frac{q-p}2$. For $x\notin2\pi\Z$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichlet} with
$a_k=\sin kx$ and $b_k=\frac1k$ gives the convergence; for $x\in2\pi\Z$ every term is $0$.
\end{proof}

Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier} evaluates the sum: it equals $\frac{\pi-x}2$ for $0<x<2\pi$. The convergence is not absolute. The terms $\frac{|\sin kx|}{k}$ do not form a convergent series. What converges is the cancelled sum that summation by parts makes visible.

\section{Stirling numbers}

Falling powers sum in one line. Ordinary powers do not. The change-of-basis coefficients are old combinatorial numbers.

\begin{definition}
For integers $n,k\ge0$, the \emph{Stirling number of the second kind} $\stirlingii nk$ is the number of partitions of an
$n$-element set into $k$ non-empty blocks. The \emph{(unsigned) Stirling number of the first kind} $\stirlingi nk$ is the
number of permutations of an $n$-element set with exactly $k$ cycles. By convention $\stirlingii00=\stirlingi00=1$, and
$\stirlingii n0=\stirlingi n0=0$ for $n\ge1$.
\end{definition}

Count them once. $\stirlingii42=7$: four partitions of type $1+3$, namely $\{a\}\{bcd\}$ and its three siblings, and three of type $2+2$, namely $\{ab\}\{cd\}$, $\{ac\}\{bd\}$, $\{ad\}\{bc\}$. And $\stirlingi42=11$: eight permutations of cycle type $3+1$, and three of type $2+2$. Volume~4, Chapter~2, found the expected number of cycles of a random permutation, $\frac1{n!}\sum_kk\stirlingi nk=H_n$. Order in the count, chance in the average.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:stirlingrec}
For $n\ge0$ and $k\ge1$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\stirlingii{n+1}k=k\stirlingii nk+\stirlingii n{k-1},\qq\stirlingi{n+1}k=n\stirlingi nk+\stirlingi n{k-1}.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Look at the element $n+1$. In a partition of $\{1,\dots,n+1\}$ into $k$ blocks, either $\{n+1\}$ is a block, and the
rest is a partition of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into $k-1$ blocks, or $n+1$ was added to one of the $k$ blocks of a partition
of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into $k$ blocks. In a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n+1\}$ with $k$ cycles, either $n+1$ is a fixed
point, and the rest is a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $k-1$ cycles, or deleting $n+1$ from its cycle (sending its
predecessor to its successor) gives a permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $k$ cycles; conversely $n+1$ can be inserted
after any of the $n$ elements, in $n$ ways.
\end{proof}

Write $x^{\overline n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)$ for the \emph{rising powers}, with $x^{\overline0}=1$. Note
$x^{\underline n}=(-1)^n(-x)^{\overline n}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:stirling}
For every $n\ge0$, as polynomials in $x$,
\[
\begin{gathered}x^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\stirlingii nk x^{\underline k},\qq x^{\overline n}=\sum_{k=0}^n\stirlingi nk x^k,\\ x^{\underline n}=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\stirlingi nk x^k.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First identity. Both sides are polynomials, so it is enough to check it at the infinitely many integers $x=m\ge1$
(two polynomials that agree at infinitely many points are equal, Volume~1, Chapter~6). The left side $m^n$ counts the maps
from $\{1,\dots,n\}$ to $\{1,\dots,m\}$. Sort them by the partition of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ into the non-empty fibres of the
map: for a partition into $k$ blocks there are $m^{\underline k}$ maps with exactly these fibres, one for each injective
assignment of distinct values to the blocks. Summing over $k$ gives $\sum_k\stirlingii nk m^{\underline k}$.

The second identity is an induction on $n$. It is true for $n=0$, both sides being $1$. If it holds for $n$, then
\[
\begin{aligned}x^{\overline{n+1}}&=(x+n)x^{\overline n}=\sum_k\stirlingi nk x^{k+1}+n\sum_k\stirlingi nk x^k\eqbr=\sum_k\Bigl(\stirlingi n{k-1}+n\stirlingi nk\Bigr)x^k,\end{aligned}
\]
Proposition~\ref{prop:stirlingrec} turns the coefficient into $\stirlingi{n+1}k$. The third identity is the second with $x$ replaced by $-x$, then multiplied by $(-1)^n$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:falling} now sums any ordinary power. Substitute the first identity:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^p=\sum_{j=0}^p\stirlingii pj\frac{n^{\underline{j+1}}}{j+1}.
\]
For $p=2$ this is $\frac{n^{\underline2}}2+\frac{n^{\underline3}}3=\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}6$, and for $p=3$ it is
$\frac{n^{\underline2}}2+n^{\underline3}+\frac{n^{\underline4}}4=\Bigl(\frac{n(n-1)}2\Bigr)^2$.

\begin{think}
$1+2+\dots+(n-1)=\frac{n(n-1)}2$ and $1^3+2^3+\dots+(n-1)^3=\bigl(\frac{n(n-1)}2\bigr)^2$. Is the sum of the fifth powers
also a polynomial in $\frac{n(n-1)}2$?
\end{think}

\section{Bernoulli numbers and sums of powers}

Stirling numbers give each power sum separately. They do not give one formula that covers every exponent. Johann Faulhaber of Ulm had computed the sums one exponent at a time, through the seventeenth powers, by 1631. Bernoulli found the pattern, in the numbers that now carry his name. Their home is a generating function. Chapter~\ref{ch:generating} builds the language properly. For this section a formal series $\sum_{n\ge0}c_nt^n$ is its list of coefficients, added term by term and multiplied by the Cauchy product
$\bigl(\sum a_nt^n\bigr)\bigl(\sum b_nt^n\bigr)=\sum_n\bigl(\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}\bigr)t^n$, without any question of
convergence. A series with $c_0\ne0$ has an inverse. Write $[n=0]$ for $1$ when $n=0$ and for $0$ otherwise. The equations $\sum_{k=0}^nc_kd_{n-k}=[n=0]$ determine $d_0=\frac1{c_0}$,
then $d_1$, $d_2$, \dots one after the other. We write $e^{at}=\sum_n\frac{a^nt^n}{n!}$; the binomial theorem gives
$e^{at}e^{bt}=e^{(a+b)t}$, since the coefficient of $\frac{t^n}{n!}$ on the left is $\sum_k\binom nka^kb^{n-k}$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{Bernoulli numbers} $B_n$ are defined by the formal identity
\[
\frac t{e^t-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}B_n\frac{t^n}{n!},
\]
where $\frac t{e^t-1}$ is the inverse of the series $\frac{e^t-1}t=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{t^n}{(n+1)!}$, whose constant term is $1$.
The \emph{Bernoulli polynomials} are defined by $\dfrac{te^{xt}}{e^t-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}B_n(x)\dfrac{t^n}{n!}$, that is,
$B_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkB_kx^{n-k}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bernoulli}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $B_0=1$, and for $n\ge1$
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}kB_k=0.
\]
Hence $B_1=-\frac12$, $B_2=\frac16$, $B_4=-\frac1{30}$,
$B_6=\frac1{42}$, $B_8=-\frac1{30}$, $B_{10}=\frac5{66}$, $B_{12}=-\frac{691}{2730}$.
\item $B_n=0$ for every odd $n\ge3$.
\item $B_n(x+1)-B_n(x)=nx^{n-1}$ for $n\ge1$, and $B_n(0)=B_n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) Multiply $\sum_kB_k\frac{t^k}{k!}$ by $e^t-1=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{t^j}{j!}$; the product is $t$. The coefficient of $t^{n+1}$ on the
left is $\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{B_k}{k!\,(n+1-k)!}$, so multiplying by $(n+1)!$ gives $\sum_{k=0}^n\binom{n+1}kB_k=[n=0]$. For $n=0$
this says $B_0=1$; for $n=1$, $1+2B_1=0$; for $n=2$, $1+3B_1+3B_2=0$, so $B_2=\frac16$; and so on.

(b) The odd Bernoulli numbers hide in $f(t)=\frac t{e^t-1}+\frac t2=\sum_{n\ne1}B_n\frac{t^n}{n!}$. This series is even precisely when $f(-t)=f(t)$. Check that identity in the ring of formal series. Multiply by $e^t-1$. The factor is not invertible, but a product that starts with a non-zero term cannot be the zero series. Using $e^te^{-t}=1$,
\[
(e^t-1)f(t)=t+\frac t2(e^t-1)=\frac t2(e^t+1),
\]
and
$(e^t-1)f(-t)=(e^t-1)\bigl(\frac{-t}{e^{-t}-1}-\frac t2\bigr)=te^t-\frac t2(e^t-1)=\frac t2(e^t+1)$, using
$\frac{-t}{e^{-t}-1}=\frac{te^t}{e^t-1}$ (multiply numerator and denominator by $-e^t$). So $(e^t-1)\bigl(f(t)-f(-t)\bigr)=0$.
If $g=f(t)-f(-t)$ were non-zero, with lowest term $g_mt^m$, the product $(e^t-1)g$ would have lowest term $g_mt^{m+1}\ne0$.
So $g=0$, and the odd coefficients of $f$, which are the $B_n$ with odd $n\ge3$, vanish.

(c) Subtract the generating function at $x$ from the one at $x+1$. The denominator cancels:
\[
\frac{te^{xt}(e^t-1)}{e^t-1}=te^{xt}=\sum_{n\ge1}nx^{n-1}\frac{t^n}{n!}.
\]
That is $\Delta B_n(x)=nx^{n-1}$. At $x=0$ the generating function is the one that defines $B_n$, so $B_n(0)=B_n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Faulhaber's formula]\label{thm:faulhaber}
For integers $p\ge0$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^p&=\frac{B_{p+1}(n)-B_{p+1}}{p+1}\eqbr=\frac1{p+1}\sum_{j=0}^p\binom{p+1}jB_jn^{p+1-j}.\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:bernoulli}(c), $F(x)=\frac{B_{p+1}(x)}{p+1}$ satisfies $\Delta F=x^p$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:ftfc}
gives the first form. Expanding $B_{p+1}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{p+1}\binom{p+1}jB_jn^{p+1-j}$ and removing the last term
$j=p+1$, which is $B_{p+1}$, gives the second.
\end{proof}

For $p=1$ this is $\frac12(n^2+2B_1n)=\frac{n(n-1)}2$. For $p=5$, with $B_3=B_5=0$,
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^5=\frac{n^6}6-\frac{n^5}2+\frac{5n^4}{12}-\frac{n^2}{12}.
\]
So
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k<1000}k^5&=\frac{10^{18}}6-\frac{10^{15}}2+\frac{5\cdot10^{12}}{12}-\frac{10^6}{12}\eqbr=166\,167\,083\,333\,250\,000.\end{aligned}
\]
Four terms finish the fifth powers below $1000$. That is the boast, in a smaller case. The question above has a yes. Write $T=\frac{n(n-1)}2$. Then
\[
\frac{n^6}6-\frac{n^5}2+\frac{5n^4}{12}-\frac{n^2}{12}=\frac{n^2\bigl(2n^4-6n^3+5n^2-1\bigr)}{12},
\]
and $(n-1)^2(2n^2-2n-1)=2n^4-6n^3+5n^2-1$, which you can check by expanding the left-hand side. The second factor is $2n(n-1)-1=4T-1$, and $n^2(n-1)^2=4T^2$, so the sum equals $\frac{T^2(4T-1)}{3}$. For $n=4$ one has $T=6$ and $1^5+2^5+3^5=276$, and $\frac{36\cdot 23}{3}=276$.

For $p=10$ the odd Bernoulli numbers from $B_3$ onward vanish, so seven terms survive. The first thousand tenth powers run through $k=1000$. The sum in the theorem stops at $n-1$, so the formula is evaluated at $n=1001$. Those seven terms return
\[
91\,409\,924\,241\,424\,243\,424\,241\,924\,242\,500.
\]

The top of the formula is always $\frac{n^{p+1}}{p+1}-\frac{n^p}2+\frac{p}{12}n^{p-1}-\cdots$. The first term is the integral. The second is the correction from the trapezoid rule. Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} turns that observation into the Euler--Maclaurin formula.

\begin{remark}
The fractions look mild through $B_{10}=\frac5{66}$. Then the numerator $691$ appears in $B_{12}$. Euler's evaluation $\zeta(2k)=\frac{(-1)^{k+1}B_{2k}(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!}$, proved in Chapter~\ref{ch:series}, explains the size. Since $\zeta(2k)\to1$, one has $|B_{2k}|\sim\frac{2(2k)!}{(2\pi)^{2k}}$, which outgrows every exponential. The series $\frac t{e^t-1}$ therefore has radius $2\pi$. Chapter~\ref{ch:complex} will say why: the nearest singularities are the poles $\pm2\pi i$. The denominators are the theorem of von Staudt and Clausen (Exercise~\ref{ex:staudt}). The numerators meet Fermat's last theorem in Kummer's work: $691$ divides the numerator of $B_{12}$, so $691$ is an irregular prime.
\end{remark}

\section{Newton's formula and the method of differences}

Repeat the difference and you get $\Delta^nf$. Write $\Delta=E-1$, with $E$ the shift. The shifts commute, so the binomial theorem is an identity of operators:
\[
\Delta^nf(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^{n-k}\binom nkf(x+k).
\]
You can also prove the expansion by induction on $n$, using Pascal's rule. The operators are not a new algebra. They are a short way to say the same binomial sum.

\begin{theorem}[Newton's interpolation formula]\label{thm:newton}
If $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $d$, then $\Delta^{d+1}f=0$ and, for all $x$,
\[
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^d\Delta^kf(0)\binom xk.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:falling}, $\Delta\binom xk=\binom x{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$ and $\Delta\binom x0=0$. The polynomials
$\binom x0,\dots,\binom xd$ have degrees $0,\dots,d$, so they form a basis of the polynomials of degree at most $d$:
write $f=\sum_{k=0}^dc_k\binom xk$. Then $\Delta^jf=\sum_kc_k\binom x{k-j}$ (terms with $k<j$ vanish), whose value at
$0$ is $c_j$, because $\binom0{k-j}=[k=j]$. Also $\Delta^{d+1}f=0$, since each $\Delta$ lowers the degree.
\end{proof}

Newton's formula rebuilds a polynomial from the values $f(0),\dots,f(d)$, and the only operation in the table is subtraction. The numbers $\Delta^kf(0)$ are the left edge (Figure~\ref{fig:difftable}). Read the same table forwards and the only operation is addition: each new entry is the sum of two neighbours. Charles Babbage's difference engine, designed in the 1820s, was a machine for that addition. It held the differences of a polynomial standing in for a logarithm or a sine, and it added them column by column, so that a printed table would not depend on a human copyist. Figure~\ref{fig:rw01-edge} is the same left edge for the squares.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch01-difftable}
\caption{The difference table of $f(x)=x^3+x+1$. Each entry is the difference of the two above it; the third
differences are constant, equal to $3!=6$. The left edge $1,2,6,6$ gives Newton's formula
$f(x)=1+2\binom x1+6\binom x2+6\binom x3$. The last diagonal is computed from the previous one by additions alone,

\end{figure}

\label{fig:rw01-edge}
\end{figure}

Apply the formula to $f(x)=x^n$ and the Stirling numbers come out explicitly. Compare
$x^n=\sum_kk!\stirlingii nk\binom xk$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:stirling}) with Theorem~\ref{thm:newton}, by uniqueness of the
coefficients in a basis,
\[
\stirlingii nk=\frac1{k!}\Delta^k\bigl[x^n\bigr]_{x=0}=\frac1{k!}\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^{k-j}\binom kjj^n.
\]
The same count is inclusion--exclusion. $k!\stirlingii nk$ is the number of surjections from an $n$-set onto a $k$-set (Exercise~\ref{ex:surjections}).

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A sum is a difference of two values of an antidifference (Theorem~\ref{thm:ftfc}). Falling powers are the functions that difference cleanly (Proposition~\ref{prop:falling}). Summation by parts handles a product. Stirling numbers change basis (Theorem~\ref{thm:stirling}). Bernoulli numbers put every $\sum k^p$ into one line (Theorem~\ref{thm:faulhaber}). Newton's formula rebuilds a polynomial from its difference table.

One tool was used and not yet studied. The generating function defined the Bernoulli numbers, and it showed that the odd ones past $B_1$ vanish, with almost no arithmetic. A sequence was packed into a series, and the series was the easier object. The next chapter asks what else a sequence will say once it is allowed to be a function.

(a) Show that the number of surjections from an $n$-set onto a $k$-set is $k!\stirlingii nk$, and, by inclusion--exclusion,
that it is $\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^{k-j}\binom kjj^n$.
(b) Deduce that $\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^{n-j}\binom njj^n=n!$ and that $\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\binom njj^m=0$ for $0\le m<n$. Prove the

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:engine}
A polynomial of degree $3$ takes the values $1,3,11,31,69$ at $x=0,1,2,3,4$. Find it from the difference table, then

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:powersmodp}
Let $p$ be a prime and $m\ge1$. Show that
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}k^m\equiv\begin{cases}-1\pmod p&\text{if }(p-1)\mid m,\\0\pmod p&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:staudt}
\emph{The theorem of von Staudt and Clausen (1840).} Let $m\ge2$ be even. This exercise proves that
\[
B_m+\sum_{p\text{ prime},\ (p-1)\mid m}\frac1p\ \in\ \Z.
\]
For example $B_2+\frac12+\frac13=1$ and $B_{12}+\frac12+\frac13+\frac15+\frac17+\frac1{13}=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $B_m=\sum_{k=0}^m\frac{(-1)^kk!}{k+1}\stirlingii mk$ for $m\ge1$. (The chapter gave two formulas for
$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j^m$, one with Stirling numbers and one with Bernoulli numbers. Compare the coefficients of $n$.)
\item Show that if $k+1$ is composite and $k+1\ne4$, then $\frac{k!}{k+1}$ is an integer.
\item Show that $\stirlingii m3$ is even for even $m\ge2$, so the term $k=3$ is an integer.
\item For $k+1=p$ prime, show that $(p-1)!\stirlingii m{p-1}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j^m\pmod p$, and conclude with
Exercise~\ref{ex:powersmodp}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:rw01a}
Compute $\Delta\bigl(x^{\underline 3}\bigr)$ from the definition, and hence $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k(k-1)(k-2)$. Check the case $n=4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw01b}
Write $x^3$ as a sum of falling powers, using $\stirlingii 31=1$, $\stirlingii 32=3$, $\stirlingii 33=1$. Deduce $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^3=\bigl(\frac{n(n-1)}2\bigr)^2$. Check $n=4$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw01c}
From Proposition~\ref{prop:bernoulli}(a) with $n=4$, and the values $B_0=1$, $B_1=-\frac12$, $B_2=\frac16$, $B_3=0$, compute $B_4$. Then use Theorem~\ref{thm:faulhaber} to find $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^4$, and check that the sum through $k=2$ is $17$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:rw01d}
Let $c\ne1$. Find a closed form for $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k\,c^k$ by summation by parts, and check it for $c=3$, $n=3$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:k22k}
\idea One power of $k$ is removed at each summation by parts, and $2^k$ survives every time, since it is its own
difference. Two summations by parts bring $k^2$ down to a constant.
\writeup Let $S=\sum_{k=0}^nk^22^k=\sum_0^{n+1}u\,\Delta v$ with $u(k)=k^2$ and $v(k)=2^k$. Then $\Delta u=2k+1$ and
$Ev(k)=2^{k+1}$, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:byparts}
\[
\begin{aligned}S&=(n+1)^22^{n+1}-\sum_{k=0}^n(2k+1)2^{k+1}\\&=(n+1)^22^{n+1}-4\sum_{k=0}^nk2^k-2\sum_{k=0}^n2^k.\end{aligned}
\]
By Example~\ref{ex:k2k} with $n+1$ in place of $n$, $\sum_{k=0}^nk2^k=(n-1)2^{n+1}+2$, and $\sum_{k=0}^n2^k=2^{n+1}-1$.
Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}S&=2^{n+1}\bigl((n+1)^2-4(n-1)-2\bigr)-8+2\eqbr=(n^2-2n+3)2^{n+1}-6.\end{aligned}
\]
Check: $n=2$ gives $0+2+16=18=3\cdot8-6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:triple}
\idea The product of three consecutive integers in the denominator is a negative falling power, whose antidifference is
the next one up.
\writeup $\frac1{k(k+1)(k+2)}=(k-1)^{\underline{-3}}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:falling}, $\Delta x^{\underline{-2}}=-2x^{\underline{-3}}$, so
$x^{\underline{-3}}=\Delta\bigl(-\frac12x^{\underline{-2}}\bigr)$ and
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=1}^n(k-1)^{\underline{-3}}&=-\frac12\Bigl(n^{\underline{-2}}-0^{\underline{-2}}\Bigr)\eqbr=\frac14-\frac1{2(n+1)(n+2)}.\end{aligned}
\]
As $n\to\infty$ the sum tends to $\frac14$. Check: $n=1$ gives $\frac16=\frac14-\frac1{12}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:kHk}
\idea $H_k$ is the discrete logarithm and $k$ a power, so this is $\int x\ln x\dd x$ in disguise, done by parts with the
power integrated.
\writeup Take $u(k)=H_k$ and $v(k)=\frac{k^{\underline2}}2$, so $\Delta v=k$, $\Delta u=\frac1{k+1}$ and $Ev(k)=\frac{(k+1)k}2$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}kH_k&=\frac{n(n-1)}2H_n-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac k2\eqbr=\frac{n(n-1)}2H_n-\frac{n(n-1)}4,\end{aligned}
\]
which is the formula. Check: for $n=3$, $0+1\cdot1+2\cdot\frac32=4$ and $3\bigl(\frac{11}6-\frac12\bigr)=4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:surjections}
\idea Count the same set twice; for (b), a polynomial identity checked by counting needs the counting to be empty.
\writeup (a) A surjection $f:N\to K$ with $|N|=n$, $|K|=k$ determines the partition of $N$ into its $k$ non-empty fibres,
and a bijection between the set of fibres and $K$; conversely each such pair gives one surjection. So there are
$k!\stirlingii nk$. For the second count, for $A\subseteq K$ let $M_A$ be the set of maps $N\to K\setminus A$, of size
$(k-|A|)^n$. By inclusion--exclusion (Volume~1, Chapter~2) the maps missing no point number
$\sum_{A}(-1)^{|A|}(k-|A|)^n=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i\binom ki(k-i)^n$; put $j=k-i$.
(b) With $k=n$, the surjections from an $n$-set onto an $n$-set are the $n!$ bijections. With $n$ in place of $k$ and $m$
in place of $n$, the number of surjections from an $m$-set onto an $n$-set is $0$ when $m<n$, so
$\sum_j(-1)^{n-j}\binom njj^m=0$, and multiplying by $(-1)^n$ gives the claim. With Theorem~\ref{thm:newton}: the sum
is $(-1)^n\Delta^n[x^m]_{x=0}$, and $\Delta^n$ kills polynomials of degree $m<n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:engine}
\idea Differences of a cubic reach a constant at the third step; the left edge of the table is the list of Newton
coefficients.
\writeup The table is $1,3,11,31,69$; then $2,8,20,38$; then $6,12,18$; then $6,6$. The left edge $1,2,6,6$ gives
$f(x)=1+2x+6\binom x2+6\binom x3=1+2x+3x(x-1)+x(x-1)(x-2)=x^3+x+1$. Four values determine a polynomial of degree at most
$3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:newton} uses only $f(0),\dots,f(3)$), and the fifth checks that the degree is indeed at most~$3$:
it is what makes the two third differences equal.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:powersmodp}
\idea Modulo $p$, the non-zero residues are the powers of a primitive root, so the sum is geometric.
\writeup Let $g$ be a primitive root modulo $p$, so that $k=g^j$ for $j=0,\dots,p-2$ runs over the non-zero residues.
Then $\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}k^m\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{p-2}(g^m)^j$. If $(p-1)\mid m$ then $g^m\equiv1$ and the sum is $p-1\equiv-1$.
Otherwise $g^m\not\equiv1$, and $(g^m-1)\sum_{j=0}^{p-2}g^{mj}=g^{m(p-1)}-1\equiv0$ by Fermat's little theorem; since
$g^m-1$ is invertible modulo $p$, the sum is $\equiv0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:staudt}
\idea Express $B_m$ as a sum of terms $\frac{k!}{k+1}\times$ integer: such a term is an integer unless $k+1$ is prime (or
$4$), and in the prime case its fractional part is decided by sums of powers modulo $p$, which the previous exercise
knows.
\writeup (a) After Theorem~\ref{thm:stirling} we found $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j^m=\sum_{k=0}^m\stirlingii mk\frac{n^{\underline{k+1}}}{k+1}$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:faulhaber} with $p=m$ says the same sum is $\frac1{m+1}\sum_{j=0}^m\binom{m+1}jB_jn^{m+1-j}$. Both are
polynomials in $n$, and they agree at every integer $n\ge1$, so they are the same polynomial. Compare the coefficients of $n$.
In Faulhaber's formula the term in $n$ is the one with $j=m$, and it is $\frac1{m+1}\binom{m+1}mB_m\,n=B_m\,n$. In the other
formula, $n^{\underline{k+1}}=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-k)$, and its coefficient of $n$ is $(-1)(-2)\cdots(-k)=(-1)^kk!$. So
$B_m=\sum_{k=0}^m\frac{(-1)^kk!}{k+1}\stirlingii mk$. Check with $m=2$: $-\frac12\cdot1+\frac23\cdot1=\frac16$.

(b) Suppose $k+1=ab$ with $2\le a\le b\le k$, and $k+1\ne4$. If $a<b$, then $a$ and $b$ are different factors of $k!$, so $ab$ divides $k!$. If $a=b$, then $a\ge3$, and both $a$ and $2a$ sit strictly below $a^2=k+1$, so they are different factors of $k!$ and $a^2$ divides $k!$. In every composite case except $4$, the fraction $k!/(k+1)$ is an integer.

(c) The explicit formula at the end of the chapter gives $6\stirlingii m3=3^m-3\cdot2^m+3$. For even $m\ge2$, $3^m=9^{m/2}\equiv1\pmod 4$ and
$3\cdot2^m\equiv0\pmod 4$, so $6\stirlingii m3\equiv4\equiv0\pmod 4$, hence $\stirlingii m3$ is even. The term $k=3$ is
$-\frac64\stirlingii m3=-3\cdot\frac{\stirlingii m3}2\in\Z$.

(d) For $k+1=p$ prime,
\[
(p-1)!\stirlingii m{p-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^{p-1-j}\binom{p-1}jj^m.
\]
In $\binom{p-1}j=\prod_{i=1}^j\frac{p-i}i$
each factor is $\equiv-1$ modulo $p$, so $\binom{p-1}j\equiv(-1)^j$, and $(-1)^{p-1-j}(-1)^j=(-1)^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$
(for $p=2$, $-1\equiv1$). The term $j=0$ vanishes since $m\ge1$. So $(p-1)!\stirlingii m{p-1}\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j^m$,
which by Exercise~\ref{ex:powersmodp} is $\equiv-1$ if $(p-1)\mid m$ and $\equiv0$ otherwise. Hence the term of (a)
with $k=p-1$, namely $\frac{(-1)^{p-1}(p-1)!\stirlingii m{p-1}}p$, is an integer plus $-\frac1p$ when $(p-1)\mid m$
(for odd $p$ the sign $(-1)^{p-1}$ is $1$; for $p=2$ it does not matter, since $\frac12\equiv-\frac12$ modulo $\Z$), and an
integer otherwise.

The pieces now fit. Every term in (a) with $k+1$ composite, including the case $k+1=4$, is an integer, and the term $k=0$ is $0$ for $m\ge1$. Each prime $p$ with $(p-1)\mid m$ contributes an integer minus $1/p$. A prime with $p-1>m$ does not appear: the sum in (a) stops at $k=m$, and $(p-1)$ cannot divide $m$. Therefore
\[
B_m+\sum_{(p-1)\mid m}\frac1p\in\Z.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw01a}
\idea A falling cube differences to three times a falling square. The fundamental theorem does the sum.
\writeup $\Delta x^{\underline 3}=3x^{\underline 2}$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:falling}. So
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^{\underline 3}=\frac{n^{\underline 4}}{4}.
\]
For $n=4$ the only non-zero term is $3\cdot2\cdot1=6$, and $4^{\underline 4}/4=24/4=6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw01b}
\idea Change to the falling basis, then sum. The coefficient of $k^{\underline 2}$ is $3$, and $\sum k^{\underline 2}=n^{\underline 3}/3$, so that term contributes $n^{\underline 3}$, not a half.
\writeup Theorem~\ref{thm:stirling} gives $x^3=x^{\underline 3}+3x^{\underline 2}+x$. Proposition~\ref{prop:falling} sums the three pieces separately:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^3=\frac{n^{\underline 4}}4+n^{\underline 3}+\frac{n^{\underline 2}}2=\frac{n(n-1)}4\Bigl((n-2)(n-3)+4(n-2)+2\Bigr).
\]
The bracket is $(n-2)(n+1)+2=n(n-1)$. Multiply by the front factor: $\frac{n(n-1)}{4}\cdot n(n-1)=\bigl(\frac{n(n-1)}2\bigr)^2$. For $n=4$ both sides equal $1^3+2^3+3^3=36$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw01c}
\idea One linear equation from the recurrence, then four terms of Faulhaber's formula.
\writeup Proposition~\ref{prop:bernoulli}(a) at $n=4$ reads $\sum_{k=0}^4\binom5k B_k=0$. With $B_3=0$ this is
\[
1+5\Bigl(-\frac12\Bigr)+10\cdot\frac16+5B_4=0,
\]
so $1-\frac52+\frac53+5B_4=0$. The rational part is $\frac16$, hence $B_4=-\frac1{30}$. Theorem~\ref{thm:faulhaber} with $p=4$ gives
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k^4=\frac1{5}\Bigl(n^5-\frac52 n^4+\frac53 n^3-\frac16 n\Bigr).
\]
Through $k=2$, so at $n=3$, the right-hand side is $\frac15\bigl(243-\frac{405}2+45-\frac12\bigr)=\frac15\cdot85=17$, and $0+1+16=17$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw01d}
\idea $c^k$ is a constant times its own difference. Sum by parts and sum the geometric series that remains.
\writeup Since $\Delta c^k=(c-1)c^k$, the function $v(k)=\frac{c^k}{c-1}$ has $\Delta v=c^k$. Take $u(k)=k$, so $\Delta u=1$ and $Ev(k)=\frac{c^{k+1}}{c-1}$. Proposition~\ref{prop:byparts} gives
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}kc^k=\frac{nc^n}{c-1}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{c^{k+1}}{c-1}=\frac{nc^n}{c-1}-\frac{c(c^n-1)}{(c-1)^2}=\frac{(n-1)c^{n+1}-nc^n+c}{(c-1)^2}.
\]
For $c=3$ and $n=3$ the closed form is $\frac{2\cdot 81-3\cdot 27+3}{4}=21$, and $0+1\cdot3+2\cdot9=21$.
\end{solution}
