\chapter{The Discrete Fourier Transform and the FFT}\label{ch:fft}

\begin{center}\itshape
For $n=2^{20}$ samples, the plain rule uses about $10^{12}$ multiplications.\\
The fast transform uses about $10^{7}$.
\end{center}

A million is $2^{20}=1\,048\,576$. The square of that number is about $1.10\times10^{12}$. Half of $n$ times $\log_2 n$ is $10\,485\,760$, about $10^{7}$. That gap is the subject of this chapter.

In 1805 Gauss, interpolating the orbit of the asteroid Pallas, found a fast way to compute these sums and left it unpublished. An eccentricity of Juno is one of the clues that date the manuscript. In 1963, at a meeting of President Kennedy's Science Advisory Committee on detecting nuclear tests from seismometer readings, John Tukey sketched the same idea. Richard Garwin saw what it meant, and found James Cooley at IBM to make it run. Their paper appeared in 1965. In 1994 Gilbert Strang called the fast Fourier transform the most important numerical algorithm of our lifetime.

Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier} decomposed a function on the circle into infinitely many frequencies. A computer only ever sees finitely many samples. On $n$ samples there are exactly $n$ frequencies, the characters of the cyclic group $\Z/n\Z$, which Volume~3, Chapter~11, already studied. The transform is a matrix. Computing it by the definition costs $n^2$ multiplications. For a million samples, that is the difference between about $10^{12}$ multiplications and about $10^{7}$. The fast transform made digital audio, medical imaging, radar, and modern cryptography practical. Volume~1, Chapter~5, said that this volume turns the roots-of-unity filter into a fast way to multiply polynomials.

\begin{think}
To multiply two polynomials of degree $n-1$ the schoolbook method computes $n^2$ products of coefficients. A polynomial of degree $2n-2$ is determined by its values at $2n-1$ points, and values multiply pointwise. What is the catch, and which points would you choose?
\end{think}

The catch is the cost of evaluating and of interpolating. The right points are the $n$th roots of unity, because the fast transform evaluates a polynomial at all of them at once.

\section{The discrete Fourier transform}

Fix $n\ge1$. Set $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$, and index vectors of $\C^n$ by $\Z/n\Z$, so that $a_{k+n}=a_k$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{discrete Fourier transform} (DFT) of $a=(a_0,\dots,a_{n-1})\in\C^n$ is $\hat a\in\C^n$ with
\[
\hat a_j=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_k\omega^{-jk}\qquad(j\in\Z/n\Z).
\]
In matrix form $\hat a=F_na$, where $F_n=(\omega^{-jk})_{0\le j,k<n}$.
\end{definition}

This is the convention of most software. Volume~3, Chapter~11, divided by $n$. The DFT evaluates the polynomial $A(X)=\sum_ka_kX^k$ at the $n$th roots of unity: $\hat a_j=A(\omega^{-j})$.

Try $n=4$, so $\omega=i$, and $a=(1,2,3,4)$. Then $\hat a_0=1+2+3+4=10$, the sum of the entries. Next
\[
\hat a_1=1+2(-i)+3(-1)+4i=-2+2i.
\]
Then $\hat a_2=1-2+3-4=-2$, the alternating sum, and $\hat a_3=-2-2i$. The last value is the conjugate of $\hat a_1$, because $a$ is real. Keep these four numbers. You have $1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2=30$, and $\frac14\bigl(10^2+|-2+2i|^2+|-2|^2+|-2-2i|^2\bigr)=\frac14(100+8+4+8)=30$ as well. Figure~\ref{fig:rw07-dft} draws the four bins. The next theorem says the match of the two sums of squares is the general fact.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rootsum}
For every integer $m$, $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\omega^{mk}$ is $n$ if $n$ divides $m$, and $0$ otherwise.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $n$ divides $m$, every term is $1$, and the sum is $n$. Otherwise $q=\omega^m\ne1$, and the geometric sum is $\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}=0$, because $q^n=(\omega^n)^m=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[inversion and Parseval]\label{thm:dftinversion}
For $a\in\C^n$,
\[
\begin{gathered}a_k=\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat a_j\omega^{jk},\qq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|a_k|^2=\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|\hat a_j|^2.\end{gathered}
\]
Equivalently, $\frac1{\sqrt n}F_n$ is a unitary matrix, and $F_n^{-1}=\frac1n\overline{F_n}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The product of matrices has entries
\[
(\overline{F_n}F_n)_{lk}=\sum_j\omega^{jl}\omega^{-jk}=\sum_j\omega^{j(l-k)}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum} says this sum is $n$ if $l=k$ and $0$ otherwise. So $\overline{F_n}F_n=nI$, which is the inversion formula: $a=\frac1n\overline{F_n}\hat a$. The matrix $F_n$ is symmetric, so $\overline{F_n}=F_n^*$, the conjugate transpose. Hence $\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt n}F_n\bigr)^*\frac1{\sqrt n}F_n=I$. A unitary matrix preserves the Hermitian norm, and that is Parseval's identity for the DFT.
\end{proof}

Check inversion on $a_0$ for $(1,2,3,4)$. Since $\omega^{j\cdot0}=1$,
\[
a_0=\frac14\bigl(10+(-2+2i)+(-2)+(-2-2i)\bigr)=\frac14\cdot4=1,
\]
which is the entry we started from.

The DFT is the Fourier series of Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier} read through samples. Sampling a function at $n$ equally spaced points cannot tell the frequency $j$ from the frequency $j+n$, because $e^{i(j+n)t}=e^{ijt}$ at $t=\frac{2\pi k}n$. The samples add those frequencies together.

\begin{proposition}[aliasing]\label{prop:aliasing}
Let $f$ be continuous and $2\pi$-periodic with $\sum_m|c_m(f)|<\infty$, so that $f=\sum_mc_m(f)e_m$ everywhere (the series converges uniformly to a continuous function with the same coefficients, which is $f$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer}), and let $a_k=f\bigl(\frac{2\pi k}n\bigr)$. Then
\[
\frac1n\hat a_j=\sum_{l\in\Z}c_{j+ln}(f).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand $f$ and the transform:
\[
\hat a_j=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m\in\Z}c_m(f)\,\omega^{mk}\omega^{-jk}.
\]
The double sum converges absolutely, because $\sum_m|c_m(f)|<\infty$ and there are $n$ values of $k$. Sum in $k$ first. Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum} keeps the frequencies $m\equiv j\pmod n$, each with weight $n$. Divide by $n$.
\end{proof}

So $\frac1n\hat a_j$ approximates $c_j(f)$ for $|j|<\frac n2$. The error is the tail of the coefficients at the frequencies $j\pm n$, $j\pm2n$, and so on. The tail is small when $f$ is smooth. Suppose $|c_m(f)|\le\frac C{m^2}$ for $m\ne0$, as for the periodic function equal to $|x|$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$. That function is continuous, with a kink, and $c_m=-\frac2{\pi m^2}$ for $m$ odd, while $c_m=0$ for $m\ne0$ even. For $|j|\le\frac n2$ and $l\ne0$ one has $|j+ln|\ge\bigl(|l|-\frac12\bigr)n$, so the error is at most
\[
\frac{2C}{n^2}\sum_{l\ge1}\frac1{\bigl(l-\frac12\bigr)^2}=\frac{\pi^2 C}{n^2}.
\]
Doubling the number of samples divides the error by four. The sum in the constant is $\sum_{l\ge1}\bigl(l-\frac12\bigr)^{-2}=\frac{\pi^2}2$: one has $\bigl(l-\frac12\bigr)^{-2}=4(2l-1)^{-2}$, and the sum of the reciprocals of the odd squares is $\frac{\pi^2}8$.

\section{Convolution}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{cyclic convolution} of $a,b\in\C^n$ is $(a*b)_k=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}a_lb_{k-l}$, indices modulo $n$.
\end{definition}

Multiplying the transforms multiplies the sequences, in the cyclic sense. That is the whole point of evaluating at roots of unity.

\begin{theorem}[convolution theorem]\label{thm:convolution}
$\widehat{a*b}_j=\hat a_j\hat b_j$ for every $j$. Hence $a*b=F_n^{-1}\bigl(F_na\cdot F_nb\bigr)$, with $\cdot$ the entrywise product.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Expand the definition:
\[
\widehat{a*b}_j=\sum_k\sum_l a_l b_{k-l}\omega^{-jk}.
\]
Write the power as $\omega^{-jl}\omega^{-j(k-l)}$. Set $m=k-l$. As $k$ runs through $\Z/n\Z$, so does $m$. The sum factors into $\bigl(\sum_l a_l\omega^{-jl}\bigr)\bigl(\sum_m b_m\omega^{-jm}\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

In polynomial language, $\hat a_j\hat b_j$ is the value of the product $AB$ at $\omega^{-j}$. The vector $a*b$ is the coefficient vector of $AB$ reduced modulo $X^n-1$, because $X^n=1$ at every $n$th root of unity. If $\deg A+\deg B<n$, nothing wraps around, and the convolution is the product.

\begin{corollary}[multiplying polynomials]\label{cor:polymul}
Let $A$, $B$ have degrees $d$ and $e$, and $n>d+e$. Pad their coefficient vectors with zeros to length $n$. Then the coefficients of $AB$
are $F_n^{-1}(F_na\cdot F_nb)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The entry $(a*b)_k=\sum_l a_l b_{k-l}$. With the zero padding, every index that appears stays in $[0,n[$ without wrapping: if $l\le d$ and $k-l\le e$, then $k\le d+e<n$. So $(a*b)_k$ is the coefficient of $X^k$ in $AB$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:convolution}.
\end{proof}

This is the strategy of the think box. Evaluate at the $n$th roots of unity, multiply the values, and interpolate back. The catch was the cost of the two transforms, $n^2$ each by the definition. The FFT removes that cost.

\section{The fast Fourier transform}

Let $n=2m$ be even. Then $\omega^2=e^{2\pi i/m}$ is a root of unity of order $m$. Split $a$ into the even entries and the odd entries, $a^{\mathrm{even}}=(a_0,a_2,\dots,a_{n-2})$ and $a^{\mathrm{odd}}=(a_1,a_3,\dots,a_{n-1})$. These are vectors of length $m$, with transforms $E$ and $O$.

\begin{lemma}[butterfly]\label{lem:butterfly}
For $0\le j<m$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\hat a_j=E_j+\omega^{-j}O_j,\qq\hat a_{j+m}=E_j-\omega^{-j}O_j.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In polynomial language, $A(X)=A^{\mathrm{even}}(X^2)+XA^{\mathrm{odd}}(X^2)$. At $X=\omega^{-j}$, the square $X^2=(\omega^2)^{-j}$ is an $m$th root of unity, so $A^{\mathrm{even}}(X^2)=E_j$ and $A^{\mathrm{odd}}(X^2)=O_j$. The transforms $E$ and $O$ have period $m$. Replacing $j$ by $j+m$ sends $X$ to $-X$, because $\omega^{-m}=-1$, and it leaves $X^2$ unchanged. The odd half therefore changes sign.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{fig:rw07-butterfly} is one of these steps, for $n=8$ and $j=1$. The twiddle is $w=\omega^{-1}=e^{-2\pi i/8}$. The upper output is $E_1+wO_1$ and the lower output is $E_1-wO_1$. The halving works because squaring maps the $n$th roots of unity two-to-one onto the $m$th roots. The $n$ evaluations of $A$ need only $m$ evaluations of each half.

The recursive algorithm is three lines.

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.9\linewidth}
\textbf{FFT}$(a)$, for $a$ of length $n=2^r$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if $n=1$, return $a$;
\item $E\gets\mathrm{FFT}(a_0,a_2,\dots)$, $O\gets\mathrm{FFT}(a_1,a_3,\dots)$;
\item for $0\le j<\frac n2$: $t\gets\omega^{-j}O_j$, $\hat a_j\gets E_j+t$, $\hat a_{j+n/2}\gets E_j-t$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\begin{theorem}[Cooley--Tukey]\label{thm:fft}
For $n=2^r$, the FFT computes the DFT with at most $\frac n2\log_2n$ complex multiplications and $n\log_2n$ complex additions,
the powers $\omega^{-j}$ being precomputed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Correctness is Lemma~\ref{lem:butterfly}, by induction on $r$. Let $M(n)$ and $S(n)$ count the multiplications and the additions. Step 3 uses $\frac n2$ multiplications and $n$ additions, so
\[
M(n)=2M\Bigl(\frac n2\Bigr)+\frac n2,\qquad S(n)=2S\Bigl(\frac n2\Bigr)+n,
\]
with $M(1)=S(1)=0$. The induction step is
\[
M(2^r)=2\cdot\frac{2^{r-1}}2(r-1)+2^{r-1}=\frac{2^r}2\,r
\]
and $S(2^r)=2\cdot2^{r-1}(r-1)+2^r=2^r r$. For $n=16=2^4$ this says $32$ multiplications and $64$ additions. You can check the recurrence by hand: $M(2)=1$, $M(4)=4$, $M(8)=12$, $M(16)=32$.
\end{proof}

Unrolling the recursion gives an iterative algorithm that works in place (Figure~\ref{fig:butterfly}). The leaves of the recursion are the inputs in bit-reversed order, and $\log_2 n$ passes of butterflies produce the transform. The inverse transform is the same algorithm with $\omega$ replaced by $\overline\omega$, followed by division by $n$. For $n$ not a power of $2$, one can split along any factorisation $n=n_1n_2$, or use Bluestein's trick (Exercise~\ref{ex:bluestein}).

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:fastmul}
Two polynomials of degree less than $N$ can be multiplied with $O(N\log N)$ arithmetic operations on complex numbers.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Take $n$ the least power of $2$ with $n\ge2N$, so $n<4N$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:polymul} the product costs three FFTs of length $n$ and $n$ multiplications, that is $O(n\log n)=O(N\log N)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Exact transforms modulo a prime}

A floating-point FFT makes rounding errors. Rounding is tolerable in a signal. The digits of an integer need an exact answer. The argument uses an element $w$ of order exactly $n$, and the invertibility of $n$ and of $w^m-1$ whenever $n$ does not divide $m$. A finite field provides both.

\begin{theorem}[number-theoretic transform]\label{thm:ntt}
Let $p$ be a prime with $p\equiv1\pmod n$. Then there is $w\in\Z/p\Z$ of order exactly $n$, and with $\hat a_j=\sum_ka_kw^{-jk}$ computed in $\Z/p\Z$,
the inversion formula $a_k=n^{-1}\sum_j\hat a_jw^{jk}$, the convolution theorem and the FFT of Theorem~\ref{thm:fft} (for $n=2^r$) hold in $\Z/p\Z$.
Consequently, if $a,b$ are vectors of $N$ integers in $[0,B[$, padded with zeros to length $n\ge2N-1$, and $p>N(B-1)^2$, the product of the
corresponding polynomials is computed exactly by transforms modulo $p$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The group $(\Z/p\Z)^\times$ is cyclic of order $p-1$ (Volume~2, Chapter~5). Since $n$ divides $p-1$, if $g$ is a generator then $w=g^{(p-1)/n}$ has order $n$. Also $n<p$, so $n$ is invertible modulo $p$. Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum} holds in $\Z/p\Z$. If $n$ does not divide $m$, then $w^m\ne1$, so $w^m-1$ is invertible, and the geometric sum vanishes. The proofs of inversion, of the convolution theorem, and of the butterfly used nothing else. In particular $w^{n/2}=-1$, because it is a square root of $1$ different from $1$, and in a field the only square roots of $1$ are $\pm1$. Finally, each coefficient of the product is a sum of at most $N$ products of integers in $[0,B-1]$, so it lies in $[0,N(B-1)^2]$. Knowing it modulo a prime $p>N(B-1)^2$ determines it.
\end{proof}

To multiply two $1000$-digit numbers in base $10$, take $N=1000$ and $B=10$. The coefficients of the product are at most $81\,000$, and $n=2048$. The smallest prime $p>81\,000$ with $p\equiv1\pmod{2048}$ is $83\,969=41\cdot2^{11}+1$. Practical libraries use primes such as $998\,244\,353=119\cdot2^{23}+1$, which allows $n$ up to $2^{23}$ and has primitive root $3$, together with larger digits $B$ and several primes combined by the Chinese remainder theorem. After the transform, the carries are propagated in linear time.

\begin{remark}
Iterating this idea, Sch\"onhage and Strassen (1971) multiplied $N$-bit integers in $O(N\log N\log\log N)$ bit operations, and Harvey and van der Hoeven (2019) reached $O(N\log N)$, which is believed to be optimal. Every computation of $\pi$ to billions of digits, and of the Mersenne primes of Volume~2, Chapter~2, runs on FFT multiplication.
\end{remark}

\section{The discrete cosine transform}

A signal on $\{0,\dots,N-1\}$ that is not periodic acquires a jump when it is wrapped around a circle. By Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}, a jump means slowly decaying coefficients. The cure is to reflect the signal before transforming it, so the seam has no jump.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dct}
Let $x\in\C^N$ and let $y\in\C^{2N}$ be its even extension
\[
y=(x_0,\dots,x_{N-1},x_{N-1},\dots,x_0).
\]
Then, for $0\le j<2N$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\hat y_j=2e^{i\pi j/(2N)}X_j,\qq X_j=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_k\cos\frac{\pi j(2k+1)}{2N}.\end{gathered}
\]
The vector $(X_0,\dots,X_{N-1})$ is the \emph{discrete cosine transform} (DCT-II) of $x$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/(2N)}=e^{i\pi/N}$. The entry $y_{2N-1-k}=x_k$ contributes $x_k\omega^{-j(2N-1-k)}=x_k\omega^{j(k+1)}$, so
\[
\hat y_j=\sum_k x_k\bigl(\omega^{-jk}+\omega^{j(k+1)}\bigr).
\]
Factor out $\omega^{j/2}=e^{i\pi j/(2N)}$ and set $\theta_k=\frac{\pi j(2k+1)}{2N}$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}\omega^{-jk}+\omega^{j(k+1)}&=e^{i\pi j/(2N)}\bigl(e^{-i\theta_k}+e^{i\theta_k}\bigr)\eqbr=2e^{i\pi j/(2N)}\cos\theta_k.\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

The DCT costs one FFT of length $2N$, and inverting the FFT inverts the cosine transform. The even extension $y$ has no jump at the seam, so for samples of a smooth function its coefficients decay much faster than those of $x$ itself. A few of them carry most of the energy. This is why the JPEG image format cuts an image into $8\times8$ blocks, applies a two-dimensional DCT to each block, and keeps the large coefficients.

\section{Gauss sums as Fourier coefficients}

Let $p$ be an odd prime, $\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}$, and $\chi(k)=\bigl(\frac kp\bigr)$ the Legendre symbol, extended by $\chi(0)=0$. This is a function on $\Z/p\Z$. Let
\[
g=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\chi(k)\omega^k
\]
be its Gauss sum. The sum already appeared in the exercises of Volume~2, Chapter~6.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gaussfourier}
The DFT of $\chi$ is $\hat\chi_j=\chi(-j)\,g$ for every $j$. Consequently $|g|^2=p$, and $g=\sum_{a=0}^{p-1}\omega^{a^2}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $j=0$, one has $\hat\chi_0=\sum_k\chi(k)=0$, because there are as many residues as non-residues, and $\chi(0)=0$. For $j\ne0$, substitute $k=-j^{-1}m$. As $m$ runs through $\Z/p\Z$, so does $k$, and $\omega^{-jk}=\omega^m$. Multiplicativity gives $\chi(k)=\chi(-j^{-1})\chi(m)=\chi(-j)\chi(m)$, since $\chi$ takes values $\pm1$ on the non-zero residues. So $\hat\chi_j=\chi(-j)\,g$. Parseval, Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}, now gives
\[
p-1=\sum_k|\chi(k)|^2=\frac1p\sum_j|\hat\chi_j|^2=\frac1p(p-1)|g|^2,
\]
so $|g|^2=p$. Finally, the number of $a$ with $a^2\equiv m$ is $1+\chi(m)$, so $\sum_a\omega^{a^2}=\sum_m(1+\chi(m))\omega^m$. The sum of $\omega^m$ over $m$ is $0$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum}, and the sum against $\chi$ is $g$.
\end{proof}

This recovers an exercise of Volume~1, Chapter~5. The quadratic Gauss sum has modulus $\sqrt p$, because the Legendre symbol, like any character, has a flat spectrum. Its exact value is $\sqrt p$ when $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $i\sqrt p$ when $p\equiv3\pmod4$. That is Gauss's theorem. Chapter~\ref{ch:transform} proves the sign with Poisson summation.

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

On $n$ samples there are $n$ frequencies. The transform that passes from one list to the other is a unitary matrix up to the factor $\sqrt n$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}). It turns convolution into pointwise multiplication (Theorem~\ref{thm:convolution}), and multiplying polynomials is a convolution (Corollary~\ref{cor:polymul}). That answers the think box. Evaluate at the roots of unity, multiply the values, and come back. The catch was the cost of going and of coming back. The butterfly removes it. Squaring folds the $n$th roots of unity onto the $m$th roots, so one transform of length $n$ is two transforms of length $\frac n2$ and $\frac n2$ further multiplications. The total is at most $\frac n2\log_2 n$ multiplications (Theorem~\ref{thm:fft}). The same algebra runs in $\Z/p\Z$, with no rounding (Theorem~\ref{thm:ntt}). That is how very large integers are multiplied.

Two remarks point forward. A sampled function shows its Fourier coefficients folded modulo $n$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:aliasing}). Chapter~\ref{ch:transform} meets the same folding on the line, where it is the Poisson summation formula. The Legendre symbol has a flat spectrum, $|g|=\sqrt p$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussfourier}). The sign of $g$, which cost Gauss four years, falls out of that same formula.

\begin{beauty}
The butterfly is the Chinese remainder theorem for the polynomial $X^n-1$. It factors as $(X^{n/2}-1)(X^{n/2}+1)$, and reducing modulo the two factors is one butterfly. After $\log_2 n$ steps, $X^n-1$ has split into the linear factors $X-\omega^j$.
\end{beauty}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:dftorder}
Prove $F_n^2=nR$, where $R$ is the permutation matrix of $k\mapsto-k$ on $\Z/n\Z$, and deduce $F_n^4=n^2I$. Which numbers can occur as eigenvalues of the unitary matrix $\frac1{\sqrt n}F_n$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:circulantfft}
A \emph{circulant} matrix has entries $C_{kl}=c_{k-l}$ for some $c\in\C^n$. Prove $C=F_n^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\hat c)F_n$. Deduce $\det C=\prod_j\hat c_j$, as in Volume~1, Chapter~5. When $n$ is a power of $2$, explain how to solve $Cx=y$ in $O(n\log n)$ operations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:karatsuba}
(Karatsuba, 1960.) Prove that $(a_0+a_1X)(b_0+b_1X)$ can be computed with three multiplications of coefficients, where the schoolbook count is four. From that identity, build a recursion that multiplies polynomials of degree less than $n=2^r$ with exactly $3^r=n^{\log_23}$ multiplications of coefficients. Compare that count with $3\cdot2^r(r+1)+2^{r+1}$, the number of complex multiplications in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:fastmul}: three FFTs of length $2^{r+1}$, plus the pointwise products.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:bluestein}
(Bluestein's trick.) Let $n\ge1$ be arbitrary and set $\zeta=e^{i\pi/n}$, so $\zeta^2=\omega$. From the identity $2jk=j^2+k^2-(j-k)^2$, write $\hat a_j$ as $\zeta^{-j^2}$ times a convolution of $(a_k\zeta^{-k^2})$ with $(\zeta^{m^2})$. Deduce that a DFT of any length $n$ costs $O(n\log n)$ operations.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:nttsmall}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:midpoint}
Let $n\ge3$, and let $z=(z_0,\dots,z_{n-1})\in\C^n$ be the vertices of a polygon with centroid $0$. Its \emph{midpoint polygon} is $Mz$, with $(Mz)_k=\frac12(z_k+z_{k+1})$ and indices modulo $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that there are complex numbers $a$ and $b$, depending on $z$, such that, with $\omega=e^{2i\pi/n}$,
\[
\frac{(M^tz)_k}{\cos^t(\pi/n)}-\bigl(a\,e^{i\pi t/n}\omega^k+b\,e^{-i\pi t/n}\omega^{-k}\bigr)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0
\quad\text{for each }k.
\]
\item Prove that $k\mapsto a\omega^k+b\omega^{-k}$ is the image of the regular polygon $k\mapsto\omega^k$ under a real-linear map of the plane. The map is invertible when $|a|\ne|b|$. The iterated midpoint polygons, after rescaling, become \emph{affinely regular} (Figure~\ref{fig:midpoint}).
\item For $n=4$, what does one step do?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:napoleon}
\emph{Napoleon's theorem.} Let $ABC$ be a triangle, labelled counterclockwise. On each side, build an equilateral triangle pointing outwards, and let $P$, $Q$, $R$ be the centres of those triangles.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove that $PQR$ is equilateral, and that it has the same centroid as $ABC$.
\item Repeat the construction with the equilateral triangles pointing inwards, and write $P'$, $Q'$, $R'$ for their centres. Prove that $P'Q'R'$ is equilateral, and that the area of $PQR$ minus the area of $P'Q'R'$ equals the area of $ABC$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:pdn}
\emph{The Petr--Douglas--Neumann theorem.} Let $n\ge3$, let $z\in\C^n$ be a polygon, and let $\theta\in\left]0,2\pi\right[$. Write $A_\theta z$ for the polygon whose $k$-th vertex is the point $p$ satisfying $z_k-p=e^{i\theta}(z_{k+1}-p)$. That point is the apex of the isosceles triangle of apex angle $\theta$ built on the side $z_kz_{k+1}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find the eigenvalues of the linear map $A_\theta$ on the Fourier modes $k\mapsto\omega^{jk}$.
\item Apply $A_{2\pi k/n}$ for $k=1,2,\dots,n-2$, in any order. Prove that the result is a regular $n$-gon, possibly reduced to a point, whose centre is the centroid of $z$ (Figure~\ref{fig:pdn}).
\item What do the cases $n=3$ and $n=4$ say?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:cdg}
\emph{A fast random walk modulo $p$ (Chung, Diaconis and Graham, 1987).} Let $p$ be an odd prime. Set $X_0=0$ and $X_{n+1}=2X_n+\varepsilon_n\bmod p$, where the $\varepsilon_n$ are independent and uniform in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Let $d_n$ be the total variation distance between the law of $X_n$ and the uniform law.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove $d_n\ge1-3^n/p$. So at least $\log_3p$ steps are necessary.
\item Prove $\mathbb E\bigl[e^{2i\pi tX_n/p}\bigr]=\prod_{j<n}\frac{1+2\cos(2\pi2^jt/p)}3$.
\item Set $L=\lceil\log_2p\rceil+1$. For $t\not\equiv0$, prove that among any $L$ consecutive indices $j$, at least one satisfies $\cos(2\pi2^jt/p)\le0$.
\item Deduce $d_n\le\frac12\sqrt p\,3^{-\lfloor n/L\rfloor}$. So $O(\log^2p)$ steps suffice.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:newman}
\emph{Newman's phenomenon.} Let $s(n)$ be the sum of the binary digits of $n$, and set $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item For $k\ge1$ and $j\in\{0,1,2\}$, prove that the number of $n<2^k$ with $s(n)\equiv j\pmod3$ is $\frac13\bigl(2^k+2\cos\frac{\pi(k-2j)}3\bigr)$.
\item Prove
\[
\sum_{\substack{0\le n<4^k\\3\mid n}}(-1)^{s(n)}=2\cdot3^{k-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{\substack{0\le n<2\cdot4^k\\3\mid n}}(-1)^{s(n)}=3^k.
\]
Among the multiples of $3$, the ones with an even digit sum are ahead. The margin, for numbers below $N$, has order $N^{\log3/\log4}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:bent}
\emph{Bent functions.} Identify $\{-1,1\}^n$ with $\mathbb F_2^n$, so that the characters are $x\mapsto(-1)^{a\cdot x}$ for $a\in\mathbb F_2^n$. For $f:\mathbb F_2^n\to\{-1,1\}$, write $\hat f(a)=2^{-n}\sum_x f(x)(-1)^{a\cdot x}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Prove $\max_a|\hat f(a)|\ge2^{-n/2}$. Call $f$ \emph{bent} when $|\hat f(a)|=2^{-n/2}$ for every $a$. Prove that a bent function can exist only when $n$ is even.
\item Prove that $f(x,y)=(-1)^{x\cdot y}$ on $\mathbb F_2^m\times\mathbb F_2^m$ is bent.
\item The \emph{nonlinearity} of $f$ is its Hamming distance to the nearest function $\pm(-1)^{a\cdot x}$. Prove that this distance equals $2^{n-1}\bigl(1-\max_a|\hat f(a)|\bigr)$. Deduce that it is at most $2^{n-1}-2^{n/2-1}$, with equality exactly for bent functions.
\item Prove that a bent function takes the value $-1$ on exactly $2^{n-1}\pm2^{n/2-1}$ points.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:dftorder}
\idea Transforming twice sums the characters twice. Orthogonality keeps only the frequency $-j$.
\writeup The entries are $(F_n^2)_{jl}=\sum_k\omega^{-jk}\omega^{-kl}=\sum_k\omega^{-k(j+l)}$, which is $n$ if $l\equiv -j$ and $0$ otherwise, by Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum}. So $F_n^2=nR$. Since $R^2=I$, one has $F_n^4=n^2I$. For $U=\frac1{\sqrt n}F_n$ this says $U^4=I$, so every eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda^4=1$. The possible eigenvalues are $1$, $-1$, $i$, and $-i$. For $n\ge5$ all four occur, with multiplicities close to $\frac n4$. For $n=4$ the eigenvalues are $1$, $1$, $-1$, $-i$. Chapter~\ref{ch:transform} meets the eigenvectors of the continuous transform, the Hermite functions, of which the Gaussian is the first.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:circulantfft}
\idea A circulant matrix is convolution with its first row. Convolution is diagonal in the Fourier basis.
\writeup The product is $(Cx)_k=\sum_l c_{k-l}x_l=(c*x)_k$, so $F_nCx=\hat c\cdot\hat x=\operatorname{diag}(\hat c)F_nx$ for every $x$. That is $C=F_n^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\hat c)F_n$. Taking determinants, $\det C=\prod_j\hat c_j$. And $\hat c_j=P(\omega^{-j})$ with $P(X)=\sum_k c_kX^k$, which is the formula of Volume~1, Chapter~5. To solve $Cx=y$, compute $\hat y$ by an FFT, set $\hat x_j=\frac{\hat y_j}{\hat c_j}$, and invert the FFT. Division is possible precisely when no $\hat c_j$ vanishes, which is when $C$ is invertible. The cost is three FFTs and $n$ divisions. Gaussian elimination costs $O(n^3)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:karatsuba}
\idea Trade one multiplication for a few additions, then repeat the trade at every level of a recursion.
\writeup Compute $a_0b_0$, $a_1b_1$, and $(a_0+a_1)(b_0+b_1)$. The middle coefficient is $(a_0+a_1)(b_0+b_1)-a_0b_0-a_1b_1$. Write $A=A_0+X^{n/2}A_1$ and $B=B_0+X^{n/2}B_1$, with halves of degree less than $\frac n2$. The same identity gives $AB$ from the three products $A_0B_0$, $A_1B_1$, and $(A_0+A_1)(B_0+B_1)$, plus additions. The number of coefficient multiplications satisfies $K(n)=3K(\frac n2)$ and $K(1)=1$, so $K(2^r)=3^r=n^{\log_2 3}\approx n^{1.585}$. Compare $3^r$ with $3\cdot2^r(r+1)+2^{r+1}$, the count in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:fastmul}. For $r=1,\ldots,9$ the Karatsuba counts are $3$, $9$, $27$, $81$, $243$, $729$, $2187$, $6561$, $19683$, and the FFT counts are $16$, $44$, $112$, $272$, $640$, $1472$, $3328$, $7424$, $16384$. Karatsuba uses fewer multiplications up through $n=256$. From $n=512$ on, the FFT is smaller. The ratio then grows like $\frac{n^{0.585}}{3\log_2 n}$. In practice, once additions and memory traffic are counted, libraries switch from the schoolbook method to Karatsuba around a few dozen machine words, and to FFT methods at thousands of words.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:bluestein}
\idea The product $jk$ couples the input index to the output index. Writing it with squares turns the coupling into a function of $j-k$, which is a convolution.
\writeup Since $\zeta^2=\omega$, one has $\omega^{-jk}=\zeta^{-2jk}=\zeta^{-j^2}\zeta^{-k^2}\zeta^{(j-k)^2}$. Therefore
\[
\hat a_j=\zeta^{-j^2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}u_kv_{j-k},
\]
with $u_k=a_k\zeta^{-k^2}$ and $v_m=\zeta^{m^2}$, for $-(n-1)\le m\le n-1$. This is an ordinary convolution of a vector of length $n$ with a vector of length $2n-1$. Pad both with zeros to a power of $2$ at least $3n-2$. The convolution becomes cyclic, with no wrap-around. Theorem~\ref{thm:convolution} and three FFTs of that length compute it in $O(n\log n)$. The extra multiplications by powers of $\zeta$ cost $O(n)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:nttsmall}
\idea Run the same three steps as with complex numbers, with $9$ in the role of $e^{2\pi i/8}$.
\writeup The powers of $3$ modulo $17$ begin $3$, $9$, $10$, $13$, $5$, $15$, $11$, $16$. So $3$ has order $16$, and $w=9$ has order $8$. We use $w^{-1}=2$, because $9\cdot2=18\equiv1$. The transform $\hat a_j=\sum_k a_k 2^{jk}$ evaluates at the powers of $2$: $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, $16$, $15$, $13$, $9$. For $a=(1,2,3)$ the values $A(2^j)$ are $6$, $0$, $6$, $5$, $2$, $9$, $7$, $7$. For $b=(4,5)$ the values $B(2^j)$ are $9$, $14$, $7$, $10$, $16$, $11$, $1$, $15$. The products modulo $17$ are $3$, $0$, $8$, $16$, $15$, $14$, $7$, $3$. Inverting, $c_k=15\sum_j\hat c_j 9^{jk}$ modulo $17$, gives $c_0=4$, $c_1=13$, $c_2=5$, $c_3=15$, and $c_k=0$ for $k\ge4$. The true product has a coefficient $22$ where the transform found $5$, and $22\equiv5\pmod{17}$. Indeed $(1+2X+3X^2)(4+5X)=4+13X+22X^2+15X^3$. The coefficient $22$ exceeds $p=17$, so this prime is too small for an exact product. Theorem~\ref{thm:ntt} asks for $p>N(B-1)^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:midpoint}
\idea Taking midpoints treats every vertex the same way, so the map is diagonal in the Fourier basis of $\C^n$. The mode $k\mapsto\omega^{jk}$ is multiplied by $\frac{1+\omega^j}2=\cos\frac{\pi j}n\,e^{i\pi j/n}$. The centroid is mode $0$. The largest remaining moduli are those of $j=1$ and $j=n-1$, both $\cos\frac\pi n$. After many steps only these two modes are visible, and two opposite modes make an affine image of a regular polygon.

\writeup (a) By discrete Fourier inversion, Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}, one has $z_k=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat z_j\omega^{jk}$ with $\hat z_j=\frac1n\sum_k z_k\omega^{-jk}$, and $\hat z_0=0$ is the centroid. For the mode $u_k=\omega^{jk}$,
\[
(Mu)_k=\frac12(1+\omega^j)\omega^{jk},\qquad\lambda_j=\frac{1+\omega^j}2=e^{i\pi j/n}\cos\frac{\pi j}n.
\]
So $(M^tz)_k=\sum_j\lambda_j^t\hat z_j\omega^{jk}$. Now $\lambda_1=\cos\frac\pi n\,e^{i\pi/n}$ and $\lambda_{n-1}=\cos\frac\pi n\,e^{-i\pi/n}$. For $2\le j\le n-2$, one has $|\lambda_j|=\bigl|\cos\frac{\pi j}n\bigr|<\cos\frac\pi n$. Dividing by $\cos^t\frac\pi n$, those modes tend to $0$. What remains is $a e^{i\pi t/n}\omega^k+b e^{-i\pi t/n}\omega^{-k}$, with $a=\hat z_1$ and $b=\hat z_{n-1}$.

(b) The map $L(u)=au+b\bar u$ is linear over $\R$ on $\C=\R^2$, and $L(\omega^k)=a\omega^k+b\omega^{-k}$. On the basis $1,i$, with $a=a_1+ia_2$ and $b=b_1+ib_2$, one has $L(1)=(a_1+b_1,a_2+b_2)$ and $L(i)=(b_2-a_2,a_1-b_1)$, so $\det L=|a|^2-|b|^2$. The rescaled polygons approach the image of the regular polygon, rotated by $\frac{\pi t}n$, under an invertible linear map when $|a|\ne|b|$. The image is a polygon inscribed in an ellipse, at equally spaced parameters.

(c) For $n=4$, one has $\lambda_2=\frac{1+\omega^2}2=0$. After one step only the modes $1$ and $3$ remain, and $Mz$ is an affine image of a square, a parallelogram. This is Varignon's theorem. The midpoints of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram.
\tools This chapter (discrete Fourier inversion); Volume~1, Chapters~5 and~7 (roots of unity, determinants).
\leads Darboux (1878) studied this iteration, and Berlekamp, Gilbert and Sinden (1965) used it for random polygons. Averaging a value with its two neighbours on a cycle of $n$ vertices multiplies Fourier mode $j$ by $\cos\frac{\pi j}n$. After $t$ steps the slowest non-constant modes have been multiplied by $\cos^t\frac\pi n$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:napoleon}
\idea This is the case $n=3$ of Problem~\ref{pb:pdn}, and it is worth doing by hand. The centres are apexes of isosceles triangles with apex angle $120^\circ$, a circulant operation on the triangle. It kills one of the two non-trivial Fourier modes of $\Z/3\Z$. One mode alone is an equilateral triangle. The signed area of a polygon is a sum of contributions $\pm|\hat z_j|^2$ of its modes.

\writeup Let $\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}$, and let $z_0,z_1,z_2$ be $A$, $B$, $C$, labelled counterclockwise. The point $p$ with $z_k-p=\omega(z_{k+1}-p)$ is the centre of the rotation by $120^\circ$ that sends $z_{k+1}$ to $z_k$, so it is the centre of the equilateral triangle on the side $z_kz_{k+1}$. For $z_k=0$ and $z_{k+1}=1$ one finds $p=\frac{-\omega}{1-\omega}=\frac12-\frac i{2\sqrt3}$, below the side, outside the counterclockwise triangle. So $p_k=\frac{z_k-\omega z_{k+1}}{1-\omega}$. Write $z_k=\hat z_0+\hat z_1\omega^k+\hat z_2\omega^{2k}$. The mode $\omega^{jk}$ is multiplied by $\frac{1-\omega^{j+1}}{1-\omega}$: the factor is $1$ for $j=0$, it is $\frac{1-\omega^2}{1-\omega}=1+\omega$ for $j=1$, and it is $0$ for $j=2$. So
\[
p_k=\hat z_0+(1+\omega)\hat z_1\,\omega^k,
\]
an equilateral triangle centred at $\hat z_0$, the centroid of $ABC$. Inwards, $\omega$ becomes $\omega^2$. Then $p'_k=\frac{z_k-\omega^2 z_{k+1}}{1-\omega^2}$ kills the mode $j=1$, and $p'_k=\hat z_0+c\,\hat z_2\omega^{2k}$ with $c=1+\omega^2$. The inner triangle is equilateral, with the same centre. This proves (a) and the first half of (b).

The signed area of a polygon is $\frac12\operatorname{Im}\sum_k\bar z_k z_{k+1}$. With $z_k=\sum_j\hat z_j\omega^{jk}$ and $\sum_k\omega^{(l-j)k}=3\cdot\mathbf 1_{j=l}$, one gets $\sum_k\bar z_k z_{k+1}=3\sum_j|\hat z_j|^2\omega^j$. The signed area is $\frac{3\sqrt3}4\bigl(|\hat z_1|^2-|\hat z_2|^2\bigr)$. For $ABC$ this is the area. The outer triangle has only mode $1$, and $|1+\omega|=1$, so its area is $\frac{3\sqrt3}4|\hat z_1|^2$. The inner triangle has only mode $2$, and $|1+\omega^2|=1$, so its signed area is $-\frac{3\sqrt3}4|\hat z_2|^2$. It is clockwise, of area $\frac{3\sqrt3}4|\hat z_2|^2$. The difference of the areas is the area of $ABC$.

\leads The theorem is attributed to Napoleon Bonaparte, who was a good amateur geometer, though there is no evidence that he found it. It was printed by W.~Rutherford in 1825. \begin{solution}{pb:pdn}
\idea Erecting the same triangle on every side is a circulant map, diagonal on Fourier modes. The apex angle $\frac{2\pi k}n$ is tuned to kill exactly one mode. The maps are diagonal in the same basis, so they commute. After $n-2$ of them, only the centroid and one regular polygon remain.

\writeup (a) Solving $z_k-p=e^{i\theta}(z_{k+1}-p)$ gives $p=\frac{z_k-e^{i\theta}z_{k+1}}{1-e^{i\theta}}$, so $A_\theta$ is linear. For $z_k=\omega^{jk}$,
\[
(A_\theta z)_k=\omega^{jk}\frac{1-e^{i\theta}\omega^j}{1-e^{i\theta}}.
\]
The eigenvalue is $\mu_j(\theta)=\frac{1-e^{i\theta}\omega^j}{1-e^{i\theta}}$. It equals $1$ for $j=0$, and it equals $0$ precisely when $e^{i\theta}=\omega^{-j}$, that is, when $\theta=\frac{2\pi(n-j)}n$.

(b) The map $A_{2\pi k/n}$ kills the mode $j=n-k$. All the maps $A_\theta$ are diagonal in the Fourier basis, so they commute. The product of $A_{2\pi k/n}$ over $k=1,\ldots,n-2$, in any order, multiplies mode $j$ by $\prod_k\mu_j\bigl(\frac{2\pi k}n\bigr)$. That product is $0$ for $j=2,\ldots,n-1$, it is $1$ for $j=0$, and it is some constant $c$ for $j=1$. The result is $w_k=\hat z_0+c\,\hat z_1\omega^k$, a regular $n$-gon, or a point if $c\hat z_1=0$, centred at the centroid of $z$.

(c) For $n=3$ there is one step, with $\theta=120^\circ$. That is Napoleon's theorem, Problem~\ref{pb:napoleon}. For $n=4$, the angle $\theta=90^\circ$ erects squares on the sides and takes their centres, and $\theta=180^\circ$ takes midpoints. The centres of the squares erected on the sides of a quadrilateral form a quadrilateral whose midpoint polygon is a square. By Varignon's theorem, Problem~\ref{pb:midpoint}, the order can be reversed: the centres of the squares on the sides of the Varignon parallelogram form a square.
\tools This chapter (discrete Fourier inversion); Volume~1, Chapters~5 and~7 (rotations, diagonalisation).
\leads Petr (1908), Douglas (1940) and Neumann (1941) found this independently. For $n=4$ the statement contains van Aubel's theorem. The segments joining the centres of opposite squares are equal and perpendicular.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:cdg}
\idea The walk is a random binary expansion $X_n=\sum_{j<n}2^{n-1-j}\varepsilon_j$, so its Fourier transform is a product. A factor is small as soon as the doubling orbit of $t/p$ visits the far half of the circle. An orbit cannot stay near $0$ for more than $\log_2 p$ steps, because doubling pushes it away.

\writeup (a) The variable $X_n$ takes at most $3^n$ values, so its law puts mass $1$ on a set $A$ with $|A|\le3^n$. That set has uniform measure $|A|/p$. The total variation distance is at least $1-\frac{3^n}p$.

(b) By induction, $X_n\equiv\sum_{j<n}2^{n-1-j}\varepsilon_j$. The steps $\varepsilon_j$ are independent, and $\mathbb E\bigl[e^{2i\pi s\varepsilon/p}\bigr]=\frac{1+2\cos(2\pi s/p)}3$. The transform is the product $\prod_{j<n}\frac{1+2\cos(2\pi 2^{n-1-j}t/p)}3$, which is the stated product after the change of index $j\mapsto n-1-j$.

(c) Let $\theta_j\in\,]-\frac12,\frac12]$ be the representative of $2^jt/p$ modulo $1$. Since $t\not\equiv0$, the number $\theta_j$ is a non-zero multiple of $\frac1p$, so $|\theta_j|\ge\frac1p$. If $|\theta_j|<\frac14$, then $2\theta_j$ still lies in $]-\frac12,\frac12[$ and $\theta_{j+1}=2\theta_j$. If $|\theta_j|<\frac14$ for every $j$ in a window of length $L$, then $|\theta_{j_0+L-1}|=2^{L-1}|\theta_{j_0}|\ge\frac{2^{L-1}}p\ge1$, which is impossible. Some $j$ in the window has $|\theta_j|\ge\frac14$, so $\cos(2\pi\theta_j)\le0$.

(d) When the cosine is at most $0$, the factor $\bigl|\frac{1+2\cos}3\bigr|$ is at most $\frac13$. Every factor has modulus at most $1$. Among $j<n$ there are $\lfloor n/L\rfloor$ disjoint windows of length $L$, so $|\hat P(t)|\le3^{-\lfloor n/L\rfloor}$ for $t\ne0$. Cauchy--Schwarz and Parseval on $\Z/p\Z$, Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}, give
\[
\begin{aligned}d_n=\frac12\sum_x\Bigl|P(X_n=x)-\frac1p\Bigr|&\le\frac12\sqrt p\Bigl(\sum_x\Bigl|P(X_n=x)-\frac1p\Bigr|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}\\&=\frac12\Bigl(\sum_{t\ne0}|\hat P(t)|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}\le\frac12\sqrt p\,3^{-\lfloor n/L\rfloor}.\end{aligned}
\]
So $d_n\le\varepsilon$ once $\lfloor n/L\rfloor\ge\log_3\frac{\sqrt p}{2\varepsilon}$, which is $n=O(\log^2 p)$ for fixed $\varepsilon$.
\tools Volume~4, Chapter~6 (characteristic functions); this chapter (inversion and Parseval on $\Z/p\Z$); Volume~4, Chapter~9 (total variation).
\leads Chung, Diaconis and Graham proved that $O(\log p\log\log p)$ steps always suffice, that about $1.02\log_2 p$ steps suffice for almost all primes $p$, and that for some primes, of the form $2^k-1$, the order $\log p\log\log p$ is needed. The walk $X_{n+1}=X_n+\varepsilon_n$ needs about $p^2$ steps. Multiplying by $2$ spreads the position out. Hildebrand, and Eberhard and Varj\'u, have since sharpened the picture.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:newman}
\idea To pick out a residue class modulo $3$, average the three characters of $\Z/3\Z$. Digit sums then factor over the bits, and the sums reduce to powers of $1+\omega$ and $1-\omega$.

\writeup (a) Let $N_j$ be the count. Since $\frac13\sum_{t=0}^2\omega^{tm}$ is $1$ if $3$ divides $m$ and $0$ otherwise,
\[
N_j=\frac13\sum_{t=0}^2\omega^{-tj}\sum_{n<2^k}\omega^{ts(n)}=\frac13\sum_{t=0}^2\omega^{-tj}(1+\omega^t)^k.
\]
The map from $n<2^k$ to its $k$ bits is a bijection onto $\{0,1\}^k$, and $\omega^{ts(n)}$ is the product over the bits. The term $t=0$ is $2^k$. And $1+\omega=e^{i\pi/3}$, so the term $t=1$ is $e^{i\pi(k-2j)/3}$, and the term $t=2$ is its conjugate. Hence $N_j=\frac13\bigl(2^k+2\cos\frac{\pi(k-2j)}3\bigr)$.

(b) The same filter, now on the multiples of $3$, gives, for $K\ge1$,
\[
\sum_{\substack{n<2^K\\3\mid n}}(-1)^{s(n)}=\frac13\sum_{t=0}^2\prod_{i=0}^{K-1}\bigl(1-\omega^{t\,2^i}\bigr).
\]
The term $t=0$ vanishes. Since $2^i\equiv1$ or $2\pmod3$ according to the parity of $i$, the factors for $t=1$ alternate between $1-\omega$ and $1-\omega^2$, and $(1-\omega)(1-\omega^2)=3$. For $K=2k$ the terms $t=1$ and $t=2$ are both $3^k$, and the sum is $\frac23\cdot3^k=2\cdot3^{k-1}$. For $K=2k+1$ they are $3^k(1-\omega)$ and $3^k(1-\omega^2)$, and the sum is $3^k$. With $N=4^k$, the sum below $N$ is $\frac23\cdot3^k$. Since $3^k=N^{\log3/\log4}$, the sum equals $\frac23 N^{\log3/\log4}$. For $k=1$ and $N=4$ the multiples of $3$ below $4$ are $0$ and $3$, both with even digit sum, and the sum of the two signs is $2$. And $\frac23\cdot3^1=2$, while $3^1=3$ is a larger number. The sum is two thirds of $3^k$, and $3^k$ itself is the size of the order $N^{\log3/\log4}$.
\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~5 (roots of unity); this chapter (Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum}).}
\leads D.~J.~Newman (1969) proved that $\sum_{n<N,\,3\mid n}(-1)^{s(n)}$ is positive for every $N\ge1$, and of exact order $N^{\log3/\log4}$. J.~Coquet (1983) found the exact formula. The sum is $N^{\log3/\log4}F(\log_4 N)$ plus a bounded error, with $F$ continuous, periodic, and nowhere differentiable. Mauduit and Rivat (2010) proved that $s(p)$ is equidistributed modulo $2$ along the primes (Problem~\ref{pb:digitsclt}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bent}
\idea Parseval forces the $2^n$ squared coefficients to average $2^{-n}$, so the largest is at least that. The number $2^n\hat f(a)$ is an integer, which decides when the coefficients can all be equal. Distance to a linear function is a correlation.

\writeup (a) One has $\sum_a\hat f(a)^2=\mathbb E[f^2]=1$, a sum of $2^n$ terms, so some $\hat f(a)^2\ge2^{-n}$. If $f$ is bent, then $2^n\hat f(a)$ is an integer equal to $\pm2^{n/2}$, and $2^{n/2}$ is an integer only for $n$ even.

(b) Compute $\hat f(a,b)=2^{-2m}\sum_x(-1)^{a\cdot x}\sum_y(-1)^{(x+b)\cdot y}$. The inner sum is $2^m$ if $x=b$ and $0$ otherwise, so $\hat f(a,b)=2^{-m}(-1)^{a\cdot b}$. The modulus is $2^{-m}=2^{-n/2}$, with $n=2m$.

(c) For $g=\pm(-1)^{a\cdot x}$, the number of disagreements is $\frac12\sum_x\bigl(1-f(x)g(x)\bigr)=2^{n-1}(1\mp\hat f(a))$. Minimising over $a$ and the sign gives $2^{n-1}\bigl(1-\max_a|\hat f(a)|\bigr)$, which is at most $2^{n-1}(1-2^{-n/2})$ by (a). Equality means $\max_a|\hat f(a)|=2^{-n/2}$. The squares average $2^{-n}$, so they are then all equal to $2^{-n}$, and $f$ is bent.

(d) One has $\hat f(0)=2^{-n}\sum_x f(x)=\pm2^{-n/2}$, so $\sum_x f(x)=\pm2^{n/2}$. The number of $x$ with $f(x)=-1$ is $\frac12\bigl(2^n\mp2^{n/2}\bigr)=2^{n-1}\mp2^{n/2-1}$.
\tools{Volume~3, Chapter~11 (characters of finite abelian groups and their orthogonality, which gives Parseval on $\mathbb F_2^n$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}).}
\leads Bent functions were defined and named by Oscar Rothaus in the 1960s, in research published in 1976. A cipher whose components are close to linear functions is vulnerable to linear cryptanalysis (Matsui, 1993), so high nonlinearity is a design goal. By (d) a bent function is unbalanced, and cryptographers trade a little nonlinearity for balance. The set $\{x:f(x)=-1\}$ of a bent function is a Hadamard difference set, and the matrix $\bigl(f(x+y)\bigr)_{x,y}$ is a Hadamard matrix (Problem~\ref{pb:hadamard}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:uncertainty}
\idea The transform evaluates a polynomial at roots of unity. A polynomial with $s$ terms is pinned down by $s$ consecutive evaluations, because the matrix is a Vandermonde matrix at $s$ distinct points. So $\hat a$ has no run of $s$ zeros. A circle of $n$ places with no run of $s$ zeros needs at least $\frac ns$ non-zero places.

\writeup (a) Let $\operatorname{supp}a=\{k_1,\ldots,k_s\}$, and set $x_r=\omega^{-k_r}$, so the $x_r$ are distinct and non-zero. Suppose $\hat a_j=0$ for $j=j_0+i$ and $0\le i<s$. Then
\[
0=\hat a_{j_0+i}=\sum_{r=1}^s a_{k_r}x_r^{j_0+i}=\sum_{r=1}^s\bigl(a_{k_r}x_r^{j_0}\bigr)x_r^i.
\]
The matrix $(x_r^i)$ is a Vandermonde matrix, with determinant $\prod_{r<r'}(x_{r'}-x_r)\ne0$ (Volume~1, Chapter~7). So every $a_{k_r}$ vanishes, a contradiction.

(b) Let $s=|\operatorname{supp}a|$ and let $J=\operatorname{supp}\hat a$. The set $J$ is not empty, because $\hat a\ne0$ by inversion. Give each index of $\Z/n\Z$ to the nearest element of $J$ at or before it, going round the circle. By (a), an element of $J$ is followed by at most $s-1$ indices outside $J$, so it receives at most $s$ indices. Every index is given to someone, so $n\le s\,|J|$.

(c) Here $a_k=1$ for $k=dm$ with $0\le m<\frac nd$, and $a_k=0$ otherwise. So $\hat a_j=\sum_{m<n/d}\omega^{-jdm}$. The root $\omega^d$ has order $\frac nd$. Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum}, with $\frac nd$ in place of $n$, says $\hat a_j=\frac nd$ if $\frac nd$ divides $j$, and $\hat a_j=0$ otherwise. The support of $a$ has $\frac nd$ elements and the support of $\hat a$ has $d$. The product is $n$. For a check, take $n=6$ and $d=2$. Then $a=(1,0,1,0,1,0)$, the support has $3$ elements, and $\hat a$ is non-zero at the multiples of $3$, two of them. The product is $6$.

(d) Let $a$ and $a'$ each have at most $s$ non-zero entries, with $\hat a_j=\hat a'_j$ on $2s$ consecutive indices. Then $b=a-a'$ has support of size $s'\le2s$, and $\hat b$ vanishes on $2s\ge s'$ consecutive indices. If $b\ne0$ this contradicts (a). So $a=a'$.
\tools{This chapter (definition of the transform, inversion, Lemma~\ref{lem:rootsum}); Volume~1, Chapter~7 (Vandermonde determinant).}
\leads Part (d) is the principle behind Reed--Solomon codes. Send $2s$ extra evaluations, and any $s$ errors can be located. It is also an early theorem of compressed sensing, where a short signal is recovered from few Fourier coefficients. For $n=p$ prime the truth is stronger. Tao (2005) showed $|\operatorname{supp}a|+|\operatorname{supp}\hat a|\ge p+1$, from a theorem of Chebotarev that every minor of $F_p$ is non-zero.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw07a}
\idea Two of the four inputs vanish, and $\omega=i$, so each bin is $1$ plus $(-1)$ times a power of $i$.
\writeup With $\omega=i$,
\begin{align*}
\hat a_0&=1+0+(-1)+0=0,\\
\hat a_1&=1+(-1)\cdot i^{-2}=1+(-1)\cdot(-1)=2,\\
\hat a_2&=1+(-1)\cdot i^{-4}=1-1=0,\\
\hat a_3&=1+(-1)\cdot i^{-6}=1+(-1)\cdot(-1)=2.
\end{align*}
So $\hat a=(0,2,0,2)$. The left side of Parseval is $1^2+0+(-1)^2+0=2$. The right side is $\frac14(0+4+0+4)=2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw07b}
\idea One butterfly is two linear combinations, $E\pm wO$. The parallelogram law cancels the cross term, and $|w|=1$ makes the two inputs contribute the same energy.
\writeup (a) Lemma~\ref{lem:butterfly} with $n=8$, $m=4$ and $j=1$ gives $\hat a_1=2+2w$ and $\hat a_5=2-2w$, where $w=\omega^{-1}=e^{-2\pi i/8}=e^{-i\pi/4}$.
(b) Set $u=2$ and $v=2w$. Then $|u+v|^2+|u-v|^2=2|u|^2+2|v|^2=2\cdot4+2\cdot4=16$, because $|w|=1$. Writing $w=\frac{\sqrt2}2(1-i)$ gives $2w=\sqrt2(1-i)$, so
\[
|2+2w|^2=(2+\sqrt2)^2+(\sqrt2)^2=8+4\sqrt2,\qquad|2-2w|^2=8-4\sqrt2.
\]
Numerically these are about $13.6569$ and $2.3431$, and their sum is $16$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw07c}
\idea The convolution at index $k$ uses only $a_0$ and $a_1$. Bin $0$ of any DFT is the sum of the entries, so the product of the two bin-$0$ values is the sum of the convolution.
\writeup (a) With indices modulo $4$, $(a*b)_k=a_0b_k+a_1b_{k-1}=b_k+2b_{k-1}$. The four values are
\[
\begin{aligned}
(a*b)_0&=1+2\cdot0=1,\\
(a*b)_1&=0+2\cdot1=2,\\
(a*b)_2&=3+2\cdot0=3,\\
(a*b)_3&=0+2\cdot3=6.
\end{aligned}
\]
So $a*b=(1,2,3,6)$. The entries add to $12$.
(b) With $\omega=i$,
\[
\hat a=(3,\ 1-2i,\ -1,\ 1+2i),\qquad\hat b=(4,\ -2,\ 4,\ -2).
\]
The entrywise product is $(12,\ -2+4i,\ -4,\ -2-4i)$. The DFT of $(1,2,3,6)$ has the same four values. At bin $0$ both sides equal $12$, the product of the two sums of entries. As a second check, $1^2+2^2+0+0=5$ and $\frac14\bigl(9+|1-2i|^2+1+|1+2i|^2\bigr)=\frac14(9+5+1+5)=5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw07d}
\idea The recurrence unfolds into four lines of arithmetic. The polynomial product is a convolution that does not wrap, because the degrees add to $3$ and the length is $4$. The samples of $\cos t$ repeat every four steps, and frequency $-1$ lands in bin $3$.
\writeup (a) One has $M(2)=1$, $M(4)=2\cdot1+2=4$, $M(8)=2\cdot4+4=12$, and $M(16)=2\cdot12+8=32$. Likewise $S(2)=2$, $S(4)=8$, $S(8)=24$, and $S(16)=64$. These are $\frac{16}2\log_2 16=32$ and $16\log_2 16=64$.
(b) Pad to $a=(1,1,0,0)$ and $b=(1,1,1,0)$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
(a*b)_0&=1,\\
(a*b)_1&=1+1=2,\\
(a*b)_2&=1+1=2,\\
(a*b)_3&=1.
\end{aligned}
\]
The schoolbook product is $(1+X)(1+X+X^2)=1+2X+2X^2+X^3$. The four coefficients agree. Their sum is $6$, and $\hat a_0\hat b_0=(1+1)(1+1+1)=6$.
(c) The samples are $\cos0=1$, $\cos\frac\pi2=0$, $\cos\pi=-1$, $\cos\frac{3\pi}2=0$, so $a=(1,0,-1,0)$. Exercise~\ref{ex:rw07a} computed $\hat a=(0,2,0,2)$, and $\frac14\hat a=\bigl(0,\frac12,0,\frac12\bigr)$. Since $\cos t=\frac12 e^{it}+\frac12 e^{-it}$, one has $c_1(f)=c_{-1}(f)=\frac12$ and every other coefficient is $0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:aliasing} puts $c_1$ in bin $1$ and $c_{-1}$ in bin $3$, because $-1\equiv3\pmod4$. Both contributions equal $\frac12$.
\end{solution}
