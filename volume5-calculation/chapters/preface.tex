\chapter*{Preface}
Some of the numbers in this volume are exact. The sum of the reciprocals of the squares is $\pi^2/6$. Wallis's product writes $\pi$ as an infinite product of rationals. The Gaussian integral equals $\sqrt\pi$. And $\zeta(-1)=-\frac1{12}$, in the sense the functional equation gives that value. Other numbers are approximations with a remainder one can name. Stirling's series is one. Gauss's arithmetic--geometric mean is another: each iteration doubles the number of correct digits of $\pi$.

The book is a course in getting those numbers. A sum, a product or an integral either has an exact value, or it has an approximation whose error is part of the theorem.

Chapters~1--3 treat finite sums and the series that encode them. A power sum becomes an antidifference. A linear recurrence becomes a rational function. The sine function becomes a product over its zeros, and that product evaluates $\zeta(2k)$. Chapters~4 and~5 compute integrals. Differentiation under the integral sign is justified before it is used, and the Gamma function is picked out of all the curves through the factorials by log-convexity. Chapters~6--8 are one idea in three places. On the circle a function becomes its Fourier series. On $\Z/n\Z$ the same idea is the discrete Fourier transform, and the fast algorithm that computes it. On the line it is the Fourier transform, and Poisson summation decides the sign of a Gauss sum. Chapter~9 turns a real integral into a residue. Chapter~10 builds the zeta function and proves its functional equation. Chapter~11 says what a divergent series means once it carries a remainder. Chapter~12 spends the preceding tools on one question: in how many ways can $n$ be written as a sum of positive integers? The answer is the formula of Hardy and Ramanujan, and the symmetry in it is the same symmetry as the functional equation. Chapter~13 is Gauss's arithmetic--geometric mean.

The theorems are the toolkit Volume~6 uses on the primes, and that the later volumes use on probability. They are also a pleasure on their own, because different methods keep returning the same number.

Every theorem, proposition and lemma is proved. A result quoted only for its interest is a remark, with a reference, and nothing later depends on it. Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a good afternoon and \textbf{$\star\star\star$} for a gem. Each one has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its chapter. The solution begins with \emph{How to find it}, then the \emph{Write-up}. A ``Pause and think'' box asks you to guess before the text continues.

Very little is used without a proof, and only inside exercises. Two exercises admit Tonelli's theorem for non-negative integrands (Volume~7, Theorem~9.5). One exercise of Chapter~6 admits the volume of a convex set (Volume~7, Theorem~7.11). One exercise of Chapter~5 quotes Fulton and Harris, Proposition~3.12, on the exterior powers of the reflection representation. A closing problem of Chapter~13 admits Mertens's third theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~1), and one solution quotes the prime number theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~5) for a comparison only. Each of these is labelled admitted or quoted. No theorem of this volume depends on them. Chapter~4 proves Fubini's theorem for continuous functions on a rectangle, which is all the text itself uses.

The series asks whether pure order and pure chance can be told apart, and why the primes look random while they obey laws. This volume builds the instruments: series, integrals, and the functional equation of $\zeta$. The next volume takes up the primes in bulk, where a law and a Gaussian meet. The zeros do not yet carry the primes.
