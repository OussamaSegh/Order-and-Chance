\chapter{The Hardy--Ramanujan Formula}\label{ch:hr}
\begin{center}\itshape
Four can be written in five ways. The series of 1918\\
gives all thirteen digits of $p(200)=3\,972\,999\,029\,388$.
\end{center}

One symmetry has already given two formulas. In Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta}, Poisson summation produced the theta relation, and the theta relation produced the functional equation of $\zeta$. The same summation, applied to Euler's pentagonal series from Chapter~\ref{ch:generating}, produces a second relation of the same kind. That relation counts partitions.

Let $p(n)$ be the number of partitions of $n$. You know $p(4)=5$: the partitions are $4$, $3+1$, $2+2$, $2+1+1$ and $1+1+1+1$. The question of this chapter is the size of $p(n)$ for large $n$, and the first hard number is $p(200)$.

Percy MacMahon, a former major of the Royal Artillery, was the leading expert on partitions in England. He computed $p(n)$ by hand, from Euler's pentagonal recurrence, for every $n\le 200$. He did the arithmetic for two colleagues at Trinity College, Cambridge, who wanted a numerical test of a formula. Godfrey Harold Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan had guessed that $p(n)$ grows like $e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$. They then wrote $p(n)$ as an integral around a circle, read the generating function near every point where it blows up, and added the contributions. The paper of 1918, with MacMahon's table at the end, gives a series whose first few terms produce $p(200)=3\,972\,999\,029\,388$ to within a fraction of a unit. Littlewood later wrote that ``a severe numerical test was now made, which elicited the astonishing facts about $p(100)$ and $p(200)$''.

The method is called the \emph{circle method}. Hardy and Littlewood turned it on sums of powers and on sums of primes. Volume~14 follows that story: the circle method, and the Kloosterman sums that appear when more than one arc is kept. That is a preview. Nothing in this chapter depends on it. Here we prove the leading term, and we keep every constant in the error.
\[
p(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,n}.
\]

The table says $p(100)$ is about $1.9\times10^8$. Before reading on, guess the shape of the answer. Is $p(n)$ closer to a power of $n$, to $2^n$, or to something in between? One clue is already proved: Exercise~\ref{ex:partitionbound} of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} showed $\ln p(n)\le\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$. The generating function is $P(z)=\sum p(n)z^n$. Cauchy's formula writes $p(n)$ as an integral of $P(z)z^{-n-1}$ over a circle just inside the unit circle. The integrand is largest near $z=1$. The symmetry of $\eta$ describes $P$ there, and the Gaussian integral of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} turns that description into the main term. The short arc near $z=1$ is the \emph{major arc}. The rest of the circle is the \emph{minor arc}, and $P$ is exponentially smaller there. Figure~\ref{fig:rw12-farey} draws the cut for $n=100$.

\section{The partition product as a function}

Chapter~\ref{ch:generating} treated $\sum p(n)x^n=\prod_k\frac1{1-x^k}$ as an identity of formal series. The letter $x$ was not a number. To integrate around a circle we need a complex number $z$ with $|z|<1$. The next lemma checks that the formulas we want survive that change.

Two notations come first. For $j\in\Z$ let $g_j=\frac{j(3j-1)}2$ be the $j$-th pentagonal number, so that $g_0,g_1,g_{-1},g_2,g_{-2},\dots$ are $0,1,2,5,7,\dots$ Each step of this list goes up, because $g_{-j}-g_j=j$ and $g_{j+1}-g_{-j}=2j+1$ for $j\ge1$. The numbers $g_j$ are distinct non-negative integers.

\begin{lemma}[the product on the disc]\label{lem:pdisc}
Let $|z|<1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The series $P(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}p(n)z^n$ converges absolutely, and $P$ is holomorphic on the disc $D(0,1)$.
\item The series $E(z)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^jz^{g_j}$ converges absolutely, $E$ is holomorphic on $D(0,1)$, and $P(z)E(z)=1$. In particular $P(z)\ne0$.
\item $\prod_{k\le N}(1-z^k)\to E(z)$ as $N\to\infty$. So $P(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac1{1-z^k}$ and $E(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}(1-z^k)$.
\item The series $L(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}$ converges absolutely and $P(z)=e^{L(z)}$. Consequently $|P(z)|\le P(|z|)$, and $\log P(r)=L(r)$ for $0\le r<1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) \emph{Real $r$ first.} Let $0\le r<1$ and $N\ge1$. The finite product $\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}$ is a product of $N$ geometric series, each absolutely convergent. Multiplying them out, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:eulerproduct}, the coefficient of $r^n$ counts the partitions of $n$ into parts of size at most $N$. For $n\le N$ that is every partition of $n$. Every term is non-negative, so
\[
\sum_{n\le N}p(n)r^n\le\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}.
\]
Now bound the product. For $0\le u<1$, $-\log(1-u)=\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{u^i}i\le\sum_{i\ge1}u^i=\frac u{1-u}$. With $u=r^k$ and $1-r^k\ge1-r$,
\[
\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}\le\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{r^k}{1-r}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(\frac r{(1-r)^2}\Bigr).
\]
The bound does not depend on $N$. The partial sums of $\sum p(n)r^n$ are bounded, so the series converges. \emph{Complex $z$.} Since $|p(n)z^n|=p(n)|z|^n$, the series converges absolutely for $|z|<1$. Its radius of convergence is at least $1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:powerseries} says the sum is holomorphic on $D(0,1)$.

(b) The exponents $g_j$ are distinct non-negative integers, so $\sum_j|z|^{g_j}\le\sum_{n\ge0}|z|^n<\infty$. Listed by increasing exponent, $E$ is a power series with coefficients $0$ and $\pm1$ and radius at least $1$, so it is holomorphic. In $\Z[[x]]$, Proposition~\ref{prop:eulerproduct} gives $\bigl(\sum p(n)x^n\bigr)\prod_k(1-x^k)=1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:pentagonal} says $\prod_k(1-x^k)=\sum_j(-1)^jx^{g_j}$. The Cauchy product of the two coefficient sequences is $1,0,0,\dots$ Both series converge absolutely at $z$. The product of their sums is the sum of their Cauchy product (the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:packets}), which is $1$.

(c) Let $E_N(x)=\prod_{k\le N}(1-x^k)$, a polynomial. In $\Z[[x]]$, $E_N(x)\sum p(n)x^n=\prod_{k>N}\frac1{1-x^k}$. Expanding as before, this is $\sum_nc_N(n)x^n$, where $c_N(n)$ counts the partitions of $n$ with every part larger than $N$. So $c_N(0)=1$, $c_N(n)=0$ for $1\le n\le N$, and $c_N(n)\le p(n)$ always. A polynomial and an absolutely convergent series multiply term by term, so
\[
\bigl|E_N(z)P(z)-1\bigr|=\Bigl|\sum_{n>N}c_N(n)z^n\Bigr|\le\sum_{n>N}p(n)|z|^n,
\]
the tail of a convergent series. The tail tends to $0$. Hence $E_N(z)\to\frac1{P(z)}=E(z)$ by (b).

(d) \emph{One factor.} For $|u|<1$, $\exp\bigl(\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{u^m}m\bigr)=\frac1{1-u}$. Both sides are holomorphic on $D(0,1)$, and for real $u$ in $\left]-1,1\right[$ the sum is $-\log(1-u)$. Two holomorphic functions on the disc that agree on an interval are equal (Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}). \emph{All factors.} The double family $\frac{z^{km}}m$, for $k,m\ge1$, is summable:
\[
\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\sum_{k\ge1}|z|^{km}=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac{|z|^m}{1-|z|^m}\le\frac1{1-|z|}\sum_{m\ge1}|z|^m<\infty.
\]
Summing over $k$ first gives $L(z)$, which therefore converges absolutely. Summing over $m$ first, for $k\le N$ only, gives the exponent of $\frac1{E_N(z)}$, by the one-factor identity with $u=z^k$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:packets}(c) these partial sums over $k\le N$ tend to the full sum $L(z)$. Since $\exp$ is continuous, $\frac1{E_N(z)}\to e^{L(z)}$, and by (c) the limit is $P(z)$.

For the consequences, $|P(z)|=e^{\operatorname{Re}L(z)}$, and $\bigl|\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}\bigr|\le\frac{|z|^m}{1-|z|^m}$ because $|1-z^m|\ge1-|z|^m$. So $\operatorname{Re}L(z)\le L(|z|)$ and $|P(z)|\le e^{L(|z|)}=P(|z|)$. For real $r$, $L(r)$ is real and $e^{L(r)}=P(r)$, so $L(r)=\log P(r)$.
\end{proof}

A quick check of (b) with the first terms: $(1+z+2z^2+3z^3+5z^4+\cdots)(1-z-z^2+\cdots)=1+0z+0z^2+0z^3+0z^4+\cdots$. The coefficient of $z^4$ is $5-3-2=0$.

Part (d) already gives the right order of magnitude of $P$ near $1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:plog}
For every $t>0$, $\log P(e^{-t})\le\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(d) with $r=e^{-t}$, $\log P(e^{-t})=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac1{e^{mt}-1}$. Since $e^u-1\ge u$, each term is at most $\frac1{m^2t}$. So $\log P(e^{-t})\le\frac1t\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:zeta2k}.
\end{proof}

So $P(e^{-t})$ is at most $e^{\pi^2/(6t)}$. As $t\to0$ it explodes, and the explosion is controlled. The asymptotic formula needs an equality, and the equality comes from a symmetry.

\section{Dedekind's eta function}

Write $\mathbb H=\{\tau\in\C:\operatorname{Im}\tau>0\}$ for the upper half-plane. If $\tau\in\mathbb H$ then $z=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ has $|z|=e^{-2\pi\operatorname{Im}\tau}<1$, so $E(z)$ makes sense.

\begin{definition}
\emph{Dedekind's eta function} is, for $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
\eta(\tau)=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\prod_{m\ge1}\bigl(1-e^{2\pi im\tau}\bigr)=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\,E\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

It is holomorphic on $\mathbb H$, as a product of compositions of holomorphic functions, and it never vanishes, by Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(b). The partition function sits inside it:
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}}{\eta(\tau)}.
\]

The factor $e^{\pi i\tau/12}$ looks odd. It is there because of what it does to the pentagonal exponents. Complete the square:
\[
g_j+\frac1{24}=\frac{36j^2-12j+1}{24}=\frac{(6j-1)^2}{24}.
\]
Multiply the series $E(e^{2\pi i\tau})=\sum_j(-1)^je^{2\pi i\tau g_j}$ by $e^{\pi i\tau/12}=e^{2\pi i\tau/24}$. Each exponent $g_j$ becomes $g_j+\frac1{24}$, and we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etatheta}
\eta(\tau)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^j\,e^{\pi i\tau(6j-1)^2/12}.
\end{equation}
The exponents are now perfect squares, as in the theta series $\sum_ne^{-\pi n^2t}$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:theta}. Read this way, Euler's pentagonal theorem says that $\eta$ is a theta series. Theta series have a symmetry, and Poisson summation finds it.

\begin{think}
The theta relation of Corollary~\ref{cor:theta} exchanges $t$ and $\frac1t$ and costs a factor $\sqrt t$. On the imaginary axis $\tau=it$, formula~\eqref{eq:etatheta} reads $\eta(it)=\sum_j(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}$. What relation between $\eta(it)$ and $\eta(\frac it)$ would you bet on?
\end{think}

Take $\tau$ in the upper half-plane $\mathbb H$. Then $-i\tau$ has real part $\operatorname{Im}\tau>0$, so it sits in the slit plane of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}, where the principal logarithm is holomorphic. Define $\sqrt{-i\tau}=e^{\frac12\operatorname{Log}(-i\tau)}$. That square root has positive real part. On the imaginary axis $\tau=it$ with $t>0$ it is the ordinary positive $\sqrt t$. The map $\tau\mapsto-1/\tau$ stays inside $\mathbb H$, because
\[
\operatorname{Im}\Bigl(-\frac1\tau\Bigr)=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\tau}{|\tau|^2}>0.
\]

\begin{theorem}[the transformation of $\eta$]\label{thm:eta}
For every $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
\eta\Bigl(-\frac1\tau\Bigr)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\;\eta(\tau).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Stage 1: the imaginary axis, by Poisson summation.} Fix $t>0$ and let
\[
f(x)=e^{\pi ix}\,e^{-3\pi t\,(x-\frac16)^2}\qquad(x\in\R).
\]
At an integer $j$, $e^{\pi ij}=(-1)^j$ and $3(j-\frac16)^2=\frac{(6j-1)^2}{12}$. So by~\eqref{eq:etatheta},
\[
\sum_{j\in\Z}f(j)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}=\eta(it).
\]
The job of the next three lines is to compute $\hat f$. First, by Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussianft} with $a=(3t)^{-1/2}$, the Gaussian $e^{-3\pi tx^2}$ has transform $(3t)^{-1/2}e^{-\pi\xi^2/(3t)}$. Second, shifting $x$ by $\frac16$ multiplies the transform by $e^{-2\pi i\xi/6}=e^{-\pi i\xi/3}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbasic}(a)). Third, multiplying by $e^{\pi ix}=e^{2\pi i\frac12x}$ replaces $\xi$ by $\xi-\frac12$ (the same proposition). So
\[
\hat f(\xi)=\frac1{\sqrt{3t}}\,e^{-\pi(\xi-\frac12)^2/(3t)}\,e^{-\pi i(\xi-\frac12)/3}.
\]
Both $f$ and $\hat f$ are Gaussians in modulus, so they are bounded by a constant times $(1+|x|)^{-2}$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} applies: $\sum_jf(j)=\sum_m\hat f(m)$. At an integer $m$ put $n=2m-1$, which runs over the odd integers. Then $(m-\frac12)^2=\frac{n^2}4$ and
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac1{\sqrt{3t}}\sum_{n\text{ odd}}e^{-\pi n^2/(12t)}\,e^{-\pi in/6}.
\]
Group $n$ with $-n$. Since $e^{-\pi in/6}+e^{\pi in/6}=2\cos\frac{\pi n}6$,
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac2{\sqrt{3t}}\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\n\text{ odd}}}\cos\frac{\pi n}6\;e^{-\pi n^2/(12t)}.
\]
The cosines are the bookkeeping. For odd $n\ge1$, $\cos\frac{\pi n}6$ depends on $n$ modulo $12$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccc}
$n\bmod12$ & $1$ & $3$ & $5$ & $7$ & $9$ & $11$\\\hline
$\cos\frac{\pi n}6$ & $\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $0$ & $-\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $-\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $0$ & $\frac{\sqrt3}2$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The multiples of $3$ drop out. What remains are the $n\ge1$ prime to $6$, and each of them is $|6j-1|$ for exactly one $j\in\Z$: the numbers $5,11,17,\dots$ are $6j-1$ with $j\ge1$, and $1,7,13,\dots$ are $1-6j$ with $j\le0$. Check the sign on the table. For $j=0,1,-1,2,-2$ we get $n=1,5,7,11,13$, with cosines $+,-,-,+,+$ times $\frac{\sqrt3}2$. In general $|6j-1|$ is congruent modulo $12$ to $1$ or $11$ when $j$ is even and to $5$ or $7$ when $j$ is odd, so
\[
\cos\frac{\pi|6j-1|}6=(-1)^j\,\frac{\sqrt3}2.
\]
Therefore
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac2{\sqrt{3t}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^je^{-\pi(6j-1)^2/(12t)}=\frac1{\sqrt t}\,\eta\Bigl(\frac it\Bigr),
\]
again by~\eqref{eq:etatheta}, now at $\tau=\frac it$. Poisson summation has given
\[
\eta\Bigl(\frac it\Bigr)=\sqrt t\;\eta(it)\qquad(t>0).
\]
This is the theorem for $\tau=it$, since then $-\frac1\tau=\frac it$ and $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt t$.

\emph{Stage 2: the half-plane, by the identity theorem.} Both $\tau\mapsto\eta(-1/\tau)$ and $\tau\mapsto\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\eta(\tau)$ are holomorphic on $\mathbb H$. The half-plane is open and connected. Stage~1 says the two functions agree on the ray $\{it:t>0\}$, and that ray has accumulation points inside $\mathbb H$. Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}(b) spreads the identity from the ray to the whole half-plane.
\end{proof}

Check a point where the square root is $1$. At $\tau=i$ we have $-\frac1\tau=i$ and $\sqrt{-i\tau}=1$, so the theorem says $\eta(i)=\eta(i)$. Now a point off the axis: at $\tau=0.3+0.7i$, summing forty terms of~\eqref{eq:etatheta} on each side gives two complex numbers that agree to more than thirty decimal places. The square root matters here, since $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt{0.7-0.3i}$ is the principal root, and that root is not real.

Three earlier theorems did all the work. The pentagonal theorem turned the product into a theta series. Poisson summation gave the symmetry on the imaginary axis. The identity theorem carried that symmetry onto the half-plane. Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta} used the same Poisson summation on the plain Gaussian $e^{-\pi t x^2}$ and got the functional equation of $\zeta$. The tool is the same. The Gaussian is not.

Now translate back to partitions.

\begin{lemma}[the transformation of $P$]\label{lem:ptrans}
For $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\;e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}\,e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}\,P\bigl(e^{-2\pi i/\tau}\bigr).
\]
In particular, for real $t>0$,
\[
P(e^{-t})=\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}\;\exp\Bigl(\frac{\pi^2}{6t}-\frac t{24}\Bigr)\,P\bigl(e^{-4\pi^2/t}\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $P(e^{2\pi i\sigma})=e^{\pi i\sigma/12}\,\eta(\sigma)^{-1}$ twice, at $\sigma=\tau$ and at $\sigma=-\frac1\tau$. At $\sigma=-\frac1\tau$ it reads $\eta(-\frac1\tau)^{-1}=e^{\pi i/(12\tau)}P(e^{-2\pi i/\tau})$. With Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} in the middle step,
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}}{\eta(\tau)}=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}\sqrt{-i\tau}}{\eta(-1/\tau)}=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\sqrt{-i\tau}\;e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}P\bigl(e^{-2\pi i/\tau}\bigr).
\]
For the second form take $\tau=\frac{it}{2\pi}$. Then $e^{2\pi i\tau}=e^{-t}$, $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}$, $e^{\pi i\tau/12}=e^{-t/24}$, $\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$ and $e^{-2\pi i/\tau}=e^{-4\pi^2/t}$.
\end{proof}

Read the second form slowly. When $t$ is small, $e^{-4\pi^2/t}$ is tiny, and $P$ of a tiny number is $1$ to many decimals. So
\[
\log P(e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}+\frac12\log\frac t{2\pi}-\frac t{24}+O\bigl(e^{-4\pi^2/t}\bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:plog} was right to leading order, and now every term is present. Try $t=0.01$. The product $P(e^{-0.01})$ is about $1.0947\times10^{70}$. Adding its logarithm directly takes thousands of terms. The lemma computes the same number from the value of $P$ at $e^{-3948}$, which is $1$.

The inversion $\tau\mapsto-1/\tau$ trades the point $z=1$, where the generating function blows up, for the point $z=0$, where the product is $1$. The rest of the chapter is the bookkeeping of that trade.

\section{The theorem of Hardy and Ramanujan}

\begin{theorem}[Hardy and Ramanujan, 1918]\label{thm:hr}
As $n\to\infty$,
\[
p(n)=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,n}\Bigl(1+O\bigl(n^{-1/4}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof has five stages. Each stage has one job, named in italics at its start. The radius of the circle is ours to choose. The whole argument depends on choosing it at the saddle point, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics}.

\begin{proof}
\emph{The integral.} The job of this stage is to write $p(n)$ as an integral on the interval $\bigl[-\frac12,\frac12\bigr]$, and to choose the height $y$ so that the two pieces of the exponent match at $x=0$.

Fix $y>0$, to be chosen, and put $\tau=x+iy$ with $-\frac12\le x\le\frac12$. As $x$ runs over this interval, $z=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ runs once around the circle of radius $e^{-2\pi y}<1$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(a), $P$ is holomorphic on the unit disc with Taylor coefficients $p(n)$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:analytic} gives $p(n)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int P(z)z^{-n-1}\dd z$ over that circle. With $\dd z=2\pi iz\dd x$,
\[
p(n)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)\,e^{-2\pi in\tau}\dd x.
\]
Let $N'=n-\frac1{24}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} the integrand is
\[
\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}-2\pi iN'\tau\Bigr)P(w),\qquad w=e^{-2\pi i/\tau}.
\]
The factor $e^{\pi i\tau/12}$ of the lemma has combined with $e^{-2\pi in\tau}$ and turned $n$ into $N'$. Now choose
\[
y=\frac1{\sqrt{24N'}},\qq a=\frac\pi{12y}=2\pi N'y=\pi\sqrt{\frac{N'}6},
\]
so that $2a=\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}$. This $y$ is the height that makes the two terms of the exponent equal at $x=0$: each equals $a$, and their sum is $2a$. We assume $n\ge17$. Then $\frac1y=\sqrt{24N'}\ge20.1$ and $a\ge5.2$, so $y\le\frac1{20}$ and $a\ge3$. The last two bounds are the ones the later estimates spend. Figure~\ref{fig:rw12-farey} draws this cut at $n=100$, where $y=0.0204$.

\emph{The exponent.} The job of this stage is to see a Gaussian. On the scale $x=yt$ the exponent is $2a$ minus $at^2$, plus a phase of size $a|t|^3$.

Write $x=yt$, so that $\tau=iy(1-it)$ and $\dd x=y\dd t$. Then
\[
\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}=\frac a{1-it}=\frac{a(1+it)}{1+t^2},\qquad-2\pi iN'\tau=a(1-it).
\]
Add them:
\[
a\,\frac{1+it+(1-it)(1+t^2)}{1+t^2}=a\,\frac{2+t^2-it^3}{1+t^2}=2a-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{iat^3}{1+t^2}.
\]
So the integrand has modulus $e^{2a}$ times a factor that decays like the Gaussian $e^{-at^2}$ near $t=0$. Also $-\frac{2\pi i}\tau=-\frac{2\pi(1+it)}{y(1+t^2)}$, so
\[
|w|=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{2\pi}{y(1+t^2)}\Bigr).
\]
The point $w$ is where the transformed series $P$ is evaluated. On the major arc it will be tiny.

\emph{The major arc.} The job of this stage is to evaluate the integral on $|x|\le y$, which is $|t|\le1$. Four estimates do the work. The first controls the square root, the second the phase, the third the value of $P(w)$, and the fourth the difference between the damped Gaussian and $e^{-at^2}$.

Since $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt y\,(1-it)^{1/2}$, the contribution of this arc is $y^{3/2}e^{2a}J$, where
\[
J=\int_{-1}^1(1-it)^{1/2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{iat^3}{1+t^2}\Bigr)P(w)\dd t.
\]
We compare $J$ with $\int_{\R}e^{-at^2}\dd t=\sqrt{\pi/a}$.

\emph{Square root.} Its job is to replace $(1-it)^{1/2}$ by $1$. For $|t|\le1$,
\[
\bigl|(1-it)^{1/2}-1\bigr|=\frac{|t|}{|(1-it)^{1/2}+1|}\le|t|,
\]
because the principal root has positive real part, so the denominator is at least $1$.

\emph{Phase.} Its job is to replace the oscillation by $1$. For real $\varphi$, $|e^{-i\varphi}-1|\le|\varphi|$. With $\varphi=\frac{at^3}{1+t^2}$, the phase differs from $1$ by at most $a|t|^3$.

\emph{The value $P(w)$.} Its job is to replace $P(w)$ by $1$. For $|t|\le1$ we have $1+t^2\le2$, so $|w|\le e^{-\pi/y}\le e^{-20\pi}$. And $p(m)\le2^{m-1}$ for $m\ge1$: listing the parts of a partition of $m$ in decreasing order gives a composition of $m$, different partitions give different compositions, and there are $2^{m-1}$ compositions. So
\[
|P(w)-1|\le\sum_{m\ge1}2^{m-1}|w|^m=\frac{|w|}{1-2|w|}\le2|w|\qquad\text{whenever }|w|\le\tfrac14,
\]
and in particular $|P(w)|\le2$.

Now write the integrand of $J$ as $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\bigl(1+\delta(t)\bigr)$, where $1+\delta=(1-it)^{1/2}e^{-i\varphi}P(w)$. Split
\[
\delta=\bigl((1-it)^{1/2}-1\bigr)e^{-i\varphi}P(w)+\bigl(e^{-i\varphi}-1\bigr)P(w)+\bigl(P(w)-1\bigr).
\]
The three facts give $|\delta|\le2|t|+2a|t|^3+2e^{-\pi/y}$. The coefficient $2$ on the first term is $|P(w)|\le2$. The coefficient $2$ on the second term is the same bound. The last term is at most $2|w|\le2e^{-\pi/y}$.

\emph{Gaussian comparison.} Its job is to replace $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}$ by $e^{-at^2}$. On $[-1,1]$, $\frac{t^2}{1+t^2}\ge\frac{t^2}2$, so $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\le e^{-at^2/2}$ there, and
\[
0\le e^{-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}}-e^{-at^2}=e^{-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\frac{at^4}{1+t^2}}\Bigr)\le at^4e^{-at^2/2}.
\]
The inequality $1-e^{-u}\le u$ produced the factor $at^4$.

So $J-\sqrt{\pi/a}$ is a sum of three pieces: the integral of $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\delta$ over $[-1,1]$, the integral of $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}-e^{-at^2}$ over $[-1,1]$, and minus the integral of $e^{-at^2}$ over $|t|>1$. We need four Gaussian integrals. Each follows from the substitution $u=\frac{at^2}2$ and the Gamma integral of Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma}:
\[
\int_{\R}|t|e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac2a,\quad\int_{\R}|t|^3e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac4{a^2},\quad\int_\R t^4e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{5/2}},\quad\int_\R e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}.
\]
Here is what each piece of $\delta$ contributes, after the comparison $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\le e^{-at^2/2}$. The term $2|t|$ integrates to at most $2\cdot\frac2a=\frac4a$. The term $2a|t|^3$ integrates to at most $2a\cdot\frac4{a^2}=\frac8a$. The term $2e^{-\pi/y}$ integrates to at most $2e^{-\pi/y}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}$. The Gaussian difference integrates to at most $a\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{5/2}}=\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{3/2}}$. And $\int_{|t|>1}e^{-at^2}\dd t\le\int_{|t|>1}|t|e^{-at^2}\dd t=\frac{e^{-a}}a$. Putting the pieces together,
\[
\Bigl|J-\sqrt{\frac\pi a}\Bigr|\le\frac4a+\frac8a+2e^{-\pi/y}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}+\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{3/2}}+\frac{e^{-a}}a\le\frac{20}a.
\]
For the last step use $a\ge3$. Then $\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{3/2}}\le\frac{4.4}a$, because $\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt a}\le\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sqrt3}<4.4$. The two exponentially small terms are each below $\frac1a$, because $\frac\pi y=12a$, so $e^{-\pi/y}=e^{-12a}$ and $e^{-a}$ are far smaller than $1$ once $a\ge3$. The coefficients add as $4+8+4.4+1+1=18.4\le20$.

So the major arc contributes $y^{3/2}e^{2a}\sqrt{\pi/a}\,\bigl(1+\theta\frac{20}{\sqrt{\pi a}}\bigr)$ for some complex $\theta$ with $|\theta|\le1$. As $\sqrt{\pi/a}=\sqrt{12y}$ and $y^2=\frac1{24N'}$, the main term is $\sqrt{12}\,y^2e^{2a}$. The arithmetic is $\frac{\sqrt{12}}{24}=\frac{\sqrt3}{12}=\frac1{4\sqrt3}$, so
\[
\sqrt{12}\,y^2e^{2a}=\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}.
\]

\emph{The minor arc.} The job of this stage is a lower bound on $\log P(r)-\log|P(z)|$ for $y\le|x|\le\frac12$. One term of the series for $L$ is enough, and it costs a factor $e^{-1/(80y)}$.

Let $r=e^{-2\pi y}$, so $z=re^{2\pi ix}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(d), $\log P(r)-\log|P(z)|=L(r)-\operatorname{Re}L(z)$, a sum over $m\ge1$ of the terms $\frac1m\bigl(\frac{r^m}{1-r^m}-\operatorname{Re}\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}\bigr)$. Each term is non-negative, as we saw in the proof of that lemma. Keep only $m=1$:
\[
\log P(r)-\log|P(z)|\ge\frac r{1-r}-\frac r{|1-z|}.
\]
The point $z$ lies on the circle of radius $r$, away from $1$, so $|1-z|$ is larger than $1-r$. To measure it,
\[
|1-z|^2=(1-r)^2+4r\sin^2\pi x\ge(1-r)^2+16rx^2,
\]
because $|\sin\pi x|\ge2|x|$ for $|x|\le\frac12$. And $|x|\ge y\ge\frac{1-r}{2\pi}$, since $1-e^{-u}\le u$ with $u=2\pi y$. So $|1-z|\ge(1-r)\bigl(1+\frac{4r}{\pi^2}\bigr)^{1/2}$.

Two bounds now move the same way. We have $r\ge e^{-\pi/10}>0.73$, and $1-r\le2\pi y$, so $\frac r{1-r}\ge\frac{0.73}{2\pi y}$. The factor $\bigl(1+\frac{4r}{\pi^2}\bigr)^{-1/2}$ is largest when $r$ is smallest, and at $r=0.73$ it is still below $0.879$. The gain keeps at least the fraction $1-0.879=0.121$ of $\frac r{1-r}$:
\[
\frac r{1-r}\,(1-0.879)\ge\frac{0.73\times0.121}{2\pi y}>\frac1{80y}.
\]
The constant $80$ is a convenient rounding of $2\pi/(0.73\times0.121)\approx71.1$.

Now the size of the integrand, before this gain is spent. By Lemma~\ref{lem:plog} with $t=2\pi y$, $\log P(r)\le\frac{\pi^2}{12\pi y}=a$. And $|e^{-2\pi in\tau}|=e^{2\pi ny}=e^{2\pi N'y+\pi y/12}\le e^{a+1}$, where the extra $e^{\pi y/12}$ is at most $e$ because $y\le\frac1{20}$ and $\pi/(20\cdot12)<1$. So on the minor arc the integrand has modulus at most $e^{2a+1-1/(80y)}$. The arc has length less than $1$, so it contributes at most
\[
e^{2a+1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}.
\]

\emph{Conclusion.} The job of this stage is to compare the three contributions, and then to replace $N'$ by $n$.

The main term is $\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}$. The major-arc error is at most $\frac{20}{\sqrt{\pi a}}$ times the main term. Since $a$ is of order $\sqrt n$, this relative error is $O(n^{-1/4})$. The minor arc is $e^{2a}$ times $e^{1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}$, which decreases faster than any power of $n$. So
\[
p(n)=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}\Bigl(1+O\bigl(n^{-1/4}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
Finally replace $N'$ by $n$. Since $\sqrt{N'}=\sqrt n-\frac1{48\sqrt n}+O(n^{-3/2})$, we get $e^{\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}}=e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1/2})\bigr)$, and $\frac1{N'}=\frac1n\bigl(1+O(\frac1n)\bigr)$. The passage from $N'$ to $n$ changes the relative error by $O(n^{-1/2})$. That is a finer error than the $O(n^{-1/4})$ already proved, so it is absorbed.
\end{proof}

At $n=100$ one can see how loose the proved error still is. There $a=12.82$ and $\frac{20}{\sqrt{\pi a}}=3.15$. A relative error of $3.15$ is larger than the main term, so the inequality has not yet become a sharp numerical bound. The true ratio in the table is $0.956$. The $O(n^{-1/4})$ is an asymptotic statement. It becomes small only for much larger $n$.

Where did the three constants in $\mathrm{HR}(n)$ come from? The exponent $\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$ is $2a$. Half of it is the growth $\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$ of $P$ near $1$, which is $\zeta(2)$ in disguise, and half is the factor $e^{2\pi ny}$ from Cauchy's formula. The saddle point is the height where the two halves are equal. The factor $4\sqrt3\,n$ in the denominator is the factor $\sqrt{-i\tau}$ from the transformation of $\eta$, times the width of the Gaussian.

\begin{think}
The proof gives a relative error $O(n^{-1/4})$. The true relative error is of order $n^{-1/2}$. Figure~\ref{fig:hrratio} shows it: $\sqrt n\bigl(1-\frac{p(n)}{\mathrm{HR}(n)}\bigr)$ settles near $0.443$. The factor $n^{-1/2}$ was lost in the bound $\bigl|(1-it)^{1/2}-1\bigr|\le|t|$. The first-order term of $(1-it)^{1/2}$ is $-\frac{it}2$, an odd function of $t$, and it integrates to zero against the even Gaussian. What survives is of size $\frac1a$. Since $a$ is of order $\sqrt n$, that relative error is of order $n^{-1/2}$. The bound $|t|$ cannot see the cancellation, and it leaves a term of size $\frac1{\sqrt a}$, which is $n^{-1/4}$. Where do the two pieces $\frac1C$ and $\frac C{48}$ of the constant $0.443$ come from?
\end{think}

\section{The other arcs, and an exact formula}

The minor-arc bound threw away a lot. Figure~\ref{fig:circlearcs} shows $\log|P(re^{2\pi ix})|$ at $r=0.99$ along the whole circle. Besides the huge peak at $x=0$ there are peaks at every fraction: at $\frac12$, about a quarter of the height of the main peak; at $\frac13$ and $\frac23$, about a ninth; at $\frac hk$, about $\frac1{k^2}$. Exercise~\ref{ex:minusone} proves this at $x=\frac12$. The reason, which we state and do not prove, is that $\eta$ has a transformation rule for every matrix of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\Z)$, and not only for $\tau\mapsto-\frac1\tau$. The matrix sending $\frac hk$ to $\infty$ shows that near $e^{2\pi ih/k}$, $P$ behaves like $\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi^2}{6k^2(1-r)}\bigr)$ times a root of unity.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{hr-circle}
\caption{$\log|P(re^{2\pi ix})|/\log P(r)$ for $r=0.99$ and $0\le x\le1$, where $\log P(0.99)=160.45$. The main peak at $x=0$ leaves the picture. Rust marks show heights $\frac1{k^2}$ above $x=\frac1k$. The measured peak heights are $0.237$ at $\frac12$, $0.097$ at $\frac13$, $0.048$ at $\frac14$ and $0.026$ at $\frac15$. They approach $\frac1{k^2}$ as $r\to1$, slowly, because of lower-order terms.}
\label{fig:circlearcs}
\end{figure}

Hardy and Ramanujan put a major arc around every $\frac hk$ with $k$ up to about $\sqrt n$, and obtained an asymptotic series whose $k$-th term is of size $e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}/k}$. In 1937 Derrick Henry Lehmer proved that their series, continued indefinitely, diverges. In the same year Hans Rademacher changed two things. He cut the circle at the Farey fractions, and he used the exact transformation of $\eta$ on each arc, where Hardy and Ramanujan had used an approximation. His series converges. The cut in Figure~\ref{fig:rw12-farey} is the first of those Farey arcs: one major arc at $0$, and the minor arcs on the rest of the interval. Rademacher keeps an arc at every fraction.

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher, 1937; quoted]\label{thm:rademacher}
For $n\ge1$,
\[
p(n)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt2}\sum_{k\ge1}A_k(n)\sqrt k\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dn}\Biggl(\frac{\sinh\bigl(\frac\pi k\sqrt{\frac23(n-\frac1{24})}\bigr)}{\sqrt{n-\frac1{24}}}\Biggr),
\]
where $A_k(n)=\sum_{0\le h<k,\,(h,k)=1}e^{\pi is(h,k)-2\pi inh/k}$ and $s(h,k)=\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\frac rk\bigl(\{\frac{hr}k\}-\frac12\bigr)$ is a Dedekind sum.
\end{theorem}

We quote this series and do not prove it. Nothing later in this book uses it. The proof is in the sources at the end of the chapter. The series is easy to try. For $n=200$, the term $k=1$ alone gives $p(200)$ with an error of $36\,202$, out of almost four trillion. Two terms leave an error of $81$, three terms $6.7$, five terms $0.14$, eight terms $0.024$. Since $p(200)$ is an integer, five terms already determine it. The quantity $n-\frac1{24}$ is the same shift $N'$ as in our proof. The sums $A_k(n)$ are close cousins of the Kloosterman sums. Volume~14 studies those sums, together with the circle method beyond this one leading term. That volume is a preview.

\begin{beauty}
A partition is about as elementary an object as mathematics has: a way of breaking a pile of pebbles into smaller piles. Its count is governed by the symmetry $\tau\mapsto-\frac1\tau$ of the upper half-plane, by $\pi$ and $\sqrt{2/3}$, and by the shift $\frac1{24}$, which is $-\frac12\zeta(-1)$ from Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta}. The closed form is the order in the count. The error term, and the peaks at the other fractions, are the part that behaves like a sum of many smaller contributions. Ramanujan, looking at MacMahon's table, saw a different structure as well: $p(5m+4)$, $p(7m+5)$ and $p(11m+6)$ are divisible by $5$, $7$ and $11$. He proved the first two in his paper of 1919. We do not prove them here. The table of $p(n)$ for $n\le5000$ confirms all three.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Three results carry the chapter. Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc} turned Euler's formal product into a holomorphic function on the disc. Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} found its symmetry, from the pentagonal theorem, Poisson summation and the identity theorem, with the square root the principal root of positive real part. Theorem~\ref{thm:hr} spent that symmetry at a saddle point and got the size of $p(n)$, with a proved relative error $O(n^{-1/4})$. The factor $n^{-1/2}$ in the true error was lost in the bound on $(1-it)^{1/2}$. Rademacher's formula was quoted, and it is not used again.

The volume has now used Poisson summation twice, for $\zeta$ and for $p(n)$. The last chapter changes the question from \emph{how big} to \emph{how fast}. Gauss's arithmetic--geometric mean is an iteration that doubles the number of correct digits of $\pi$ at every step.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:etacheck}
\emph{The symmetry, by hand.} Use the series~\eqref{eq:etatheta} to compute $\eta(2i)$ and $\eta(\frac i2)$ to six decimal places. How many terms does each sum need? Check that $\eta(\frac i2)=\sqrt2\,\eta(2i)$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} says.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:ratio}
Deduce from Theorem~\ref{thm:hr} that $\frac{p(n+1)}{p(n)}\to1$, and that $\log p(n)=\pi\sqrt{2n/3}-\log n+O(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:saddle}
\emph{A one-line upper bound.} Show that $p(n)\le e^{nt}P(e^{-t})$ for every $t>0$. With Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} and $t=\frac\pi{\sqrt{6n}}$, deduce that $p(n)\le2(24n)^{-1/4}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$ for every $n\ge1$. By what power of $n$ is this off the asymptotic of Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:minusone}
\emph{The arc at $\frac12$.} Show that $P(-q)=\frac{P(q^2)^3}{P(q)P(q^4)}$ for $|q|<1$. Deduce that $\log P(-r)\sim\frac{\pi^2}{24(1-r)}$ as $r\to1^-$, a quarter of $\log P(r)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:ones}
\emph{How many ones?} Show that the total number of parts equal to $1$, over all partitions of $n$, is $\sum_{k=1}^np(n-k)$. Deduce that the average number of ones in a partition of $n$ is asymptotic to $\frac{\sqrt{6n}}\pi$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:distinct}
\emph{Distinct parts.} Let $q(n)$ be the number of partitions of $n$ into distinct parts, with generating function $Q(z)=\prod_{m\ge1}(1+z^m)$. Prove that
\[
q(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{n/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}\,n^{3/4}}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:rw12a}
\emph{The saddle at $n=100$.} Compute $N'=n-\frac1{24}$, the height $y=\frac1{\sqrt{24N'}}$ and the common value $a=\pi\sqrt{N'/6}$ of the two halves of the exponent. Check that $\frac\pi{12y}$ and $2\pi N'y$ agree with $a$. Then compute the main term $\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}$ to the nearest integer, and compare it with $p(100)=190\,569\,292$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw12b}
\emph{Four logarithms.} The pentagonal recurrence gives $p(20)=627$ and $p(50)=204226$, and the table gives $p(10)$ and $p(100)$. Compute $\log p(n)$ and $\log\mathrm{HR}(n)$ to three decimals at $n=10$, $20$, $50$ and $100$, and list the four gaps $\log\mathrm{HR}(n)-\log p(n)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:rw12c}
\emph{The odd piece.} Show that $t\mapsto t\,e^{-at^2}$ is odd, so its integral over $[-1,1]$ vanishes. In the major-arc bound the linear piece of $(1-it)^{1/2}$ is charged at size $|t|$, and the comparison integral $\int_{\R}|t|e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac2a$ comes from the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}. The main term of $J$ is $\sqrt{\pi/a}$. At the saddle $a=12.8228$ of $n=100$, compute $\frac{2/a}{\sqrt{\pi/a}}$ and $\frac1a$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:rw12d}
\emph{When the minor-arc bound wins.} The minor arc contributes at most $e^{2a+1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}$, and the main term is $\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}$. Show that the bound is smaller than the main term once $\frac{\sqrt{24N'}}{80}>1+\log(4\sqrt3\,N')$. Check the two sides at $n=100$ and at $n=60000$. At $n=100$, also compute the ratio of the minor-arc bound to the main term.
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:etacheck}
\idea The series converges like a Gaussian, so a handful of terms is enough. The smaller $t$ is, the more terms matter, which is exactly why a symmetry exchanging $t$ and $\frac1t$ is useful.
\writeup For $\tau=it$ the term of index $j$ is $(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}$, and $(6j-1)^2=1,25,49,121$ for $j=0,1,-1,2$.
At $t=2$: $e^{-\pi/6}=0.5923849$, then $-e^{-25\pi/6}=-0.0000021$, and the next term is about $-7\cdot10^{-12}$. Two terms give $\eta(2i)=0.592383$.
At $t=\frac12$: $e^{-\pi/24}=0.8773058$, $-e^{-25\pi/24}=-0.0379118$, $-e^{-49\pi/24}=-0.0016383$, and $e^{-121\pi/24}=0.0000001$. Four terms give $\eta(\frac i2)=0.837756$.
Finally $\sqrt2\times0.5923828=0.837756$. The two sides agree to six places.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:ratio}
\idea The leading factor $e^{C\sqrt n}$ changes by a factor tending to $1$ when $n$ grows by $1$, because $\sqrt n$ is so flat.
\writeup Let $C=\pi\sqrt{2/3}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:hr},
\[
\frac{p(n+1)}{p(n)}=e^{C(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n)}\,\frac n{n+1}\,\frac{1+O(n^{-1/4})}{1+O(n^{-1/4})},
\]
and $0<\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\to0$, so every factor tends to $1$. Taking logarithms in the theorem, $\log p(n)=C\sqrt n-\log n-\log(4\sqrt3)+\log(1+O(n^{-1/4}))$, and the last two terms are bounded.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:saddle}
\idea One coefficient of a power series with positive coefficients is at most the whole sum; choosing the radius well is the saddle point method without the integral.
\writeup For $0<x<1$, $p(n)x^n\le\sum_mp(m)x^m=P(x)$, so $p(n)\le e^{nt}P(e^{-t})$ with $x=e^{-t}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans}, $P(e^{-t})\le\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}e^{\pi^2/(6t)}P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})$. With $t=\frac\pi{\sqrt{6n}}$, $\frac{\pi^2}{6t}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{6n}}6=\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}$ and $nt=\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}$; their sum is $2\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}3}$. Next, $\frac t{2\pi}=\frac1{2\sqrt{6n}}=(24n)^{-1/2}$. Finally $\frac{4\pi^2}t=4\pi\sqrt{6n}\ge30$, so $P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})\le1+2e^{-30}\le2$ by the bound $|P(w)-1|\le2|w|$ from the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}. Hence $p(n)\le2(24n)^{-1/4}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$. Compared with Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}, the bound is too large by a factor of order $n^{3/4}$: $n^{-1/4}$ against $n^{-1}$. The integral over the circle recovers the missing $n^{-3/4}$, which is the width $\sqrt{\pi/a}\asymp n^{-1/4}$ of the Gaussian times the circumference factor $y\asymp n^{-1/2}$. (At $n=1000$ the bound is $1129$ times $p(n)$, and $1000^{3/4}\approx178$.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:minusone}
\idea At $q\to-1$ the product splits into a part at $q^2\to1$, which is large, and a part that cancels most of it.
\writeup For $|q|<1$, $\frac1{P(-q)}=\prod_m(1-(-q)^m)=\prod_{m\text{ even}}(1-q^m)\prod_{m\text{ odd}}(1+q^m)$. The first product is $\frac1{P(q^2)}$. Since $1+q^m=\frac{1-q^{2m}}{1-q^m}$, $\prod_m(1+q^m)=\frac{P(q)}{P(q^2)}$, and the odd factors are this product divided by the even ones, $\prod_m(1+q^{2m})=\frac{P(q^2)}{P(q^4)}$. So $\prod_{m\text{ odd}}(1+q^m)=\frac{P(q)P(q^4)}{P(q^2)^2}$ and $\frac1{P(-q)}=\frac{P(q)P(q^4)}{P(q^2)^3}$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans}, $\log P(e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}+O(\log\frac1t)$ as $t\to0^+$. With $r=e^{-t}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\log P(-r)&=3\log P(e^{-2t})-\log P(e^{-t})-\log P(e^{-4t})\\&=\frac{\pi^2}t\Bigl(\frac14-\frac16-\frac1{24}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\log\frac1t\Bigr)\\&=\frac{\pi^2}{24t}+O\Bigl(\log\frac1t\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
As $t=-\log r\sim1-r$, $\log P(-r)\sim\frac{\pi^2}{24(1-r)}=\frac14\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{6(1-r)}$. This is the peak of height $\frac14$ at $x=\frac12$ in Figure~\ref{fig:circlearcs}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:ones}
\idea Counting pairs (partition, one of its ones) by the position of the one gives a sum of $p(n-k)$; the asymptotic formula turns that sum into a geometric series with ratio $e^{-C/(2\sqrt n)}$.
\writeup \emph{The identity.} Let $m_1(\lambda)$ be the number of ones in $\lambda$. Then $\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}m_1(\lambda)=\sum_{k\ge1}\#\{\lambda\vdash n:m_1(\lambda)\ge k\}$, and removing $k$ ones is a bijection from $\{\lambda\vdash n:m_1(\lambda)\ge k\}$ onto the partitions of $n-k$, with inverse ``add $k$ ones''. So the total is $\sum_{k=1}^np(n-k)$, and the average is $M_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{p(n-k)}{p(n)}$.
\emph{The asymptotics.} Let $C=\pi\sqrt{2/3}$ and $K=\lfloor n^{3/5}\rfloor$. For $k\le K$, $\sqrt{n-k}-\sqrt n=-\frac k{2\sqrt n}+O(\frac{k^2}{n^{3/2}})$ and $\frac{k^2}{n^{3/2}}\le n^{-3/10}$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}, applied to $n$ and $n-k\ge\frac n2$,
\[
\frac{p(n-k)}{p(n)}=e^{-\frac{Ck}{2\sqrt n}}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1/4})\bigr)
\]
uniformly in $k\le K$. With $\delta=\frac C{2\sqrt n}$, $\sum_{k=1}^Ke^{-k\delta}=\frac{e^{-\delta}(1-e^{-K\delta})}{1-e^{-\delta}}\sim\frac1\delta=\frac{2\sqrt n}C$, because $\delta\to0$ and $K\delta\asymp n^{1/10}\to\infty$. For $k>K$, $p(n-k)\le p(n-K)$, since $p$ is non-decreasing (adding a part $1$ is an injection), so these terms add up to at most $n\frac{p(n-K)}{p(n)}\le2ne^{-K\delta}\to0$. So $M_n\sim\frac{2\sqrt n}C=\frac{\sqrt{6n}}\pi$. Numerically, $M_{1000}=24.47$ against $\frac{\sqrt{6000}}\pi=24.66$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:distinct}
\idea The same circle method, with two differences: the transformed function has no factor $\sqrt{-i\tau}$, which changes the power of $n$, and the shift is $+\frac1{24}$ instead of $-\frac1{24}$.
\writeup \emph{Transformation.} Since $1+z^m=\frac{1-z^{2m}}{1-z^m}$, $Q(z)=\frac{P(z)}{P(z^2)}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} at $\tau$ and at $2\tau$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}Q(e^{2\pi i\tau})&=\frac{\sqrt{-i\tau}\,e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}P(w_1)}{\sqrt{-2i\tau}\,e^{\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}}e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}6}P(w_2)}\\&=\frac1{\sqrt2}e^{\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}}e^{-\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}\frac{P(w_1)}{P(w_2)},\end{aligned}
\]
with $w_1=e^{-2\pi i/\tau}$ and $w_2=e^{-\pi i/\tau}$.
\emph{Set-up.} As before $q(n)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}Q(e^{2\pi i\tau})e^{-2\pi in\tau}\dd x$. Let $N''=n+\frac1{24}$, $y=(48N'')^{-1/2}$ and $b=\frac\pi{24y}=2\pi N''y=\pi\sqrt{N''/12}$. With $x=yt$, $\tau=iy(1-it)$, exactly the computation of the theorem gives the exponent
\[
\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}-2\pi iN''\tau=2b-\frac{bt^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{ibt^3}{1+t^2}.
\]
Assume $n\ge9$, so that $y\le\frac1{20}$ and $b\ge1$.
\emph{Major arc.} For $|t|\le1$, $|w_1|\le e^{-\pi/y}$ and $|w_2|=\exp(-\frac\pi{y(1+t^2)})\le e^{-\pi/(2y)}$. Since $|P(w)-1|\le2|w|$, $|P(w_2)|\ge\frac12$ and $|\frac{P(w_1)}{P(w_2)}-1|\le2(|P(w_1)-1|+|P(w_2)-1|)\le8e^{-\pi/(2y)}$. As in the theorem, without the factor $(1-it)^{1/2}$, the major arc is $\frac y{\sqrt2}e^{2b}(\sqrt{\pi/b}+O(\frac1b))$. With $\sqrt{\pi/b}=\sqrt{24y}$, the main term is
\[
\frac y{\sqrt2}\sqrt{24y}\,e^{2b}=\sqrt{12}\,y^{3/2}e^{2b}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{8\cdot3^{3/4}N''^{3/4}}e^{2b}=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{N''/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}N''^{3/4}},
\]
using $48^{3/4}=8\cdot3^{3/4}$.
\emph{Minor arc.} Let $r=e^{-2\pi y}$, $z=re^{2\pi ix}$, $\theta=2\pi x$. Since $(1+r^m)^2-|1+z^m|^2=2r^m(1-\cos m\theta)$ and $-\log(1-u)\ge u$,
\[
\log(1+r^m)-\log|1+z^m|=-\frac12\log\Bigl(1-\frac{2r^m(1-\cos m\theta)}{(1+r^m)^2}\Bigr)\ge\frac{r^m(1-\cos m\theta)}4.
\]
Summing over $m$, $\log Q(r)-\log|Q(z)|\ge\frac14\bigl(\frac r{1-r}-\operatorname{Re}\frac z{1-z}\bigr)\ge\frac14\bigl(\frac r{1-r}-\frac r{|1-z|}\bigr)$, which for $y\le|x|\le\frac12$ and $y\le\frac1{20}$ is at least $\frac1{4\cdot80y}$ by the minor arc computation of the theorem. For $\log Q(r)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} at $t$ and $2t$ gives $Q(e^{-t})=\frac1{\sqrt2}e^{\pi^2/(12t)+t/24}\frac{P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})}{P(e^{-2\pi^2/t})}\le e^{\pi^2/(12t)+t/24}$, as $P$ is increasing on $[0,1[$. With $t=2\pi y$, $\frac{\pi^2}{12t}=b$, so $\log Q(r)\le b+1$. As $e^{2\pi ny}=e^{2\pi N''y-\pi y/12}\le e^b$, the minor arc contributes at most $e^{2b+1-1/(320y)}=e^{2b+1-\sqrt{48N''}/320}$, which is $o(y^{3/2}e^{2b})$.
\emph{Conclusion.} $q(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{N''/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}N''^{3/4}}$, and $\sqrt{N''}-\sqrt n\to0$ allows us to replace $N''$ by $n$. Numerically, $q(1000)=8\,635\,565\,795\,744\,155\,161\,506$, and the ratio to the asymptotic formula is $0.9946$.
\end{solution}


\begin{solution}{ex:rw12a}
\idea The height $y$ is the one choice that makes the two halves of the exponent equal. Once $y$ is known, $a$ and the main term are arithmetic.
\writeup For $n=100$, $N'=100-\frac1{24}=\frac{2399}{24}=99.958333$. Then
\[
y=\frac1{\sqrt{24N'}}=0.020417,\qquad a=\pi\sqrt{\frac{N'}6}=12.8228.
\]
The same value comes from $\frac\pi{12y}=12.8228$ and from $2\pi N'y=12.8228$. The main term is
\[
\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}=198\,301\,300
\]
to the nearest integer. Against $p(100)=190\,569\,292$ the ratio is $0.961$. The table's $\mathrm{HR}(100)=199\,280\,893$ uses $n$ in place of $N'$, and the ratio there is $0.956$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw12c}
\idea The linear piece of the square root is odd, and the Gaussian weight is even, so their product integrates to zero. The bound $|t|$ replaces that odd function by an even one and keeps a contribution the integral cancels.
\writeup The map $t\mapsto t\,e^{-at^2}$ satisfies $f(-t)=-f(t)$, and it is continuous, so $\int_{-1}^1 f=0$. From the proof, $\int_{\R}|t|e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac2a$ and the main term of $J$ is $\sqrt{\pi/a}$. At $a=12.8228$,
\[
\frac2a=0.1560,\qquad\sqrt{\frac\pi a}=0.4950,\qquad\frac{2/a}{\sqrt{\pi/a}}=0.315,\qquad\frac1a=0.078.
\]
The first ratio is the relative size the bound $|t|$ allows. After the odd piece cancels, the next terms are of size $\frac1a$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw12d}
\idea Compare the two contributions after cancelling $e^{2a}$. The inequality that remains is a numerical check at two values of $n$.
\writeup The minor-arc bound divided by the main term is
\[
e^{1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}\cdot 4\sqrt3\,N'.
\]
This is less than $1$ once $\frac{\sqrt{24N'}}{80}>1+\log(4\sqrt3\,N')$. At $n=100$, $N'=99.958333$, the two sides are $0.612$ and $7.540$. The ratio of the bound to the main term is $1021$. At $n=60000$ the two sides are $15.000$ and $13.938$, so the bound is the smaller one.
\end{solution}
