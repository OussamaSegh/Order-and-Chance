\chapter{The Hardy--Ramanujan Formula}\label{ch:hr}
\begin{center}\itshape
Four can be written as $4$, $3+1$, $2+2$, $2+1+1$ and $1+1+1+1$:\\
five ways. Two hundred can be written in nearly four trillion ways,\\
and a formula from 1918 gets every one of its thirteen digits right.
\end{center}

One symmetry, two formulas. In Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta}, Poisson summation gave the theta relation, and the theta relation gave the functional equation of $\zeta$. In this chapter the same summation is applied to a different series, Euler's pentagonal series from Chapter~\ref{ch:generating}. It gives a second relation of the same kind, and that relation tells us how many partitions a number has.

The question is easy to state. Let $p(n)$ be the number of partitions of $n$. We know $p(4)=5$. How big is $p(200)$? How big is $p(n)$ for large $n$?

Percy MacMahon, a former major of the Royal Artillery and the leading expert on partitions in England, sat down with Euler's pentagonal recurrence and computed $p(n)$ by hand for every $n\le200$. He did it for two colleagues at Trinity College, Cambridge, who wanted to test a formula. Godfrey Harold Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan had guessed that $p(n)$ grows like $e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$. Then they did something nobody had done before. They wrote $p(n)$ as an integral around a circle, looked at every point of the circle where the generating function blows up, and added the contributions. The result, published in 1918 with MacMahon's table at the end, was a series whose first few terms give $p(200)=3\,972\,999\,029\,388$ to within a fraction of a unit. Littlewood later wrote that ``a severe numerical test was now made, which elicited the astonishing facts about $p(100)$ and $p(200)$''.

The method is called the \emph{circle method}. Hardy and Littlewood turned it on sums of powers and on sums of primes, and Volume~14 follows them there. This chapter does the first and cleanest case completely. We prove the leading term:
\[
p(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,n}.
\]
Before any proof, look at how good it is. Write $\mathrm{HR}(n)$ for the right side.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
$n$ & $p(n)$ & $\mathrm{HR}(n)$ & $p(n)/\mathrm{HR}(n)$\\\hline
$10$ & $42$ & $48.1$ & $0.873$\\
$100$ & $190\,569\,292$ & $199\,280\,893$ & $0.956$\\
$200$ & $3\,972\,999\,029\,388$ & $4.100\times10^{12}$ & $0.969$\\
$1000$ & $2.406\times10^{31}$ & $2.440\times10^{31}$ & $0.986$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A formula built from $e$, $\pi$ and two square roots is counting piles of pebbles, and it is already within $5\%$ at $n=100$.

\begin{think}
The table says $p(100)$ is about $1.9\times10^8$. Before reading on, guess the shape of the answer. Is $p(n)$ closer to a power of $n$, to $2^n$, or to something in between? One clue: Exercise~\ref{ex:partitionbound} of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} showed $\ln p(n)\le\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$. So $p(n)$ grows faster than every power of $n$ and slower than every exponential $c^n$ with $c>1$.
\end{think}

Here is the plan. The generating function of partitions is $P(z)=\sum p(n)z^n$. Cauchy's formula writes $p(n)$ as an integral of $P(z)z^{-n-1}$ over a circle just inside the unit circle. The function $P$ is largest near $z=1$, so we need to know exactly how $P(z)$ behaves there. That is what the symmetry gives. Near $z=1$ lies the \emph{major arc}, which produces the main term by the Gaussian integral of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics}. The rest of the circle is the \emph{minor arc}, where $P$ is exponentially smaller.

\section{The partition product as a function}

Chapter~\ref{ch:generating} treated $\sum p(n)x^n=\prod_k\frac1{1-x^k}$ as an identity of formal series, where $x$ is not a number. To integrate around a circle we need $x$ to be a complex number $z$ with $|z|<1$. The next lemma checks that every formula we want survives.

Two notations. For $j\in\Z$ let $g_j=\frac{j(3j-1)}2$ be the $j$-th pentagonal number, so that $g_0,g_1,g_{-1},g_2,g_{-2},\dots$ are $0,1,2,5,7,\dots$ Each step of this list goes up, because $g_{-j}-g_j=j$ and $g_{j+1}-g_{-j}=2j+1$ for $j\ge1$. So the $g_j$ are distinct non-negative integers.

\begin{lemma}[the product on the disc]\label{lem:pdisc}
Let $|z|<1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item The series $P(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}p(n)z^n$ converges absolutely, and $P$ is holomorphic on the disc $D(0,1)$.
\item The series $E(z)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^jz^{g_j}$ converges absolutely, $E$ is holomorphic on $D(0,1)$, and $P(z)E(z)=1$. In particular $P(z)\ne0$.
\item $\prod_{k\le N}(1-z^k)\to E(z)$ as $N\to\infty$. So $P(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac1{1-z^k}$ and $E(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}(1-z^k)$.
\item The series $L(z)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}$ converges absolutely and $P(z)=e^{L(z)}$. Consequently $|P(z)|\le P(|z|)$, and $\log P(r)=L(r)$ for $0\le r<1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) \emph{Real $r$ first.} Let $0\le r<1$ and $N\ge1$. The finite product $\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}$ is a product of $N$ absolutely convergent geometric series. Multiplying them out, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:eulerproduct}, the coefficient of $r^n$ counts the partitions of $n$ into parts of size at most $N$. For $n\le N$ that is every partition of $n$. All terms are non-negative, so
\[
\sum_{n\le N}p(n)r^n\le\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}.
\]
Now bound the product. For $0\le u<1$, $-\log(1-u)=\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{u^i}i\le\sum_{i\ge1}u^i=\frac u{1-u}$. With $u=r^k$ and $1-r^k\ge1-r$,
\[
\prod_{k\le N}\frac1{1-r^k}\le\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{r^k}{1-r}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(\frac r{(1-r)^2}\Bigr).
\]
The bound does not depend on $N$. So the partial sums of $\sum p(n)r^n$ are bounded and the series converges. \emph{Complex $z$.} Since $|p(n)z^n|=p(n)|z|^n$, the series converges absolutely for $|z|<1$. Its radius of convergence is at least $1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:powerseries} says the sum is holomorphic on $D(0,1)$.

(b) The exponents $g_j$ are distinct non-negative integers, so $\sum_j|z|^{g_j}\le\sum_{n\ge0}|z|^n<\infty$. Listed by increasing exponent, $E$ is a power series with coefficients $0$ and $\pm1$ and radius at least $1$, so it is holomorphic. In $\Z[[x]]$, Proposition~\ref{prop:eulerproduct} gives $\bigl(\sum p(n)x^n\bigr)\prod_k(1-x^k)=1$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:pentagonal} says $\prod_k(1-x^k)=\sum_j(-1)^jx^{g_j}$. So the Cauchy product of the two coefficient sequences is $1,0,0,\dots$ Both series converge absolutely at $z$. The product of their sums is then the sum of their Cauchy product (the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:packets}), which is $1$.

(c) Let $E_N(x)=\prod_{k\le N}(1-x^k)$, a polynomial. In $\Z[[x]]$, $E_N(x)\sum p(n)x^n=\prod_{k>N}\frac1{1-x^k}$, and expanding as before, this is $\sum_nc_N(n)x^n$, where $c_N(n)$ counts the partitions of $n$ with every part larger than $N$. So $c_N(0)=1$, $c_N(n)=0$ for $1\le n\le N$, and $c_N(n)\le p(n)$ always. A polynomial and an absolutely convergent series multiply term by term, so
\[
\bigl|E_N(z)P(z)-1\bigr|=\Bigl|\sum_{n>N}c_N(n)z^n\Bigr|\le\sum_{n>N}p(n)|z|^n,
\]
the tail of a convergent series. It tends to $0$. Hence $E_N(z)\to\frac1{P(z)}=E(z)$ by (b).

(d) \emph{One factor.} For $|u|<1$, $\exp\bigl(\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{u^m}m\bigr)=\frac1{1-u}$. Indeed both sides are holomorphic on $D(0,1)$, and for real $u$ in $\left]-1,1\right[$ the sum is $-\log(1-u)$. Two holomorphic functions on the disc that agree on an interval are equal (Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}). \emph{All factors.} The double family $\frac{z^{km}}m$, for $k,m\ge1$, is summable:
\[
\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\sum_{k\ge1}|z|^{km}=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac{|z|^m}{1-|z|^m}\le\frac1{1-|z|}\sum_{m\ge1}|z|^m<\infty.
\]
Summing over $k$ first gives $L(z)$, which therefore converges absolutely. Summing over $m$ first, for $k\le N$ only, gives the exponent of $\frac1{E_N(z)}$, by the one-factor identity with $u=z^k$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:packets}(c) these partial sums over $k\le N$ tend to the full sum $L(z)$. As $\exp$ is continuous, $\frac1{E_N(z)}\to e^{L(z)}$, and by (c) the limit is $P(z)$.

For the consequences, $|P(z)|=e^{\operatorname{Re}L(z)}$, and $\bigl|\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}\bigr|\le\frac{|z|^m}{1-|z|^m}$ because $|1-z^m|\ge1-|z|^m$. So $\operatorname{Re}L(z)\le L(|z|)$ and $|P(z)|\le e^{L(|z|)}=P(|z|)$. For real $r$, $L(r)$ is real and $e^{L(r)}=P(r)$, so $L(r)=\log P(r)$.
\end{proof}

A quick check of (b) with the first terms: $(1+z+2z^2+3z^3+5z^4+\cdots)(1-z-z^2+\cdots)=1+0z+0z^2+0z^3+0z^4+\cdots$, since for instance the coefficient of $z^4$ is $5-3-2=0$.

Part (d) already gives the right order of magnitude of $P$ near $1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:plog}
For every $t>0$, $\log P(e^{-t})\le\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(d) with $r=e^{-t}$, $\log P(e^{-t})=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1m\,\frac1{e^{mt}-1}$. Since $e^u-1\ge u$, each term is at most $\frac1{m^2t}$. So $\log P(e^{-t})\le\frac1t\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{m^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:zeta2k}.
\end{proof}

So $P(e^{-t})$ is at most $e^{\pi^2/(6t)}$: as $t\to0$ it explodes, but in a controlled way. An upper bound is not enough for an asymptotic formula. We need an equality, and it comes from a symmetry.

\section{Dedekind's eta function}

Write $\mathbb H=\{\tau\in\C:\operatorname{Im}\tau>0\}$ for the upper half-plane. If $\tau\in\mathbb H$ then $z=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ has $|z|=e^{-2\pi\operatorname{Im}\tau}<1$, so $E(z)$ makes sense.

\begin{definition}
\emph{Dedekind's eta function} is, for $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
\eta(\tau)=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\prod_{m\ge1}\bigl(1-e^{2\pi im\tau}\bigr)=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\,E\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

It is holomorphic on $\mathbb H$, as a product of compositions of holomorphic functions, and it never vanishes, by Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(b). The partition function is hidden in it:
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}}{\eta(\tau)}.
\]

Why the odd factor $e^{\pi i\tau/12}$? Because of what it does to the pentagonal exponents. Complete the square:
\[
g_j+\frac1{24}=\frac{36j^2-12j+1}{24}=\frac{(6j-1)^2}{24}.
\]
Multiply the series $E(e^{2\pi i\tau})=\sum_j(-1)^je^{2\pi i\tau g_j}$ by $e^{\pi i\tau/12}=e^{2\pi i\tau/24}$. Each exponent $g_j$ becomes $g_j+\frac1{24}$, and we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq:etatheta}
\eta(\tau)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^j\,e^{\pi i\tau(6j-1)^2/12}.
\end{equation}
The exponents are now perfect squares, as in the theta series $\sum_ne^{-\pi n^2t}$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:theta}. Euler's pentagonal theorem, read this way, says that $\eta$ is a theta series. And theta series have a symmetry, which Poisson summation finds.

\begin{think}
The theta relation of Corollary~\ref{cor:theta} exchanges $t$ and $\frac1t$ and costs a factor $\sqrt t$. On the imaginary axis $\tau=it$, formula~\eqref{eq:etatheta} reads $\eta(it)=\sum_j(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}$. What relation between $\eta(it)$ and $\eta(\frac it)$ would you bet on?
\end{think}

For $\tau\in\mathbb H$, the number $-i\tau$ has real part $\operatorname{Im}\tau>0$, so it lies in the slit plane of Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}. We write $\sqrt{-i\tau}=e^{\frac12\operatorname{Log}(-i\tau)}$ for its principal square root, the one with positive real part. It is a holomorphic function of $\tau$ on $\mathbb H$, and for $\tau=it$ with $t>0$ it is the ordinary $\sqrt t$. Also, if $\tau\in\mathbb H$ then $-\frac1\tau\in\mathbb H$, because $\operatorname{Im}\bigl(-\frac1\tau\bigr)=\frac{\operatorname{Im}\tau}{|\tau|^2}$.

\begin{theorem}[the transformation of $\eta$]\label{thm:eta}
For every $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
\eta\Bigl(-\frac1\tau\Bigr)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\;\eta(\tau).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Stage 1: the imaginary axis, by Poisson summation.} Fix $t>0$ and let
\[
f(x)=e^{\pi ix}\,e^{-3\pi t\,(x-\frac16)^2}\qquad(x\in\R).
\]
At an integer $j$, $e^{\pi ij}=(-1)^j$ and $3(j-\frac16)^2=\frac{(6j-1)^2}{12}$. So by~\eqref{eq:etatheta},
\[
\sum_{j\in\Z}f(j)=\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}=\eta(it).
\]
We compute $\hat f$ in three moves. First, by Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussianft} with $a=(3t)^{-1/2}$, the Gaussian $e^{-3\pi tx^2}$ has transform $(3t)^{-1/2}e^{-\pi\xi^2/(3t)}$. Second, shifting $x$ by $\frac16$ multiplies the transform by $e^{-2\pi i\xi/6}=e^{-\pi i\xi/3}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbasic}(a)). Third, multiplying by $e^{\pi ix}=e^{2\pi i\frac12x}$ replaces $\xi$ by $\xi-\frac12$ (the same proposition). So
\[
\hat f(\xi)=\frac1{\sqrt{3t}}\,e^{-\pi(\xi-\frac12)^2/(3t)}\,e^{-\pi i(\xi-\frac12)/3}.
\]
Both $f$ and $\hat f$ are Gaussians in modulus, so they are bounded by a constant times $(1+|x|)^{-2}$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} applies: $\sum_jf(j)=\sum_m\hat f(m)$. At an integer $m$ put $n=2m-1$, which runs over the odd integers. Then $(m-\frac12)^2=\frac{n^2}4$ and
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac1{\sqrt{3t}}\sum_{n\text{ odd}}e^{-\pi n^2/(12t)}\,e^{-\pi in/6}.
\]
Group $n$ with $-n$. Since $e^{-\pi in/6}+e^{\pi in/6}=2\cos\frac{\pi n}6$,
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac2{\sqrt{3t}}\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\n\text{ odd}}}\cos\frac{\pi n}6\;e^{-\pi n^2/(12t)}.
\]
Now look at $\cos\frac{\pi n}6$ for odd $n\ge1$. It depends on $n$ modulo $12$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccccc}
$n\bmod12$ & $1$ & $3$ & $5$ & $7$ & $9$ & $11$\\\hline
$\cos\frac{\pi n}6$ & $\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $0$ & $-\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $-\frac{\sqrt3}2$ & $0$ & $\frac{\sqrt3}2$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The multiples of $3$ drop out. What remains are the $n\ge1$ prime to $6$, and each of them is $|6j-1|$ for exactly one $j\in\Z$: the numbers $5,11,17,\dots$ are $6j-1$ with $j\ge1$, and $1,7,13,\dots$ are $1-6j$ with $j\le0$. Check the sign on the table. For $j=0,1,-1,2,-2$ we get $n=1,5,7,11,13$, with cosines $+,-,-,+,+$ times $\frac{\sqrt3}2$. In general $|6j-1|$ is congruent modulo $12$ to $1$ or $11$ when $j$ is even and to $5$ or $7$ when $j$ is odd, so
\[
\cos\frac{\pi|6j-1|}6=(-1)^j\,\frac{\sqrt3}2.
\]
Therefore
\[
\sum_{m\in\Z}\hat f(m)=\frac2{\sqrt{3t}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2\sum_{j\in\Z}(-1)^je^{-\pi(6j-1)^2/(12t)}=\frac1{\sqrt t}\,\eta\Bigl(\frac it\Bigr),
\]
again by~\eqref{eq:etatheta}, now at $\tau=\frac it$. Poisson summation has given
\[
\eta\Bigl(\frac it\Bigr)=\sqrt t\;\eta(it)\qquad(t>0).
\]
This is the theorem for $\tau=it$, since then $-\frac1\tau=\frac it$ and $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt t$.

\emph{Stage 2: the half-plane, by the identity theorem.} The two functions $\tau\mapsto\eta(-\frac1\tau)$ and $\tau\mapsto\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\eta(\tau)$ are holomorphic on $\mathbb H$, which is open and connected. By Stage~1 they agree on the half-line $\{it:t>0\}$, a set with accumulation points in $\mathbb H$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:identity}(b) they agree on all of $\mathbb H$.
\end{proof}

Check a point where nothing is hidden. At $\tau=i$ we have $-\frac1\tau=i$ and $\sqrt{-i\tau}=1$, so the theorem says $\eta(i)=\eta(i)$. Now a point off the axis: at $\tau=0.3+0.7i$, summing forty terms of~\eqref{eq:etatheta} on each side gives two complex numbers that agree to more than thirty decimal places. The square root matters here, since $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt{0.7-0.3i}$ is not real.

The proof used exactly three earlier results: the pentagonal theorem, to see a theta series; Poisson summation, for the symmetry on one line; and the identity theorem, to spread it over the half-plane. Compare with Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta}, where Poisson summation for $e^{-\pi tx^2}$ gave the functional equation of $\zeta$. Same tool, different Gaussian.

Now translate back to partitions.

\begin{lemma}[the transformation of $P$]\label{lem:ptrans}
For $\tau\in\mathbb H$,
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\;e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}\,e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}\,P\bigl(e^{-2\pi i/\tau}\bigr).
\]
In particular, for real $t>0$,
\[
P(e^{-t})=\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}\;\exp\Bigl(\frac{\pi^2}{6t}-\frac t{24}\Bigr)\,P\bigl(e^{-4\pi^2/t}\bigr).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $P(e^{2\pi i\sigma})=e^{\pi i\sigma/12}\,\eta(\sigma)^{-1}$ twice, at $\sigma=\tau$ and at $\sigma=-\frac1\tau$. At $\sigma=-\frac1\tau$ it reads $\eta(-\frac1\tau)^{-1}=e^{\pi i/(12\tau)}P(e^{-2\pi i/\tau})$. With Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} in the middle step,
\[
P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}}{\eta(\tau)}=\frac{e^{\pi i\tau/12}\sqrt{-i\tau}}{\eta(-1/\tau)}=e^{\pi i\tau/12}\sqrt{-i\tau}\;e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}P\bigl(e^{-2\pi i/\tau}\bigr).
\]
For the second form take $\tau=\frac{it}{2\pi}$. Then $e^{2\pi i\tau}=e^{-t}$, $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}$, $e^{\pi i\tau/12}=e^{-t/24}$, $\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$ and $e^{-2\pi i/\tau}=e^{-4\pi^2/t}$.
\end{proof}

Read the second form slowly. When $t$ is small, $e^{-4\pi^2/t}$ is tiny, and $P$ of a tiny number is $1$ to many decimals. So
\[
\log P(e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}+\frac12\log\frac t{2\pi}-\frac t{24}+O\bigl(e^{-4\pi^2/t}\bigr).
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:plog} was right to leading order, and now we have every term. Try $t=0.01$. The product $P(e^{-0.01})$ is about $1.0947\times10^{70}$, and adding up its logarithm directly takes thousands of terms. The lemma computes the same number from the value of $P$ at $e^{-3948}$, which is $1$.

The symmetry $\tau\mapsto-\frac1\tau$ swaps the hard point $z=1$ of the unit disc with the easy point $z=0$. Everything that follows is bookkeeping around that swap.

\section{The theorem of Hardy and Ramanujan}

\begin{theorem}[Hardy and Ramanujan, 1918]\label{thm:hr}
As $n\to\infty$,
\[
p(n)=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,n}\Bigl(1+O\bigl(n^{-1/4}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof has five stages: write $p(n)$ as an integral over a circle, simplify the exponent, treat the major arc, bound the minor arc, and collect. One piece of advice before starting. The radius of the circle is ours to choose, and the whole proof depends on choosing it at the saddle point, as in Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics}.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Stage 1: the integral.} Fix $y>0$, to be chosen, and put $\tau=x+iy$ with $-\frac12\le x\le\frac12$. As $x$ runs over this interval, $z=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ runs once around the circle of radius $e^{-2\pi y}<1$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(a), $P$ is holomorphic on the unit disc with Taylor coefficients $p(n)$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:analytic} gives $p(n)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int P(z)z^{-n-1}\dd z$ over that circle. With $\dd z=2\pi iz\dd x$,
\[
p(n)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}P\bigl(e^{2\pi i\tau}\bigr)\,e^{-2\pi in\tau}\dd x.
\]
Let $N'=n-\frac1{24}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} the integrand is
\[
\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\exp\Bigl(\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}-2\pi iN'\tau\Bigr)P(w),\qquad w=e^{-2\pi i/\tau}.
\]
The factor $e^{\pi i\tau/12}$ of the lemma has combined with $e^{-2\pi in\tau}$ and turned $n$ into $N'$. Now choose
\[
y=\frac1{\sqrt{24N'}},\qq a=\frac\pi{12y}=2\pi N'y=\pi\sqrt{\frac{N'}6},
\]
so that $2a=\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}$. This $y$ is the one that makes the two terms of the exponent equal at $x=0$. We assume $n\ge17$. Then $\frac1y=\sqrt{24N'}\ge20.1$ and $a\ge5.2$, so $y\le\frac1{20}$ and $a\ge3$.

\emph{Stage 2: the exponent.} Write $x=yt$, so that $\tau=iy(1-it)$ and $\dd x=y\dd t$. Then
\[
\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}=\frac a{1-it}=\frac{a(1+it)}{1+t^2},\qquad-2\pi iN'\tau=a(1-it).
\]
Add them:
\[
a\,\frac{1+it+(1-it)(1+t^2)}{1+t^2}=a\,\frac{2+t^2-it^3}{1+t^2}=2a-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{iat^3}{1+t^2}.
\]
So the integrand has modulus $e^{2a}$ times a factor that decays like the Gaussian $e^{-at^2}$ near $t=0$. Also $-\frac{2\pi i}\tau=-\frac{2\pi(1+it)}{y(1+t^2)}$, so
\[
|w|=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{2\pi}{y(1+t^2)}\Bigr).
\]

\emph{Stage 3: the major arc, $|x|\le y$.} Here $|t|\le1$. Since $\sqrt{-i\tau}=\sqrt y\,(1-it)^{1/2}$, the contribution of this arc is $y^{3/2}e^{2a}J$, where
\[
J=\int_{-1}^1(1-it)^{1/2}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{iat^3}{1+t^2}\Bigr)P(w)\dd t.
\]
We compare $J$ with $\int_{\R}e^{-at^2}\dd t=\sqrt{\pi/a}$. Three facts are needed for $|t|\le1$.

First, $|(1-it)^{1/2}-1|=\frac{|t|}{|(1-it)^{1/2}+1|}\le|t|$, because the principal root has positive real part.

Second, $|e^{-i\varphi}-1|\le|\varphi|$ for real $\varphi$. With $\varphi=\frac{at^3}{1+t^2}$, the phase differs from $1$ by at most $a|t|^3$.

Third, $|w|\le e^{-\pi/y}\le e^{-20\pi}$, since $1+t^2\le2$. And $p(m)\le2^{m-1}$ for $m\ge1$: listing the parts of a partition of $m$ in decreasing order gives a composition of $m$, different partitions give different compositions, and there are $2^{m-1}$ compositions. So
\[
|P(w)-1|\le\sum_{m\ge1}2^{m-1}|w|^m=\frac{|w|}{1-2|w|}\le2|w|\qquad\text{whenever }|w|\le\tfrac14,
\]
and in particular $|P(w)|\le2$.

Now write the integrand of $J$ as $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\bigl(1+\delta(t)\bigr)$, where $1+\delta=(1-it)^{1/2}e^{-i\varphi}P(w)$. Split
\[
\delta=\bigl((1-it)^{1/2}-1\bigr)e^{-i\varphi}P(w)+\bigl(e^{-i\varphi}-1\bigr)P(w)+\bigl(P(w)-1\bigr).
\]
By the three facts, $|\delta|\le2|t|+2a|t|^3+2e^{-\pi/y}$. Next, $\frac{t^2}{1+t^2}\ge\frac{t^2}2$ on $[-1,1]$, so $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\le e^{-at^2/2}$ there, and
\[
0\le e^{-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}}-e^{-at^2}=e^{-\frac{at^2}{1+t^2}}\Bigl(1-e^{-\frac{at^4}{1+t^2}}\Bigr)\le at^4e^{-at^2/2}.
\]
So $J-\sqrt{\pi/a}$ is a sum of three pieces: the integral of $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}\delta$ over $[-1,1]$, the integral of $e^{-at^2/(1+t^2)}-e^{-at^2}$ over $[-1,1]$, and minus the integral of $e^{-at^2}$ over $|t|>1$. We need four Gaussian integrals. Each follows from the substitution $u=\frac{at^2}2$ and the Gamma integral of Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma}:
\[
\int_{\R}|t|e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac2a,\quad\int_{\R}|t|^3e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac4{a^2},\quad\int_\R t^4e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{5/2}},\quad\int_\R e^{-at^2/2}\dd t=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}.
\]
And $\int_{|t|>1}e^{-at^2}\dd t\le\int_{|t|>1}|t|e^{-at^2}\dd t=\frac{e^{-a}}a$. Putting the pieces together,
\[
\Bigl|J-\sqrt{\frac\pi a}\Bigr|\le\frac4a+\frac8a+2e^{-\pi/y}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}a}+\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{3/2}}+\frac{e^{-a}}a\le\frac{20}a.
\]
For the last step use $a\ge3$: then $\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{a^{3/2}}\le\frac{4.4}a$, and the two exponentially small terms are below $\frac1a$, because $\frac\pi y=12a$.

So the major arc contributes $y^{3/2}e^{2a}\sqrt{\pi/a}\,\bigl(1+\theta\frac{20}{\sqrt{\pi a}}\bigr)$ for some complex $\theta$ with $|\theta|\le1$. As $\sqrt{\pi/a}=\sqrt{12y}$ and $y^2=\frac1{24N'}$, the main term is
\[
\sqrt{12}\,y^2e^{2a}=\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}.
\]

\emph{Stage 4: the minor arc, $y\le|x|\le\frac12$.} This is the whole rest of the circle. Let $r=e^{-2\pi y}$, so $z=re^{2\pi ix}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc}(d), $\log P(r)-\log|P(z)|=L(r)-\operatorname{Re}L(z)$, a sum over $m\ge1$ of the terms $\frac1m\bigl(\frac{r^m}{1-r^m}-\operatorname{Re}\frac{z^m}{1-z^m}\bigr)$. Each term is non-negative, as we saw in the proof of that lemma. Keep only $m=1$:
\[
\log P(r)-\log|P(z)|\ge\frac r{1-r}-\frac r{|1-z|}.
\]
The point $z$ is on the circle of radius $r$ but not near $1$, so $|1-z|$ is noticeably larger than $1-r$. To measure it,
\[
|1-z|^2=(1-r)^2+4r\sin^2\pi x\ge(1-r)^2+16rx^2,
\]
as $|\sin\pi x|\ge2|x|$ for $|x|\le\frac12$. And $|x|\ge y\ge\frac{1-r}{2\pi}$, since $1-e^{-u}\le u$. So $|1-z|\ge(1-r)\bigl(1+\frac{4r}{\pi^2}\bigr)^{1/2}$. For $y\le\frac1{20}$ we have $r\ge e^{-\pi/10}>0.73$, hence $\bigl(1+\frac{4r}{\pi^2}\bigr)^{-1/2}<0.879$ and $\frac r{1-r}\ge\frac{0.73}{2\pi y}$. The gain is at least
\[
\frac r{1-r}\,(1-0.879)\ge\frac{0.73\times0.121}{2\pi y}>\frac1{80y}.
\]
Now the size of the integrand. By Lemma~\ref{lem:plog} with $t=2\pi y$, $\log P(r)\le\frac{\pi^2}{12\pi y}=a$. And $|e^{-2\pi in\tau}|=e^{2\pi ny}=e^{2\pi N'y+\pi y/12}\le e^{a+1}$. So on the minor arc the integrand has modulus at most $e^{2a+1-1/(80y)}$. The arc has length less than $1$, so it contributes at most
\[
e^{2a+1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}.
\]

\emph{Stage 5: conclusion.} The main term is $\frac{e^{2a}}{4\sqrt3N'}$. The major arc error is at most $\frac{20}{\sqrt{\pi a}}$ times it, and $a$ is of order $\sqrt n$, so this is $O(n^{-1/4})$ times the main term. The minor arc is $e^{2a}$ times $e^{1-\sqrt{24N'}/80}$, which decreases faster than any power of $n$. So
\[
p(n)=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}}}{4\sqrt3\,N'}\Bigl(1+O\bigl(n^{-1/4}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
Finally replace $N'$ by $n$. Since $\sqrt{N'}=\sqrt n-\frac1{48\sqrt n}+O(n^{-3/2})$, we get $e^{\pi\sqrt{2N'/3}}=e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1/2})\bigr)$, and $\frac1{N'}=\frac1n\bigl(1+O(\frac1n)\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

Where did the three constants come from? The exponent $\pi\sqrt{2n/3}$ is $2a$: half of it is the growth $\frac{\pi^2}{6t}$ of $P$ near $1$, which is $\zeta(2)$ in disguise, and half is the factor $e^{2\pi ny}$ of Cauchy's formula. The saddle point is where the two halves are equal. The $4\sqrt3\,n$ in the denominator is the factor $\sqrt{-i\tau}$ of the $\eta$ transformation, times the width of a Gaussian.

Where did the proof lose a factor $n^{1/4}$? In the bound $|(1-it)^{1/2}-1|\le|t|$. The first-order term of $(1-it)^{1/2}$ is $-\frac{it}2$, an odd function of $t$, and it integrates to zero against the even Gaussian. Only the second-order terms survive, and they are of size $\frac1a$ relative to the main term, not $\frac1{\sqrt a}$. \section{The other arcs, and an exact formula}

The minor arc bound threw away a lot. Figure~\ref{fig:circlearcs} shows $\log|P(re^{2\pi ix})|$ at $r=0.99$ along the whole circle. Besides the huge peak at $x=0$ there are peaks at every fraction: at $\frac12$, about a quarter of the height of the main peak; at $\frac13$ and $\frac23$, about a ninth; at $\frac hk$, about $\frac1{k^2}$. Exercise~\ref{ex:minusone} proves this at $x=\frac12$. The reason, which we state without proof, is that $\eta$ has a transformation rule for every matrix of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\Z)$ and not only for $\tau\mapsto-\frac1\tau$. The matrix sending $\frac hk$ to $\infty$ shows that near $e^{2\pi ih/k}$, $P$ behaves like $\exp\bigl(\frac{\pi^2}{6k^2(1-r)}\bigr)$ times a root of unity.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{hr-circle}
\caption{$\log|P(re^{2\pi ix})|/\log P(r)$ for $r=0.99$ and $0\le x\le1$, where $\log P(0.99)=160.45$. The main peak at $x=0$ leaves the picture. Rust marks show heights $\frac1{k^2}$ above $x=\frac1k$. The measured peak heights are $0.237$ at $\frac12$, $0.097$ at $\frac13$, $0.048$ at $\frac14$ and $0.026$ at $\frac15$. They approach $\frac1{k^2}$ as $r\to1$, slowly, because of lower-order terms.}
\label{fig:circlearcs}
\end{figure}

Hardy and Ramanujan put a major arc around every $\frac hk$ with $k$ up to about $\sqrt n$, and obtained an asymptotic series whose $k$-th term is of size $e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}/k}$. In 1937 Derrick Henry Lehmer proved that their series, continued indefinitely, diverges. In the same year Hans Rademacher changed two things. He cut the circle at Farey fractions, and he used the exact transformation of $\eta$ on each arc where Hardy and Ramanujan had used an approximation. His series converges.

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher, 1937; quoted]\label{thm:rademacher}
For $n\ge1$,
\[
p(n)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt2}\sum_{k\ge1}A_k(n)\sqrt k\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dn}\Biggl(\frac{\sinh\bigl(\frac\pi k\sqrt{\frac23(n-\frac1{24})}\bigr)}{\sqrt{n-\frac1{24}}}\Biggr),
\]
where $A_k(n)=\sum_{0\le h<k,\,(h,k)=1}e^{\pi is(h,k)-2\pi inh/k}$ and $s(h,k)=\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\frac rk\bigl(\{\frac{hr}k\}-\frac12\bigr)$ is a Dedekind sum.
\end{theorem}

We do not prove this theorem and nothing in this book depends on it. Its proof is in the sources at the end of the chapter. But it is easy to try. For $n=200$, the term $k=1$ alone gives $p(200)$ with an error of $36\,202$, out of almost four trillion. Two terms leave an error of $81$, three terms $6.7$, five terms $0.14$, eight terms $0.024$. Since $p(200)$ is an integer, five terms already determine it. The quantity $n-\frac1{24}$ is the same shift $N'$ as in our proof. The sums $A_k(n)$ are close cousins of the Kloosterman sums that Volume~14 studies.

\begin{beauty}
A partition is about as elementary an object as mathematics has: a way of breaking a pile of pebbles into smaller piles. Its count is governed by the symmetry $\tau\mapsto-\frac1\tau$ of the upper half-plane, by $\pi$ and $\sqrt{2/3}$, and by the strange $\frac1{24}$, which is $-\frac12\zeta(-1)$ from Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta}. Ramanujan, looking at MacMahon's table, saw a different structure as well: $p(5m+4)$, $p(7m+5)$ and $p(11m+6)$ are divisible by $5$, $7$ and $11$. He proved the first two in his paper of 1919. We do not prove them here, but the table of $p(n)$ for $n\le5000$ confirms all three.
\end{beauty}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Three results carry the chapter. Lemma~\ref{lem:pdisc} turned Euler's formal product into a holomorphic function on the disc. Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} found its symmetry, from the pentagonal theorem, Poisson summation and the identity theorem. Theorem~\ref{thm:hr} spent that symmetry at a saddle point and got the size of $p(n)$. Rademacher's formula was quoted, not proved.

The volume has now used Poisson summation twice, for $\zeta$ and for $p(n)$. Volume~6 will use the transformation of $\eta$ again, when it meets modular forms. The last chapter of this volume changes the question from \emph{how big} to \emph{how fast}: Gauss's arithmetic--geometric mean is an iteration that roughly doubles the number of correct digits of $\pi$ at every step.

\section{Exercises}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:etacheck}
\emph{The symmetry, by hand.} Use the series~\eqref{eq:etatheta} to compute $\eta(2i)$ and $\eta(\frac i2)$ to six decimal places. How many terms does each need? Check that $\eta(\frac i2)=\sqrt2\,\eta(2i)$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:eta} says.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:ratio}
Deduce from Theorem~\ref{thm:hr} that $\frac{p(n+1)}{p(n)}\to1$, and that $\log p(n)=\pi\sqrt{2n/3}-\log n+O(1)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{ex:saddle}
\emph{A one-line upper bound.} Show that $p(n)\le e^{nt}P(e^{-t})$ for every $t>0$. With Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} and $t=\frac\pi{\sqrt{6n}}$, deduce that $p(n)\le2(24n)^{-1/4}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$ for every $n\ge1$. By what power of $n$ is this off?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:minusone}
\emph{The arc at $\frac12$.} Show that $P(-q)=\frac{P(q^2)^3}{P(q)P(q^4)}$ for $|q|<1$. Deduce that $\log P(-r)\sim\frac{\pi^2}{24(1-r)}$ as $r\to1^-$, a quarter of $\log P(r)$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{ex:ones}
\emph{How many ones?} Show that the total number of parts equal to $1$, over all partitions of $n$, is $\sum_{k=1}^np(n-k)$. Deduce that the average number of ones in a partition of $n$ is asymptotic to $\frac{\sqrt{6n}}\pi$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{ex:distinct}
\emph{Distinct parts.} Let $q(n)$ be the number of partitions of $n$ into distinct parts, with generating function $Q(z)=\prod_{m\ge1}(1+z^m)$. Prove that
\[
q(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{n/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}\,n^{3/4}}.
\]
\end{exercise}

\section{Solutions}

\begin{solution}{ex:etacheck}
\idea The series converges like a Gaussian, so a handful of terms is enough. The smaller $t$ is, the more terms matter, which is exactly why a symmetry exchanging $t$ and $\frac1t$ is useful.
\writeup For $\tau=it$ the term of index $j$ is $(-1)^je^{-\pi t(6j-1)^2/12}$, and $(6j-1)^2=1,25,49,121$ for $j=0,1,-1,2$.
At $t=2$: $e^{-\pi/6}=0.5923849$, then $-e^{-25\pi/6}=-0.0000021$, and the next term is about $-7\cdot10^{-12}$. Two terms give $\eta(2i)=0.592383$.
At $t=\frac12$: $e^{-\pi/24}=0.8773058$, $-e^{-25\pi/24}=-0.0379118$, $-e^{-49\pi/24}=-0.0016383$, and $e^{-121\pi/24}=0.0000001$. Four terms give $\eta(\frac i2)=0.837756$.
Finally $\sqrt2\times0.5923828=0.837756$. The two sides agree to six places.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:ratio}
\idea The leading factor $e^{C\sqrt n}$ changes by a factor tending to $1$ when $n$ grows by $1$, because $\sqrt n$ is so flat.
\writeup Let $C=\pi\sqrt{2/3}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:hr},
\[
\frac{p(n+1)}{p(n)}=e^{C(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n)}\,\frac n{n+1}\,\frac{1+O(n^{-1/4})}{1+O(n^{-1/4})},
\]
and $0<\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\to0$, so every factor tends to $1$. Taking logarithms in the theorem, $\log p(n)=C\sqrt n-\log n-\log(4\sqrt3)+\log(1+O(n^{-1/4}))$, and the last two terms are bounded.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:saddle}
\idea One coefficient of a power series with positive coefficients is at most the whole sum; choosing the radius well is the saddle point method without the integral.
\writeup For $0<x<1$, $p(n)x^n\le\sum_mp(m)x^m=P(x)$, so $p(n)\le e^{nt}P(e^{-t})$ with $x=e^{-t}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans}, $P(e^{-t})\le\sqrt{\frac t{2\pi}}e^{\pi^2/(6t)}P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})$. With $t=\frac\pi{\sqrt{6n}}$, $\frac{\pi^2}{6t}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{6n}}6=\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}$ and $nt=\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}$; their sum is $2\pi\sqrt{\frac n6}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}3}$. Next, $\frac t{2\pi}=\frac1{2\sqrt{6n}}=(24n)^{-1/2}$. Finally $\frac{4\pi^2}t=4\pi\sqrt{6n}\ge30$, so $P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})\le1+2e^{-30}\le2$ by the bound $|P(w)-1|\le2|w|$ from the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}. Hence $p(n)\le2(24n)^{-1/4}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$. Compared with Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}, the bound is too large by a factor of order $n^{3/4}$: $n^{-1/4}$ against $n^{-1}$. The integral over the circle recovers the missing $n^{-3/4}$, which is the width $\sqrt{\pi/a}\asymp n^{-1/4}$ of the Gaussian times the circumference factor $y\asymp n^{-1/2}$. (At $n=1000$ the bound is $1129$ times $p(n)$, and $1000^{3/4}\approx178$.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:minusone}
\idea At $q\to-1$ the product splits into a part at $q^2\to1$, which is large, and a part that cancels most of it.
\writeup For $|q|<1$, $\frac1{P(-q)}=\prod_m(1-(-q)^m)=\prod_{m\text{ even}}(1-q^m)\prod_{m\text{ odd}}(1+q^m)$. The first product is $\frac1{P(q^2)}$. Since $1+q^m=\frac{1-q^{2m}}{1-q^m}$, $\prod_m(1+q^m)=\frac{P(q)}{P(q^2)}$, and the odd factors are this product divided by the even ones, $\prod_m(1+q^{2m})=\frac{P(q^2)}{P(q^4)}$. So $\prod_{m\text{ odd}}(1+q^m)=\frac{P(q)P(q^4)}{P(q^2)^2}$ and $\frac1{P(-q)}=\frac{P(q)P(q^4)}{P(q^2)^3}$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans}, $\log P(e^{-t})=\frac{\pi^2}{6t}+O(\log\frac1t)$ as $t\to0^+$. With $r=e^{-t}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\log P(-r)&=3\log P(e^{-2t})-\log P(e^{-t})-\log P(e^{-4t})\\&=\frac{\pi^2}t\Bigl(\frac14-\frac16-\frac1{24}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\log\frac1t\Bigr)\\&=\frac{\pi^2}{24t}+O\Bigl(\log\frac1t\Bigr).\end{aligned}
\]
As $t=-\log r\sim1-r$, $\log P(-r)\sim\frac{\pi^2}{24(1-r)}=\frac14\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{6(1-r)}$. This is the peak of height $\frac14$ at $x=\frac12$ in Figure~\ref{fig:circlearcs}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:ones}
\idea Counting pairs (partition, one of its ones) by the position of the one gives a sum of $p(n-k)$; the asymptotic formula turns that sum into a geometric series with ratio $e^{-C/(2\sqrt n)}$.
\writeup \emph{The identity.} Let $m_1(\lambda)$ be the number of ones in $\lambda$. Then $\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}m_1(\lambda)=\sum_{k\ge1}\#\{\lambda\vdash n:m_1(\lambda)\ge k\}$, and removing $k$ ones is a bijection from $\{\lambda\vdash n:m_1(\lambda)\ge k\}$ onto the partitions of $n-k$, with inverse ``add $k$ ones''. So the total is $\sum_{k=1}^np(n-k)$, and the average is $M_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{p(n-k)}{p(n)}$.
\emph{The asymptotics.} Let $C=\pi\sqrt{2/3}$ and $K=\lfloor n^{3/5}\rfloor$. For $k\le K$, $\sqrt{n-k}-\sqrt n=-\frac k{2\sqrt n}+O(\frac{k^2}{n^{3/2}})$ and $\frac{k^2}{n^{3/2}}\le n^{-3/10}$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:hr}, applied to $n$ and $n-k\ge\frac n2$,
\[
\frac{p(n-k)}{p(n)}=e^{-\frac{Ck}{2\sqrt n}}\bigl(1+O(n^{-1/4})\bigr)
\]
uniformly in $k\le K$. With $\delta=\frac C{2\sqrt n}$, $\sum_{k=1}^Ke^{-k\delta}=\frac{e^{-\delta}(1-e^{-K\delta})}{1-e^{-\delta}}\sim\frac1\delta=\frac{2\sqrt n}C$, because $\delta\to0$ and $K\delta\asymp n^{1/10}\to\infty$. For $k>K$, $p(n-k)\le p(n-K)$, since $p$ is non-decreasing (adding a part $1$ is an injection), so these terms add up to at most $n\frac{p(n-K)}{p(n)}\le2ne^{-K\delta}\to0$. So $M_n\sim\frac{2\sqrt n}C=\frac{\sqrt{6n}}\pi$. Numerically, $M_{1000}=24.47$ against $\frac{\sqrt{6000}}\pi=24.66$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:distinct}
\idea The same circle method, with two differences: the transformed function has no factor $\sqrt{-i\tau}$, which changes the power of $n$, and the shift is $+\frac1{24}$ instead of $-\frac1{24}$.
\writeup \emph{Transformation.} Since $1+z^m=\frac{1-z^{2m}}{1-z^m}$, $Q(z)=\frac{P(z)}{P(z^2)}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} at $\tau$ and at $2\tau$ gives
\[
\begin{aligned}Q(e^{2\pi i\tau})&=\frac{\sqrt{-i\tau}\,e^{\frac{\pi i}{12\tau}}e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}P(w_1)}{\sqrt{-2i\tau}\,e^{\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}}e^{\frac{\pi i\tau}6}P(w_2)}\\&=\frac1{\sqrt2}e^{\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}}e^{-\frac{\pi i\tau}{12}}\frac{P(w_1)}{P(w_2)},\end{aligned}
\]
with $w_1=e^{-2\pi i/\tau}$ and $w_2=e^{-\pi i/\tau}$.
\emph{Set-up.} As before $q(n)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}Q(e^{2\pi i\tau})e^{-2\pi in\tau}\dd x$. Let $N''=n+\frac1{24}$, $y=(48N'')^{-1/2}$ and $b=\frac\pi{24y}=2\pi N''y=\pi\sqrt{N''/12}$. With $x=yt$, $\tau=iy(1-it)$, exactly the computation of the theorem gives the exponent
\[
\frac{\pi i}{24\tau}-2\pi iN''\tau=2b-\frac{bt^2}{1+t^2}-\frac{ibt^3}{1+t^2}.
\]
Assume $n\ge9$, so that $y\le\frac1{20}$ and $b\ge1$.
\emph{Major arc.} For $|t|\le1$, $|w_1|\le e^{-\pi/y}$ and $|w_2|=\exp(-\frac\pi{y(1+t^2)})\le e^{-\pi/(2y)}$. Since $|P(w)-1|\le2|w|$, $|P(w_2)|\ge\frac12$ and $|\frac{P(w_1)}{P(w_2)}-1|\le2(|P(w_1)-1|+|P(w_2)-1|)\le8e^{-\pi/(2y)}$. As in the theorem, without the factor $(1-it)^{1/2}$, the major arc is $\frac y{\sqrt2}e^{2b}(\sqrt{\pi/b}+O(\frac1b))$. With $\sqrt{\pi/b}=\sqrt{24y}$, the main term is
\[
\frac y{\sqrt2}\sqrt{24y}\,e^{2b}=\sqrt{12}\,y^{3/2}e^{2b}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{8\cdot3^{3/4}N''^{3/4}}e^{2b}=\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{N''/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}N''^{3/4}},
\]
using $48^{3/4}=8\cdot3^{3/4}$.
\emph{Minor arc.} Let $r=e^{-2\pi y}$, $z=re^{2\pi ix}$, $\theta=2\pi x$. Since $(1+r^m)^2-|1+z^m|^2=2r^m(1-\cos m\theta)$ and $-\log(1-u)\ge u$,
\[
\log(1+r^m)-\log|1+z^m|=-\frac12\log\Bigl(1-\frac{2r^m(1-\cos m\theta)}{(1+r^m)^2}\Bigr)\ge\frac{r^m(1-\cos m\theta)}4.
\]
Summing over $m$, $\log Q(r)-\log|Q(z)|\ge\frac14\bigl(\frac r{1-r}-\operatorname{Re}\frac z{1-z}\bigr)\ge\frac14\bigl(\frac r{1-r}-\frac r{|1-z|}\bigr)$, which for $y\le|x|\le\frac12$ and $y\le\frac1{20}$ is at least $\frac1{4\cdot80y}$ by the minor arc computation of the theorem. For $\log Q(r)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:ptrans} at $t$ and $2t$ gives $Q(e^{-t})=\frac1{\sqrt2}e^{\pi^2/(12t)+t/24}\frac{P(e^{-4\pi^2/t})}{P(e^{-2\pi^2/t})}\le e^{\pi^2/(12t)+t/24}$, as $P$ is increasing on $[0,1[$. With $t=2\pi y$, $\frac{\pi^2}{12t}=b$, so $\log Q(r)\le b+1$. As $e^{2\pi ny}=e^{2\pi N''y-\pi y/12}\le e^b$, the minor arc contributes at most $e^{2b+1-1/(320y)}=e^{2b+1-\sqrt{48N''}/320}$, which is $o(y^{3/2}e^{2b})$.
\emph{Conclusion.} $q(n)\sim\frac{e^{\pi\sqrt{N''/3}}}{4\cdot3^{1/4}N''^{3/4}}$, and $\sqrt{N''}-\sqrt n\to0$ allows us to replace $N''$ by $n$. Numerically, $q(1000)=8\,635\,565\,795\,744\,155\,161\,506$, and the ratio to the asymptotic formula is $0.9946$.
\end{solution}
