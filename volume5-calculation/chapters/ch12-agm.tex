\chapter{The Arithmetic--Geometric Mean}\label{ch:agm}

\begin{center}\itshape
We have established that the arithmetic--geometric mean between $1$ and $\sqrt2$ is $\pi/\varpi$ to the eleventh decimal place; the
demonstration of this fact will surely open up an entirely new field of analysis.\\
\upshape --- Carl Friedrich Gauss, diary, 30 May 1799
\end{center}

Take two positive numbers. Replace them by their arithmetic mean and their geometric mean, and do it again. The two sequences run toward a common limit. Once they are close, the number of correct digits doubles at each step.

\portrait{gauss}{Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777--1855). Portrait by Christian Albrecht Jensen, 1840. Public domain.}

Gauss wrote the diary entry above on 30 May 1799, at the age of twenty-one. He had computed the common limit of $1$ and $\sqrt2$ to eleven decimals, and he had computed $\pi$ divided by the length of a half-lemniscate, and the two numbers agreed. That common limit is the \emph{arithmetic--geometric mean}. This chapter proves his theorem, by a substitution Newman published in 1985. The same iteration gives the perimeter of an ellipse and Legendre's relation between the two elliptic integrals. Brent and Salamin put the pieces together in 1976: their algorithm computes $\pi$, and the number of correct digits doubles at every step. The Gamma function, Wallis's product, Stirling's formula, the lemniscate of Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma} and the fast multiplication of Chapter~\ref{ch:fft} all meet in that algorithm.

\begin{think}
Start from $1$ and $\sqrt2$. After one step you have $1.2071$ and $1.1892$. After four steps both are $1.19814023473559\ldots$ How many steps would you expect to need for $1000$ digits? Gauss compared the limit with $2\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{1-t^4}}=2.62205755\ldots$, the length of a half-lemniscate (Exercise~\ref{ex:lemniscate} of Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma}). Divide $\pi$ by that length.
\end{think}

\section{The arithmetic--geometric mean}

Let $a\ge b>0$. Define $a_0=a$, $b_0=b$ and
\[
\begin{gathered}a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n+b_n}2,\qq b_{n+1}=\sqrt{a_nb_n},\end{gathered}
\]
and $c_n=\sqrt{a_n^2-b_n^2}\ge0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:agm}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $b\le b_n\le b_{n+1}\le a_{n+1}\le a_n\le a$ for every $n$, and $(a_n)$, $(b_n)$ have a common limit $M(a,b)$, the \emph{arithmetic--geometric mean} of $a$ and $b$.
\item $c_{n+1}=\frac{a_n-b_n}2$ and $c_{n+1}=\frac{c_n^2}{4a_{n+1}}$. Moreover $0\le a_{n+1}-b_{n+1}\le\frac{(a_n-b_n)^2}{8b}$.
\item $M(\lambda a,\lambda b)=\lambda M(a,b)$ for $\lambda>0$, and $M(a,b)=M\bigl(\frac{a+b}2,\sqrt{ab}\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The arithmetic--geometric inequality $\sqrt{a_nb_n}\le\frac{a_n+b_n}2$ gives $b_{n+1}\le a_{n+1}$. Since $b_n\le a_n$, both means lie between $b_n$ and $a_n$. So $(a_n)$ decreases and $(b_n)$ increases, and both are bounded. They converge, to limits $A$ and $B$. Pass to the limit in $a_{n+1}=\frac{a_n+b_n}2$. You get $A=\frac{A+B}2$, so $A=B$.

(b) Square the next difference of squares:
\[
c_{n+1}^2=\frac{(a_n+b_n)^2}4-a_nb_n=\frac{(a_n-b_n)^2}4.
\]
The positive square root is $c_{n+1}=\frac{a_n-b_n}2$. The same difference of squares factors as
\[
\frac{a_n-b_n}2=\frac{a_n^2-b_n^2}{2(a_n+b_n)}=\frac{c_n^2}{4a_{n+1}}.
\]
For the gap itself, rationalise:
\[
a_{n+1}-b_{n+1}=\frac{(\sqrt{a_n}-\sqrt{b_n})^2}2=\frac{(a_n-b_n)^2}{2(\sqrt{a_n}+\sqrt{b_n})^2}.
\]
The denominator is at least $8b$, because $(\sqrt{a_n}+\sqrt{b_n})^2\ge4b_n\ge4b$. That is the inequality in the statement.

(c) Scaling the start by $\lambda>0$ scales every later pair by $\lambda$. Starting from $(a_1,b_1)$ is the original sequence with the index shifted by one.
\end{proof}

Part (b) is why the digits double. Once $a_n-b_n$ is small compared with $8b$, the next gap is about the square of the present one. If $a_n-b_n\approx10^{-k}$, then $a_{n+1}-b_{n+1}\approx10^{-2k}$. For $M(1,\sqrt2)$ the gaps are $0.41$, $0.018$, $3.3\cdot10^{-5}$, $1.2\cdot10^{-10}$, $1.4\cdot10^{-21}$, $2\cdot10^{-43}$. Each step doubles the number of correct digits, and ten steps give a thousand digits. While $a_n$ is still much larger than $b_n$, the gap only halves at each step (Figure~\ref{fig:agm}). The start used for $\pi$ is closer. Figure~\ref{fig:rw13-iteration} runs the iteration from $1$ and $1/\sqrt2$. The gaps there are $0.293$, $0.0127$ and $2.4\times10^{-5}$. By the third step the two sequences agree to ten decimals, both equal to $0.8472130848$.

\section{Gauss's theorem}

For $a,b>0$ let
\[
I(a,b)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta}}.
\]
The substitution $\theta\mapsto\frac\pi2-\theta$ shows $I(a,b)=I(b,a)$, $I(\lambda a,\lambda b)=\frac1\lambda I(a,b)$, and $I(a,a)=\frac\pi{2a}$.

\begin{theorem}[Gauss, 1799]\label{thm:gaussagm}
For $a,b>0$,
\[
I(a,b)=\frac\pi{2M(a,b)}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: an algebraic form.} The job of this step is to move the integral from a quarter-circle to the whole line. Substitute $t=b\tan\theta$, a bijection from $\left[0,\frac\pi2\right[$ onto $[0,\infty[$, with $\mathrm dt=b(1+\tan^2\theta)\dd\theta$.
Then $a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta\,(a^2+t^2)$ and $1+\tan^2\theta=\frac1{\cos^2\theta}=\frac{b^2+t^2}{b^2}$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}I(a,b)&=\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{(a^2+t^2)(b^2+t^2)}}\eqbr=\frac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{(a^2+t^2)(b^2+t^2)}}.\end{aligned}
\]

\emph{Step 2: Newman's substitution.} The job of this step is to show that one AGM step leaves $I$ unchanged. Let $a_1=\frac{a+b}2$, $b_1=\sqrt{ab}$, and substitute $t=\frac12\bigl(x-\frac{ab}x\bigr)$, an increasing bijection from $]0,\infty[$
onto $\R$, with $\mathrm dt=\frac12\bigl(1+\frac{ab}{x^2}\bigr)\mathrm dx=\frac{x^2+ab}{2x^2}\dd x$. Expand the squares:
\[
\begin{gathered}t^2+b_1^2=\frac{(x^2+ab)^2}{4x^2},\qq t^2+a_1^2=\frac{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}{4x^2}.\end{gathered}
\]
(For the second: $t^2+a_1^2=\frac14\bigl(x^2-2ab+\frac{a^2b^2}{x^2}+a^2+2ab+b^2\bigr)$.) Write $D=\sqrt{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}$. Then
$\sqrt{(a_1^2+t^2)(b_1^2+t^2)}=\frac{(x^2+ab)D}{4x^2}$, so
\[
\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{(a_1^2+t^2)(b_1^2+t^2)}}&=\frac{x^2+ab}{2x^2}\cdot\frac{4x^2}{(x^2+ab)D}\dd x\eqbr=\frac{2\dd x}D.\end{aligned}
\]
By Step~1, $I(a_1,b_1)=\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{2\,\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}}=I(a,b)$.

\emph{Step 3: the limit.} The job of this step is to pass to the limit. By induction $I(a,b)=I(a_n,b_n)$ for every $n$, with the sequences of Proposition~\ref{prop:agm} (after swapping $a$ and $b$ if
needed). Since $b_n^2\le a_n^2\cos^2\theta+b_n^2\sin^2\theta\le a_n^2$, we have $\frac\pi{2a_n}\le I(a_n,b_n)\le\frac\pi{2b_n}$, and both bounds tend to $\frac\pi{2M(a,b)}$.
\end{proof}

The algebra sits in Step~2. That change of variable is the whole proof. Gauss used a longer trigonometric substitution. The one above is from a short paper of D.~J.~Newman (1985).

In terms of the modulus $k\in[0,1[$ and the complementary modulus $k'=\sqrt{1-k^2}$, the \emph{complete elliptic integrals} of the first and second kind are
\[
\begin{gathered}K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}},\qq E(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}\dd\theta.\end{gathered}
\]
Since $1-k^2\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta+k'^2\sin^2\theta$, we have $K(k)=I(1,k')=\frac\pi{2M(1,k')}$. So every complete integral of the first kind is an arithmetic--geometric mean.

\portrait{jacobi}{Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804--1851). Portrait, artist unknown. Public domain.}

In 1829 Jacobi published \emph{Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum}. He treated the upper limit of $K$ as a function of the value of the integral, the inversion Abel had found in 1827. The integrals below are the ones Legendre tabulated. The inversion itself belongs to the theory of elliptic functions, and it is a preview: nothing in this chapter depends on it.

\begin{corollary}[Landen's transformation]\label{cor:landen}
For $0\le k<1$, let $k_1=\frac{1-k'}{1+k'}$. Then $K(k)=(1+k_1)K(k_1)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Step~2, together with homogeneity, gives $K(k)=I(1,k')=I\bigl(\frac{1+k'}2,\sqrt{k'}\bigr)=\frac2{1+k'}I\bigl(1,\frac{2\sqrt{k'}}{1+k'}\bigr)$. The last argument is the complementary
modulus of $k_1$, because $1-\frac{4k'}{(1+k')^2}=\frac{(1-k')^2}{(1+k')^2}$. The prefactor is the same factor: $1+k_1=\frac2{1+k'}$.
\end{proof}

For small $k$, $k_1\approx\frac{k^2}4$. Each Landen step squares the modulus, and the modulus runs to $0$, where $K(0)=\frac\pi2$. That is the AGM again, written in the modulus instead of the two means.

\begin{corollary}[the lemniscate]\label{cor:lemniscate}
Let $\varpi=2\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dr}{\sqrt{1-r^4}}$, so that the lemniscate $(x^2+y^2)^2=x^2-y^2$ has length $2\varpi$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:lemniscate}). Then
\[
\begin{gathered}\frac\varpi\pi=\frac1{M(1,\sqrt2)},\qq\varpi=2.62205755429211981\ldots\end{gathered}
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Substitute $r=\cos\theta$. Then $\mathrm dr=-\sin\theta\dd\theta$ and $1-\cos^4\theta=\sin^2\theta\,(1+\cos^2\theta)$, so
\[
\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dr}{\sqrt{1-r^4}}=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{1+\cos^2\theta}}=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{2\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}}=I(\sqrt2,1).
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussagm} says this equals $\frac\pi{2M(1,\sqrt2)}$.
\end{proof}

The same curve has a focus description. The product of the distances from a point of the curve to the foci $\bigl(\pm\frac1{\sqrt2},0\bigr)$ equals $\frac12$. At $(1,0)$ the distances are $1-\frac1{\sqrt2}$ and $1+\frac1{\sqrt2}$, and their product is $\frac12$. In general, with $c=\frac1{\sqrt2}$,
\[
\bigl((x-c)^2+y^2\bigr)\bigl((x+c)^2+y^2\bigr)=\bigl(x^2+y^2+c^2\bigr)^2-4c^2x^2.
\]
Set the product of the squares of the distances equal to $\frac14$. Since $c^2=\frac12$ and $4c^2=2$,
\[
\bigl(x^2+y^2+\tfrac12\bigr)^2-2x^2=\bigl(x^2+y^2\bigr)^2+x^2+y^2+\tfrac14-2x^2.
\]
The constant $\frac14$ cancels. With $A=x^2+y^2$ one has $A-2x^2=y^2-x^2$, so $A^2=x^2-y^2$. Figure~\ref{fig:rw13-lemniscate} sketches the curve and one pair of distances.

\begin{beauty}
This is the identity in the diary. Gauss had both numbers to eleven decimals, and the entry records the day he saw that they agreed, before he had a proof. He then built the lemniscatic functions, the inverses of $\int\frac{\mathrm dr}{\sqrt{1-r^4}}$. Those are the first elliptic functions. The ``entirely new field of analysis'' in the diary is the theory of elliptic functions and modular forms. Volume~13 opens that field. This is a preview: nothing here depends on it. With Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma}, the corollary also computes $\Gamma(\frac14)$ by the AGM (Exercise~\ref{ex:gamma14}).
\end{beauty}

\section{The perimeter of an ellipse}

Take the ellipse with semi-axes $a\ge b>0$, parametrised by $(a\cos t,b\sin t)$. Its speed is $\sqrt{a^2\sin^2t+b^2\cos^2t}$, so the perimeter is $L(a,b)=4J(a,b)$, where
\[
J(a,b)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta}\dd\theta
\]
after the change $\theta=\frac\pi2-t$. Let $k$ be the eccentricity. Then $b=ak'$ and $J(a,b)=aE(k)$. A circle gives $J(a,a)=\frac{\pi a}2$. For the rest of the section, $a,b>0$, and we write
$R=a^2\cos^2\theta+b^2\sin^2\theta$. Thus $I=\int_0^{\pi/2}R^{-1/2}$ and $J=\int_0^{\pi/2}R^{1/2}$. The AGM sequences start from $a_0=a$ and $b_0=b$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:jderiv}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $\displaystyle J(a,b)=a^2b^2\int_0^{\pi/2}R^{-3/2}\dd\theta$.
\item $I$ is of class $C^1$ on $]0,\infty[^2$, $a\partial_aI+b\partial_bI=-I$, and, for $a\ne b$,
\[
J(a,b)=a^2I(a,b)+a(a^2-b^2)\,\partial_aI(a,b).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
(a) The job is one differentiation, arranged so that the boundary term vanishes. Write $c=\cos^2\theta$ and $s=\sin^2\theta$. The derivative of $\sin\theta\cos\theta\,R^{-1/2}$ is
$(c-s)R^{-1/2}-\sin\theta\cos\theta\cdot\frac12R^{-3/2}\cdot2(b^2-a^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta$, that is $\bigl((c-s)R-(b^2-a^2)cs\bigr)R^{-3/2}$. Using $c+s=1$, the
numerator is $a^2c^2-b^2s^2$. When $a\ne b$ the same numerator is $\frac{R^2-a^2b^2}{a^2-b^2}$, because
$R^2-a^2b^2=a^4c^2+b^4s^2-a^2b^2(c^2+s^2)=(a^2-b^2)(a^2c^2-b^2s^2)$. So for $a\ne b$
\[
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta}\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sqrt R}\Bigr)=\frac{R^{1/2}-a^2b^2R^{-3/2}}{a^2-b^2}.
\]
The left side integrates to $0$ over $[0,\frac\pi2]$, and that is (a). For $a=b$, both sides of (a) equal $\frac{\pi a}2$.

(b) The job is to differentiate under the integral and then eliminate one integral. The integrand of $I$ is $C^1$ in $(a,b)$, with partial derivatives continuous on $[0,\frac\pi2]\times\left]0,\infty\right[^2$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact} (applied in each variable)
gives $\partial_aI=-a\int\cos^2\theta\,R^{-3/2}$ and $\partial_bI=-b\int\sin^2\theta\,R^{-3/2}$, both continuous. Let $P=\int c\,R^{-3/2}$ and $Q=\int s\,R^{-3/2}$. Then
$a^2P+b^2Q=\int R\cdot R^{-3/2}=I$, which is the identity $a\partial_aI+b\partial_bI=-I$, and $P+Q=\int R^{-3/2}=\frac J{a^2b^2}$ by (a). Eliminate $Q$:
$(a^2-b^2)P=I-\frac J{a^2}$. Since $\partial_aI=-aP$, this is the formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:jstep}
For $a,b>0$, with $a_1=\frac{a+b}2$ and $b_1=\sqrt{ab}$,
\[
2J(a_1,b_1)-J(a,b)=ab\,I(a,b).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The two sides are continuous in $(a,b)$, so the identity for $a\ne b$ extends to the diagonal $a=b$. The job on $a\ne b$ is to differentiate $I(a,b)=I(a_1,b_1)$ and substitute Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}. Write $I_1=\partial_1I(a_1,b_1)$ for the partial derivative of $I$ in its first variable at
$(a_1,b_1)$, and $I=I(a,b)=I(a_1,b_1)$. Differentiate the identity $I(a,b)=I\bigl(\frac{a+b}2,\sqrt{ab}\bigr)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussagm} in $a$, by the chain rule:
\[
\partial_aI(a,b)=\frac12I_1+\frac{b_1}{2a}\,\partial_2I(a_1,b_1).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}(b) at $(a_1,b_1)$, $b_1\partial_2I(a_1,b_1)=-I-a_1I_1$, so $\partial_aI(a,b)=\frac{a-a_1}{2a}I_1-\frac I{2a}=\frac{a-b}{4a}I_1-\frac I{2a}$. Put this
into Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}(b) at $(a,b)$:
\[
J(a,b)=a^2I-\frac{a^2-b^2}2I+\frac{(a^2-b^2)(a-b)}4I_1.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}(b) at $(a_1,b_1)$, where $a_1^2-b_1^2=\frac{(a-b)^2}4$, gives $J(a_1,b_1)=a_1^2I+\frac{(a+b)(a-b)^2}8I_1$. So the last term above is
$2\bigl(J(a_1,b_1)-a_1^2I\bigr)$, and
\[
\begin{aligned}J(a,b)&=2J(a_1,b_1)+\Bigl(a^2-\frac{a^2-b^2}2-\frac{(a+b)^2}2\Bigr)I\\&=2J(a_1,b_1)-abI.\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the perimeter by the AGM]\label{thm:perimeter}
For $a\ge b>0$, with $c_0^2=a^2-b^2$ and $c_n$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:agm},
\[
J(a,b)=\Bigl(a^2-\sum_{n\ge0}2^{n-1}c_n^2\Bigr)I(a,b),
\]
so the ellipse with semi-axes $a\ge b$ has perimeter
\[
L(a,b)=\frac{2\pi}{M(a,b)}\Bigl(a^2-\sum_{n\ge0}2^{n-1}c_n^2\Bigr).
\]
In terms of the modulus, with the AGM started from $1$ and $k'$, so that $c_0=k$,
\[
E(k)=\Bigl(1-\sum_{n\ge0}2^{n-1}c_n^2\Bigr)K(k).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Unwind one step.} Let $I=I(a,b)=I(a_n,b_n)$ for all $n$, and $d_n=J(a_n,b_n)-a_n^2I$. The job of $d_n$ is to hold the part of $J$ that is still moving. Proposition~\ref{prop:jstep} at $(a_n,b_n)$ reads $J(a_n,b_n)=2J(a_{n+1},b_{n+1})-a_nb_nI$, that is
\[
\begin{aligned}d_n&=2d_{n+1}+\bigl(2a_{n+1}^2-a_nb_n-a_n^2\bigr)I\eqbr=2d_{n+1}-\frac{c_n^2}2I,\end{aligned}
\]
because $2a_{n+1}^2=\frac{a_n^2+2a_nb_n+b_n^2}2$. Iterate that relation. You get $d_0=2^Nd_N-I\sum_{n<N}2^{n-1}c_n^2$.

\emph{The remainder dies.} Now $\frac\pi2b_n\le J(a_n,b_n)\le\frac\pi2a_n$ and $\frac\pi2a_n\le a_n^2I\le\frac{\pi a_n^2}{2b_n}$, so $|d_n|\le\frac{\pi(a_n^2-b_n^2)}{2b_n}\le\frac{\pi c_n^2}{2b}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:agm}(b), $c_{n+1}\le\frac{c_n^2}{4M}$ with $M=M(a,b)$. Since $c_n\to0$, there is an $N_0$ with $\frac{c_{N_0}}{4M}\le\frac12$, and then $c_{N_0+j}\le4M\cdot2^{-2^j}$. So $2^Nc_N^2\to0$ and $2^Nd_N\to0$. The series converges, and $d_0=-I\sum_{n\ge0}2^{n-1}c_n^2$, which is the formula. The perimeter is $4J$, and $I=\frac\pi{2M}$. For the last statement take $a=1$ and $b=k'$.
\end{proof}

For the ellipse with semi-axes $2$ and $1$, five steps of the AGM give
\[
L=9.68844822054767619842\ldots
\]
with every digit correct. The Earth's orbit has eccentricity $0.0167$, and its perimeter is $2\pi a\times0.99993027\ldots$. An ellipse that close to a circle is a circle for most purposes.

\begin{think}
The area of an ellipse is $\pi ab$. Is there a formula of the same kind for its perimeter, built from $a$, $b$, square roots, $\pi$ and logarithms? What would such a formula give when $b\to0$, and when $b=a$?
\end{think}

\begin{remark}
There is no such formula. $E(k)$ is not an elementary function of $k$. This follows from Liouville's theory of integration in finite terms, which decides when an integral of an algebraic function is elementary. The survey of Marchisotto and Zakeri, in the references, is a way in. Theorem~\ref{thm:perimeter} is a quadratically convergent sequence of algebraic operations, and that is the closed form the perimeter has. Ramanujan found sharp approximations. One of them is $L\approx\pi\bigl(3(a+b)-\sqrt{(3a+b)(a+3b)}\bigr)$. Its relative error is $3\cdot10^{-6}$ for $a=2b$, and at most $0.5\%$ for every ellipse.
\end{remark}

\section{Legendre's relation and the computation of $\pi$}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kederiv}
On $]0,1[$, $K$ and $E$ are differentiable, with
\[
\begin{gathered}\frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dk}=\frac{E-K}k,\qq\frac{\mathrm dK}{\mathrm dk}=\frac{E-k'^2K}{k\,k'^2}.\end{gathered}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The job is to differentiate under the integral and then quote Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}(a). Let $\Delta=\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}$. Differentiating under the integral sign (Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact}, on $[0,\frac\pi2]\times[0,k_1]$ for any $k_1<1$),
$\frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dk}=-\int\frac{k\sin^2\theta}\Delta$ and $\frac{\mathrm dK}{\mathrm dk}=\int\frac{k\sin^2\theta}{\Delta^3}$. Since $k^2\sin^2\theta=1-\Delta^2$, the first is
$\frac1k\int\frac{\Delta^2-1}\Delta=\frac{E-K}k$, and the second is $\frac1k\bigl(\int\Delta^{-3}-K\bigr)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:jderiv}(a) with $a=1$, $b=k'$ (so that $R=\Delta^2$ and
$J=E$) gives $\int\Delta^{-3}=\frac E{k'^2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Legendre's relation, 1811]\label{thm:legendre}
For $0<k<1$, with $K'=K(k')$ and $E'=E(k')$,
\[
EK'+E'K-KK'=\frac\pi2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The derivative.} Let $W(k)=EK'+E'K-KK'$. This is Legendre's auxiliary function. It depends on $k$ alone, since $k'=\sqrt{1-k^2}$. The letter $W$ is the one used here. By Proposition~\ref{prop:kederiv} and the chain rule with $\frac{\mathrm dk'}{\mathrm dk}=-\frac k{k'}$,
\[
\begin{gathered}\frac{\mathrm dE'}{\mathrm dk}=-\frac{k(E'-K')}{k'^2},\qq\frac{\mathrm dK'}{\mathrm dk}=-\frac{E'-k^2K'}{k\,k'^2}.\end{gathered}
\]
Multiply $W'=E_kK'+EK'_k+E'_kK+E'K_k-K_kK'-KK'_k$ by $kk'^2$ and substitute the four derivatives. Four types of monomial appear, and each coefficient is zero. The terms in $EE'$ are $-EE'+E'E=0$. The terms in $EK'$ are $k'^2+k^2-1=0$ times $EK'$. The terms in $E'K$ are $(-k^2-k'^2+1)E'K=0$. The terms in $KK'$ are $(-k'^2+k^2+k'^2-k^2)KK'=0$. So $W$ is constant on $]0,1[$.

\emph{The constant.} Let $k\to0^+$. First, $K-E=\int\frac{k^2\sin^2\theta}\Delta\le\frac{\pi k^2}{2k'}$. Second, $K'=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+k^2\sin^2\theta}}=O\bigl(\ln\frac1k\bigr)$:
with $u=\frac\pi2-\theta$, on $u\le\frac\pi4$ the integrand is at most $\bigl(\frac{4u^2}{\pi^2}+\frac{k^2}2\bigr)^{-1/2}$, whose integral is $\frac\pi2\operatorname{arsinh}\frac1{\sqrt2\,k}$, and on $u\ge\frac\pi4$
it is at most $\sqrt2$. Third, $E'=\int\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+k^2\sin^2\theta}\to\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\dd\theta=1$, since the integrands differ by at most $k$. Hence
$W=E'K-(K-E)K'\to1\cdot\frac\pi2-0$, because $K\to K(0)=\frac\pi2$ and $(K-E)K'=O\bigl(k^2\ln\frac1k\bigr)\to0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Brent--Salamin, 1976]\label{thm:brentsalamin}
Run the AGM from $a_0=1$, $b_0=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, and let $c_n=\frac{a_{n-1}-b_{n-1}}2$ for $n\ge1$. Then
\[
\pi=\frac{4M(1,\frac1{\sqrt2})^2}{1-\sum_{n\ge1}2^{n+1}c_n^2},
\]
and the approximations $\pi_n=\frac{4a_{n+1}^2}{1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}2^{j+1}c_j^2}$ converge to $\pi$. The first is $\pi_1=3.1405\ldots$, and the errors of $\pi_2,\dots,\pi_8$ are below $10^{-8}$, $10^{-18}$, $10^{-40}$, $10^{-83}$, $10^{-170}$, $10^{-344}$ and $10^{-693}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The job is to specialise Legendre's relation at the one modulus where $k=k'$. At $k=k'=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, the relation reads $2EK-K^2=\frac\pi2$. By Theorems~\ref{thm:gaussagm} and~\ref{thm:perimeter}, with $M=M(1,\frac1{\sqrt2})$, $K=\frac\pi{2M}$ and
$E=\bigl(1-\sum_{n\ge0}2^{n-1}c_n^2\bigr)K$, where $c_0^2=\frac12$ (and $c_n=\frac{a_{n-1}-b_{n-1}}2$ for $n\ge1$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:agm}(b)). So
\[
\frac\pi2=K^2\Bigl(1-\sum_{n\ge0}2^nc_n^2\Bigr)=\frac{\pi^2}{4M^2}\Bigl(\frac12-\sum_{n\ge1}2^nc_n^2\Bigr),
\]
which rearranges to the formula. Since $a_{n+1}\to M$ and the partial sums converge, $\pi_n\to\pi$. The sizes of the errors are computed values:
they come from running the eight steps in high precision, and Exercise~\ref{ex:agmsteps} explains why the exponent doubles.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Each step costs a few multiplications and one square root, on numbers with $N$ digits. With the fast multiplication of Chapter~\ref{ch:fft}, and with Newton's method for square roots, each of those costs $O(N\log N\log\log N)$ operations. About $\log_2 N$ steps are needed, so $N$ digits of $\pi$ cost $O\bigl(N(\log N)^2\log\log N\bigr)$. Machin's series $\frac\pi4=4\arctan\frac15-\arctan\frac1{239}$, with schoolbook arithmetic, costs $O(N^2)$. Salamin and Brent found the algorithm independently in 1976. Every ingredient was already in Gauss and Legendre. Most records since then have used the series of the Chudnovsky brothers (1988),
\[
\frac1\pi=12\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n(6n)!\,(13591409+545140134n)}{(3n)!\,(n!)^3\,640320^{3n+3/2}},
\]
which gives $14$ digits per term and is evaluated by binary splitting with fast multiplication. Its proof uses modular forms. That proof lives in Volume~13, and it is a preview.
\end{remark}

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

Two numbers, two means, and repeat. The common limit $M(a,b)$ computes the integral $I(a,b)=\frac\pi{2M(a,b)}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussagm}). The proof is one change of variable: the integral stays fixed while $(a,b)$ is replaced by the two means. The same iteration, together with the differences $c_n$ it throws off, gives the perimeter of the ellipse (Theorem~\ref{thm:perimeter}). Legendre's relation (Theorem~\ref{thm:legendre}), through the auxiliary function $W$, ties the two integrals together at $k=\frac1{\sqrt2}$. What falls out is $\pi$, and the error is squared at every step (Theorem~\ref{thm:brentsalamin}). The first think box asked for $\pi$ divided by the half-lemniscate. The answer is $M(1,\sqrt2)=\frac\pi\varpi$, which is what Gauss saw in May 1799.

This is the last chapter. The volume ends on a computation, as it began. The next book is Volume~6, \emph{Crossroads: Arithmetic Meets Analysis}. It takes the zeta function of Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta} and the asymptotic tools of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics} and turns them on the primes in bulk. The prime-number theorem is that book's theorem. The elliptic integrals $K$ and $E$, read at a complex modulus, open the door to elliptic functions and modular forms. Volume~13 walks through that door. Both of those are previews.

By how much is the period at $\theta_0=\frac\pi2$ longer than the small-oscillation period $2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:cyclewalk}
A token sits on the vertices $0,1,\dots,k-1$ of a regular $k$-gon, with $k\ge3$. At each step it moves to one of the two neighbouring vertices, each with probability $\frac12$, and the steps are independent. Let $q_n$ be the probability of being at $0$ after $n$ steps, starting from $0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $q_n=\frac1k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\cos^n\frac{2\pi j}k$.
\item Give $q_n$ in closed form for $k=3$, $4$ and $6$.
\item Find the limit of $q_n$ as $n\to\infty$, according to the parities of $k$ and $n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:detsquare}
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with independent entries, each uniform on $\{-1,1\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\mathbb E[\det A]=0$ and $\mathbb E[(\det A)^2]=n!$.
\item Deduce that some $n\times n$ matrix with entries $\pm1$ has $|\det A|\ge\sqrt{n!}$.
\item Show that every real $n\times n$ matrix with entries in $[-1,1]$ satisfies $|\det A|\le n^{n/2}$ (Hadamard's
inequality), and compare the two bounds as $n\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:hadamard}
A \emph{Hadamard matrix} of order $n$ is an $n\times n$ matrix $H$ whose entries are $\pm1$ and which satisfies $HH^{\mathsf T}=nI_n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if a Hadamard matrix of order $n>2$ exists, then $4\mid n$.
\item Show that if $H$ is a Hadamard matrix of order $n$, then $\begin{pmatrix}H&H\\H&-H\end{pmatrix}$ is one of order $2n$.
\item (Paley, 1933.) Let $p\equiv3\pmod4$ be a prime, $\chi$ the Legendre symbol modulo $p$ (with $\chi(0)=0$), and $Q$ the
$p\times p$ matrix with entries $Q_{ab}=\chi(b-a)$, $a,b\in\mathbb F_p$. Show that $Q^{\mathsf T}=-Q$, that every row of $Q$
sums to $0$, and that $QQ^{\mathsf T}=pI_p-J_p$, where $J_p$ is the all-ones matrix. Deduce a Hadamard matrix of order $p+1$.
\item List the orders $n\le64$ obtained from (b) and (c).
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:rank}
Let $G$ act transitively on a set $X$ of $n\ge2$ points. Draw $g$ uniformly from $G$, and let $F$ be the number of points $g$ fixes. The \emph{rank} $r$ of the action is the number of orbits of $G$ on $X\times X$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\mathbb E[F]=1$ and $\mathbb E[F^2]=r\ge2$.
\item Show that the following are equivalent: $G$ is $2$-transitive (transitive on ordered pairs of distinct points);
$r=2$; $\operatorname{Var}F=1$; the character $\pi-1$ is irreducible, where $\pi(g)=F(g)$ is the permutation character.
\item Find the rank of the dihedral group $D_n$ acting on the $n$ vertices of a regular polygon.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:derangements}
Let $G$ act transitively and faithfully on a set $X$ with $n\ge2$ points.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Cameron and Cohen.) Show that at least a proportion $\frac1n$ of the elements of $G$ have no fixed point.
\item Show that equality holds if and only if $G$ is \emph{sharply $2$-transitive}: for all pairs $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ of
distinct points there is exactly one $g$ with $gx=x'$ and $gy=y'$. Check it for the group of maps $t\mapsto at+b$ of
$\mathbb F_p$, $a\ne0$.
\item (Jordan.) Deduce that a finite group is never the union of the conjugates of a proper subgroup.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:noroots}
\emph{Roots of a random polynomial.} Let $f$ be a monic polynomial of degree $n\ge1$ over $\mathbb F_q$, drawn uniformly, and let $R$ be its number of roots in $\mathbb F_q$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if $n\ge q$, the values $f(a)$, $a\in\mathbb F_q$, are independent and uniform, so that $R$ is exactly binomial
with parameters $q$ and $\frac1q$. In particular $P(R=0)=(1-\frac1q)^q$.
\item For $n<q$, show that
\[
P(R=0)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom qk q^{-k},
\]
and compare with the probability $\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$ that a random permutation of $n$ points has no fixed point.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:gaussmeasure}
\emph{The Gauss measure.} On $]0,1[$ let $T(x)=\{1/x\}$, the fractional part of $1/x$, so that $a_{n+1}(x)=\lfloor1/T^nx\rfloor$ is the $(n+1)$-st partial quotient of $x$. Let $\mu$ be the probability measure on $[0,1]$ with density $\frac1{(1+x)\log2}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\mu(T^{-1}[0,t[)=\mu([0,t[)$ for every $t\in[0,1]$: $\mu$ is invariant under $T$.
\item Deduce that, if $x$ has law $\mu$, then $P(a_n=k)=\log_2\frac{(k+1)^2}{k(k+2)}$ for every $n\ge1$ and $k\ge1$.
\item If $x$ is uniform instead, compute $P(a_1=k)$, and compare with (b) for $k=1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:digitsclt}
\emph{Binary digits.} Let $s(n)$ be the number of ones in the binary expansion of $n$, and draw $n$ uniformly from $\{0,\dots,N-1\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item If $N=2^k$, show that $s(n)$ is binomial with parameters $k$ and $\frac12$.
\item For general $N$, show that $\bigl(s(n)-\frac12\log_2N\bigr)/\sqrt{\frac14\log_2N}$ converges in law to a standard Gaussian.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:kolberg}
\emph{Both parities, forever (Kolberg, 1959).} Prove that $p(n)$ is even for infinitely many $n$, and odd for infinitely many $n$.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:kummer}
\emph{Kummer's theorem (1852).} Fix a prime $p$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $v_p\binom{m+n}m$ is the number of carries when $m$ and $n$ are added in base $p$.
\item Deduce that every prime power dividing $\binom nk$ is at most $n$.
\item Deduce that $\binom nk$ divides $\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\dots,n)$, and that $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n)\ge\frac{2^n}{n+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:pascalfour}
\emph{Modulo $4$.} For $n\ge1$, let $B(n)$ be the number of times the block $10$ occurs in the binary expansion of $n$. For example, $B(10)=B(1010_2)=2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that exactly $B(n)\,2^{s(n)-1}$ entries of row $n$ are $\equiv2\pmod4$.
\item Which rows contain no entry $\equiv2\pmod4$?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:centralp}
\emph{Central binomial coefficients modulo $p$.} Let $p$ be an odd prime, and let $f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\binom{2n}nx^n=(1-4x)^{-1/2}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $f(x)\equiv(1-4x)^{(p-1)/2}f(x^p)\pmod p$, coefficient by coefficient.
\item Deduce that $\binom{2n}n\equiv\prod_i\binom{2n_i}{n_i}\pmod p$, where $n=\sum_in_ip^i$ in base $p$.
\item Show that $p\nmid\binom{2n}n$ if and only if every base-$p$ digit of $n$ is at most $\frac{p-1}2$, and that the proportion of
such $n$ below $p^k$ tends to $0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:sternfib}
\emph{Stern meets Pascal and Fibonacci.} Use the notation of Problem~\ref{pb:stern}, and let $F_n$ be the Fibonacci sequence.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $s(n+1)$ is the number of $k$ such that $\binom{n-k}k$ is odd.
\item Show that $s(n)\le F_{k+2}$ for $2^k\le n\le2^{k+1}$.
\item Show that equality holds for $n=\frac13\bigl(2^{k+2}-(-1)^k\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:thuemorse}
\emph{The Thue--Morse series is algebraic.} Let $t_n=s(n)\bmod2\in\mathbb F_2$, where $s(n)$ is again the sum of the binary digits, and let $T(x)=\sum_nt_nx^n\in\mathbb F_2[[x]]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $(1+x)^3T^2+(1+x)^2T+x=0$ in $\mathbb F_2[[x]]$.
\item Show that $(t_n)$ is not eventually periodic.
\item Deduce that $T$ is algebraic of degree $2$ over $\mathbb F_2(x)$ but not rational.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:hermite}
\emph{Powers of $e$.} First the integer powers, then every rational power and every rational logarithm.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $e^k$ is irrational for every integer $k\ge1$.
\item Deduce that $e^r$ is irrational for every rational $r\ne0$, and that $\log r$ is irrational for every rational $r>0$, $r\ne1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:centralsum}
\emph{Reciprocals of central binomial coefficients.} Part (a) begins with a Beta integral.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\displaystyle\frac1{n\binom{2n}n}=\int_0^1t^{n-1}(1-t)^n\dd t$ for $n\ge1$, and deduce that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n\binom{2n}n}=\frac\pi{3\sqrt3}$.
\item Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac1{\binom{2n}n}=\frac43+\frac{2\pi\sqrt3}{27}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star$]\label{pb:lehmer}
\emph{Series with $\binom{2n}n/4^n$.} Write $c_n=\binom{2n}n4^{-n}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{c_n}{2n+1}=\frac\pi2$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{c_n}n=2\log2$.
\item Show that $\sum_nc_n^2$ diverges, while $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{c_n^2}{n+1}=\frac4\pi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:catalanmoments}
\emph{Catalan numbers are moments.} Let $C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}n$ be the Catalan numbers.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\displaystyle\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-2}^2x^{2n}\sqrt{4-x^2}\dd x=C_n$, while the odd moments vanish.
\item Show that $\displaystyle\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^4x^n\sqrt{\frac{4-x}x}\dd x=C_n$.
\item From (b), show that $C_n\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2}}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:apcount}
\emph{Counting progressions with Fourier.} Let $p$ be an odd prime and $A\subseteq\mathbb F_p$. Let $T(A)$ be the number of pairs $(x,d)\in\mathbb F_p^2$ for which $x$, $x+d$ and $x+2d$ all lie in $A$. Pairs with $d=0$ are included.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $T(A)=\frac1p\sum_{r\in\mathbb F_p}\hat A(r)^2\hat A(-2r)$.
\item Deduce that $\bigl|T(A)-\frac{|A|^3}p\bigr|\le|A|\max_{r\ne0}|\hat A(r)|$.
\item Let $Q$ be the set of non-zero squares modulo $p$. Show that $\bigl|T(Q)-\frac{(p-1)^3}{8p}\bigr|\le\frac{(\sqrt p+1)(p-1)}4$; so $T(Q)=\frac{p^2}8+O(p^{3/2})$.
\item Let $|A|=\delta p$. Show that if $|\hat A(r)|\le\frac12\delta^2p$ for all $r\ne0$ and $\delta^2p>2$, then $A$ contains a
progression $x,x+d,x+2d$ with $d\ne0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:dftspectrum}
\emph{The eigenvalues of the discrete Fourier transform.} Let $n\ge2$, and let $F$ be the unitary DFT on $\C^n$, given by $(Fx)_j=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}x_ke(-\frac{jk}n)$, with indices modulo $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $F^2$ is the reversal $x_k\mapsto x_{-k}$, that $F^4=I$, and that $F$ is diagonalisable with eigenvalues among
$1,-1,-i,i$. Call their multiplicities $m_1$, $m_{-1}$, $m_{-i}$, $m_i$.
\item Express $\operatorname{tr}F$ and $\operatorname{tr}F^2$ in terms of the multiplicities, and compute both traces.
\item Deduce the multiplicities: writing $n=4m+s$ with $0\le s\le3$, the vector $(m_1,m_{-1},m_{-i},m_i)$ is
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
s&(m_1,m_{-1},m_{-i},m_i)\\\hline
0&(m+1,m,m,m-1)\\
1&(m+1,m,m,m)\\
2&(m+1,m+1,m,m)\\
3&(m+1,m+1,m+1,m)
\end{array}
\]
for $s=0,1,2,3$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:erdosturan}
\emph{An Erdős--Turán inequality.} Let $x_1,\dots,x_N\in[0,1[$ and $S(h)=\sum_{n=1}^Ne(hx_n)$. The \emph{discrepancy} $D$ is the supremum, over intervals $I\subseteq[0,1[$, of $\bigl|\frac1N\#\{n:x_n\in I\}-|I|\bigr|$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Let $0<\delta<1$ and let $J$ be an interval of length $L\le1-\delta$. The $1$-periodic \emph{trapezoid} $t$ is the convolution
of the periodic indicator of $J$ with $\frac1\delta1_{[0,\delta]}$. Show that $0\le t\le1$, $\int_0^1t=L$, and that its Fourier
coefficients satisfy $|\hat t(h)|\le\min\bigl(\frac1{\pi|h|},\frac1{\pi^2h^2\delta}\bigr)$ for $h\ne0$.
\item Deduce that
\[
D\le2\delta+\frac2N\sum_{h\ge1}\min\Bigl(\frac1{\pi h},\frac1{\pi^2h^2\delta}\Bigr)|S(h)|.
\]
\item Let $p$ be an odd prime and $x_k=\{\frac{k^2}p\}$ for $0\le k<p$. Show that the discrepancy of these $p$ points is at most
$\frac{\log p+10}{\pi\sqrt p}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:circlewalk}
\emph{A random walk on the circle.} Let $\alpha$ be real and let $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\dots$ be independent fair signs. Set $X_n=\{\alpha(\varepsilon_1+\cdots+\varepsilon_n)\}\in[0,1[$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if $\alpha$ is irrational, $X_n$ converges in law to the uniform distribution: $\mathbb E[g(X_n)]\to\int_0^1g$ for every
continuous $1$-periodic $g$.
\item Let $\alpha=\frac aq$ in lowest terms, $q\ge2$. Show that if $q$ is odd, $X_n$ converges in law to the uniform distribution on
$\{0,\frac1q,\dots,\frac{q-1}q\}$, and that if $q$ is even, $X_{2n}$ and $X_{2n+1}$ converge in law to the uniform distributions on two
disjoint sets of $\frac q2$ points, so that $X_n$ does not converge.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:fejer}
\emph{Non-negative trigonometric polynomials.} Let $T(\theta)=\sum_{|k|\le n}c_ke^{ik\theta}$ be real-valued, and assume $T\ge0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Fejér--Riesz, 1916.) Show that $T(\theta)=|P(e^{i\theta})|^2$ for a polynomial $P\in\C[z]$ of degree at most $n$.
\item (Fejér.) Suppose $c_0=1$. Show that $T(0)\le n+1$, with equality only for the Fejér kernel $\frac1{n+1}\bigl|\sum_{k=0}^ne^{ik\theta}\bigr|^2$.
\item (Egerváry--Szász.) Suppose $T(\theta)=1+\sum_{k=1}^na_k\cos k\theta$ with real $a_k$. Show that $a_1\le2\cos\frac\pi{n+2}$, and that equality is
attained.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:bernstein}
\emph{Bernstein's inequality.} Let $T$ be a real trigonometric polynomial of degree at most $n$, and write $\|T\|=\max|T|$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that if $\|T\|\le1$, then $T'(\theta)^2+n^2T(\theta)^2\le n^2$ for all $\theta$. Deduce $\|T'\|\le n\|T\|$, and that the same holds for
complex-valued $T$.
\item Deduce that a real polynomial $P$ of degree $n$ with $|P|\le1$ on $[-1,1]$ satisfies $|P'(x)|\le\frac n{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ for $|x|<1$.
\item Show that both inequalities are sharp.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:bernsteinabs}
\emph{Absolutely convergent Fourier series.} Let $f$ be $2\pi$-periodic, and suppose $|f(x)-f(y)|\le C|x-y|^\alpha$ for all $x,y$, with $0<\alpha\le1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that for every $h$, $\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi|f(x+h)-f(x-h)|^2\dd x=\sum_k4\sin^2(kh)\,|c_k(f)|^2$.
\item (S.~Bernstein, 1914.) Suppose $\alpha>\frac12$. Show that $\sum_k|c_k(f)|<\infty$, and that the Fourier series of $f$ converges
uniformly to $f$.
\item Let $P_j$ be the Rudin--Shapiro polynomials of Problem~\ref{pb:rudin}, and $f(x)=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}e^{i2^jx}P_j(e^{ix})$. Show that $f$
satisfies the hypothesis with $\alpha=\frac12$, but that $\sum_k|c_k(f)|=\infty$. So $\frac12$ is the threshold.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:fantaussky}
\emph{Polygons by the discrete Fourier transform.} Let $n\ge3$ and $\omega=e(\frac1n)$. Indices are read modulo $n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item (Fan, Taussky and Todd, 1955.) Let $x_0,\dots,x_{n-1}\in\C$ with $\sum_kx_k=0$. Show that
\[
\sum_k|x_{k+1}-x_k|^2\ge4\sin^2\frac\pi n\sum_k|x_k|^2,
\]
with equality if and only if $x_k=a\omega^k+b\omega^{-k}$ for some $a,b\in\C$.
\item Let $z_0,\dots,z_{n-1}$ be the vertices of a closed polygon in $\C$, with signed area $A=\frac12\operatorname{Im}\sum_k\overline{z_k}z_{k+1}$. Show that
\[
\sum_k|z_{k+1}-z_k|^2\ge4\tan\frac\pi n\cdot A,
\]
with equality if and only if the polygon is a positively oriented regular $n$-gon (or a single point).
\item Deduce that among equilateral $n$-gons of given perimeter, the regular one has the largest area.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:involasym}
\emph{How many involutions?} Let $t_n$ be the number of involutions of $\{1,\dots,n\}$, so $\sum_nt_n\frac{z^n}{n!}=T(z)=e^{z+z^2/2}$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:involutions}). Let $r=r_n>0$ be the positive root of $r+r^2=n$, and set $b=r+2r^2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\frac{t_n}{n!}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi T(re^{i\theta})\,r^{-n}e^{-in\theta}\dd\theta$.
\item Show that $|T(re^{i\theta})|\le T(r)\exp\bigl(-2r\sin^2\frac\theta2-r^2\sin^2\theta\bigr)$.
\item Show that
\[
\frac{t_n}{n!}\sim\frac{T(r)}{r^n\sqrt{2\pi b}}.
\]
(Cut the integral at $|\theta|=r^{-4/5}$.)
\item Deduce that $t_n\sim\frac1{\sqrt2}\,n^{n/2}e^{-n/2+\sqrt n-1/4}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:distinctcycles}
\emph{All cycles of different lengths.} Let $d_n$ be the probability that a uniform random permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ has all its cycle lengths different.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\sum_{n\ge0}d_nz^n=\prod_{k\ge1}\bigl(1+\frac{z^k}k\bigr)$ for $|z|<1$, and compute $d_1,\dots,d_5$.
\item Show that this product equals $\frac{h(z)}{1-z}$, where $h(z)=\prod_{k\ge1}\bigl(1+\frac{z^k}k\bigr)e^{-z^k/k}$, and that $h(1)=e^{-\gamma}$.
\item For a power series $f=\sum a_jz^j$ write $\|f\|=\sum_j|a_j|$. Show that $\|fg\|\le\|f\|\,\|g\|$ and that $\bigl\|(1+\frac{z^k}k)e^{-z^k/k}-1\bigr\|\le\frac1{k^2}$.
Deduce that the coefficients $h_j$ of $h$ satisfy $\sum_j|h_j|<\infty$ and $\sum_jh_j=h(1)$.
\item Deduce that $d_n\to e^{-\gamma}=0.5614\ldots$
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:ramanujan}
\emph{Ramanujan's $\frac13$.} Let $Q(n)$ be as in Problem~\ref{pb:poissonise}, and let $R(n)=1+\frac n{n+1}+\frac{n^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}+\cdots$. Define $\theta_n$ by $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{n^k}{k!}+\theta_n\frac{n^n}{n!}=\frac{e^n}2$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\frac{n^n}{n!}Q(n)=\sum_{k<n}\frac{n^k}{k!}$ and $\frac{n^n}{n!}R(n)=\sum_{k\ge n}\frac{n^k}{k!}$. Deduce that $Q(n)+R(n)=\frac{n!\,e^n}{n^n}$ and $\theta_n=\frac{R(n)-Q(n)}2$.
\item Show that
\[
Q(n)=n\int_1^\infty s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}\dd s,\qq R(n)=n\int_0^1s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}\dd s.
\]
\item Let $\varphi(s)=s-1-\ln s$ and $v(s)=\operatorname{sign}(s-1)\sqrt{2\varphi(s)}$, an increasing bijection from $\left]0,\infty\right[$ onto $\R$; write $s(v)$ for its inverse.
Show that
\[
Q(n)=n\int_0^\infty e^{-nv^2/2}g(v)\dd v,\qq R(n)=n\int_{-\infty}^0e^{-nv^2/2}g(v)\dd v,\qq g(v)=\frac v{s(v)-1},
\]
and that $0<g(v)\le1+|v|$ and $g(v)=1-\frac v3+O(v^2)$, uniformly on $\R$.
\item Deduce that $Q(n)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}-\frac13+O\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt n}\bigr)$ and $R(n)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}+\frac13+O\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt n}\bigr)$, so that $\theta_n\to\frac13$.
\item Deduce that $e^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{n^k}{k!}=\frac12+\frac2{3\sqrt{2\pi n}}+O\bigl(\frac1n\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:mappings}
\emph{Random maps.} Draw a map $f$ from $\{1,\dots,n\}$ to itself uniformly. The \emph{functional graph} has an arrow $x\to f(x)$ for each $x$. Each connected component contains exactly one cycle, and $x$ is \emph{cyclic} when $f^k(x)=x$ for some $k\ge1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $x$ lies on a cycle of length exactly $k$ with probability $\frac{n^{\underline k}}{n^{k+1}}$. Deduce that the expected number of cyclic points is
$Q(n)\sim\sqrt{\pi n/2}$.
\item Show that the expected number of components is $C_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{n^{\underline k}}{k\,n^k}$.
\item Show that $C_n=\frac12\bigl(\ln(2n)+\gamma\bigr)+o(1)$. (For $n=100$, $C_n=2.979$ and the approximation is $2.938$.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:xplusinv}
\emph{Adding the reciprocal.} Set $x_0=1$ and $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac1{x_n}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $x_n^2\ge2n+1$, and that $x_n^2\le2n+2+\ln n$ for $n\ge1$.
\item Show that $x_n^2=2n+\frac12\ln n+C+o(1)$ for a constant $C$. (Its value is $C=1.72314\ldots$)
\item Deduce that $x_n=\sqrt{2n}+\frac{\ln n+2C}{4\sqrt{2n}}+o\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt n}\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:hyperfactorial}
\emph{The hyperfactorial and Glaisher's constant.} Let $H(n)=1^1\,2^2\,3^3\cdots n^n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Differentiate the identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:zetaem} in the variable $s$, then set $s=-1$. Show that
\[
\ln H(n)=\Bigl(\frac{n^2}2+\frac n2+\frac1{12}\Bigr)\ln n-\frac{n^2}4+\ln A+\frac1{720n^2}+O\Bigl(\frac1{n^4}\Bigr),
\]
where $\ln A=\frac1{12}-\zeta'(-1)$.
\item The constant is $A=1.2824271291\ldots$, Glaisher's constant. Check (a) at $n=10$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:banach}
\emph{Banach's matchboxes.} A person carries two boxes of $N$ matches, one in each pocket, and each time draws a match from a pocket chosen at random. At the first moment one box is empty, let $R$ be the number of matches left in the other.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $P(R=r)=\binom{2N-r}N2^{-(2N-r)}$ for $0\le r\le N$.
\item Writing $a_r=P(R=r)$, show that $(2N-r)a_{r+1}=2(N-r)a_r$, and deduce that $\mathbb E[R]=(2N+1)\binom{2N}N4^{-N}-1$.
\item Deduce that $\mathbb E[R]=2\sqrt{N/\pi}-1+O\bigl(\frac1{\sqrt N}\bigr)$. With $50$ matches per box, how many are left on average?
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:primezeta}
\emph{Computing $\sum_p\frac1{p^2}$ to twelve digits.} For real $s>1$, write $P(s)=\sum_pp^{-s}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $\log\zeta(s)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{P(ks)}k$.
\item Show that $P(s)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mu(k)}k\log\zeta(ks)$, the double series converging absolutely.
\item Show that if the sum in (b) is stopped at $k=K$, the error is at most $\frac{4}{K}2^{-(K+1)s}$ for $s\ge2$.
\item Summing the primes up to $10^7$ gives $\sum_{p\le10^7}p^{-2}=0.4522474141\ldots$, wrong in the eighth decimal. With (b) and $K=20$ one gets
$P(2)=0.452247420041\ldots$, correct to twelve digits. Estimate the tail $\sum_{p>10^7}p^{-2}$ and check the two numbers against each other.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star\star$]\label{pb:dickmanint}
\emph{Dickman's function integrates to $e^\gamma$.} \emph{Dickman's function} $\rho$ is the continuous function on $[0,\infty[$ with $\rho(u)=1$ for $0\le u\le1$ and $u\rho'(u)=-\rho(u-1)$ for $u>1$. Solve the delay equation on $[1,2]$, then on $[2,3]$, and so on. A positive integer is \emph{$y$-smooth} when every prime factor is at most $y$, and $\Psi(t,y)$ counts the $y$-smooth integers $n\le t$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $u\rho(u)=\int_{u-1}^u\rho(t)\dd t$ for $u\ge1$. Deduce that $\rho>0$, that $\rho$ decreases on $[1,\infty[$, and that $\rho(u)\le\frac12\rho(u-1)$ for $u\ge2$.
\item Let $\hat\rho(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-su}\rho(u)\dd u$ for $s\ge0$. Extending $\rho$ by $0$ on $]-\infty,0[$, show that $(u\rho(u))'=\rho(u)-\rho(u-1)$ for $u>0$, $u\ne1$, and deduce
that $s\hat\rho'(s)=-(1-e^{-s})\hat\rho(s)$ for $s>0$.
\item Deduce that $\hat\rho(s)=\hat\rho(0)\exp\bigl(-\int_0^s\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t\bigr)$.
\item Show that $s\hat\rho(s)\to1$ as $s\to\infty$, and that $\int_0^s\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t=\log s+\gamma+o(1)$. Conclude that $\int_0^\infty\rho(u)\dd u=e^\gamma=1.78107\ldots$
\item Admit Mertens's third theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~1): $\sum\frac1n$ over the $y$-smooth $n$ equals $\prod_{p\le y}(1-\frac1p)^{-1}\sim e^\gamma\log y$. Assuming that $\Psi(t,y)\approx t\rho(\frac{\log t}{\log y})$,
show that this is exactly what (d) predicts.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:polymertens}
\emph{Mertens's theorem for polynomials.} Let $N_k$ be the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $k$ over $\mathbb F_q$, so $\sum_{k\mid m}kN_k=q^m$ (Volume~3, Chapter~9). Set $E_n=\prod_{k\le n}(1-q^{-k})^{N_k}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that $-\log E_n=H_n+\varepsilon_n$, where $H_n=\sum_{m\le n}\frac1m$ and $0\le\varepsilon_n\le4(1+\log n)q^{-n/2}$. Deduce that $E_n\sim\frac{e^{-\gamma}}n$.
\item A monic polynomial of degree $n$ with no irreducible factor of degree at most $\frac n2$ is irreducible, and the probability of that is $\frac1n+O(\frac{q^{-n/2}}n)$
(Problem~\ref{pb:randpoly}). Independent sieving would predict $E_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$. Show that the prediction exceeds the truth by a factor tending to $2e^{-\gamma}=1.1229\ldots$, the same factor as for the integers.
\item Show that $ne^\gamma E_n=1-\frac1{2n}+O(\frac1{n^2})$ for fixed $q$. For $q=5$ and $n=10$ the exact value is $ne^\gamma E_n=0.952015$. Compare.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{solution}{ex:eulerbounds}
\idea The perimeter averages $\sqrt{a^2\cos^2+b^2\sin^2}$. Compare that square root with the arithmetic mean $a\cos^2\theta+b\sin^2\theta$. Cauchy--Schwarz gives the other bound.
\writeup Write $c=\cos^2\theta$ and $s=\sin^2\theta$, so $c+s=1$. Convexity of $x\mapsto x^2$, with weights $c$ and $s$, gives $(ac+bs)^2\le a^2c+b^2s$. Hence
$\sqrt{a^2c+b^2s}\ge ac+bs$. Integrate over $[0,\frac\pi2]$, where $\int c=\int s=\frac\pi4$. You get $J\ge\frac{\pi(a+b)}4$. Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$J\le\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\sqrt{\int(a^2c+b^2s)}=\sqrt{\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{\pi(a^2+b^2)}4}$. Multiply by $4$. Equality in the first bound needs $ac+bs$ to be the quadratic mean for every
$\theta$, which forces $a=b$. Equality in the second needs a constant integrand, which again forces $a=b$. Figure~\ref{fig:perimeter} draws both bounds. The lower one is
much closer at moderate eccentricity.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:pendulum}
\idea Conserve energy, and the period becomes $\int\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\dot\theta}$. A trigonometric substitution turns that integral into $K(k)$. Gauss's theorem
then writes $K$ as an AGM.
\writeup Multiply the equation by $\dot\theta$ and integrate. You get $\dot\theta^2=\frac{2g}\ell(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)=\frac{4g}\ell\bigl(k^2-\sin^2\frac\theta2\bigr)$ with $k=\sin\frac{\theta_0}2$.
Symmetry says the period is four times the travel from $\theta=0$ to $\theta_0$. So $T=4\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\dot\theta}=2\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2(\theta/2)}}$.
Set $\sin\frac\theta2=k\sin\varphi$, with $\varphi\in[0,\frac\pi2]$. Then $\frac12\cos\frac\theta2\dd\theta=k\cos\varphi\dd\varphi$, $\sqrt{k^2-\sin^2\frac\theta2}=k\cos\varphi$ and
$\cos\frac\theta2=\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\varphi}$, so
\[
T=4\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\varphi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\varphi}}=4\sqrt{\frac\ell g}K(k).
\]
Take $k'=\cos\frac{\theta_0}2$. The identity $K(k)=\frac\pi{2M(1,k')}$ is the formula in the exercise. At $\theta_0=\frac\pi2$, $M(1,\cos\frac\pi4)=M(1,0.7071)=0.8472\ldots$, so the period is $18.0\%$ longer.
(At $10^\circ$ the stretch is $0.19\%$. At $179^\circ$ the period is almost four times the small-oscillation period.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:agmlog}
\idea When $x$ is small, $\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}$ tracks $\frac1t$ except near $0$, where the denominator stops shrinking at $t\approx x$. That cutoff produces $\ln\frac1x$. The constants are
exact antiderivatives.
\writeup Theorem~\ref{thm:gaussagm} and Step~1 of its proof give $\frac\pi{2M(1,x)}=I(1,x)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{(1+t^2)(x^2+t^2)}}$. Cut the integral at $t=1$.

On $[1,\infty[$, the integrand tends to $\frac1{t\sqrt{1+t^2}}$ as $x\to0$, uniformly, and it is bounded by that limit. Dominated convergence (Corollary~\ref{cor:dct}) sends this piece to
$\int_1^\infty\frac{\mathrm dt}{t\sqrt{1+t^2}}=\operatorname{arsinh}1=\ln(1+\sqrt2)$. The antiderivative is $-\operatorname{arsinh}\frac1t$.

On $[0,1]$, split the integrand as $\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}+\frac{g(t)}{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}$ with $g(t)=\frac1{\sqrt{1+t^2}}-1$. First, $\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dt}{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}=\operatorname{arsinh}\frac1x
=\ln\frac{1+\sqrt{1+x^2}}x=\ln\frac2x+o(1)$. Second, $|g(t)|\le\frac{t^2}2$, so $\bigl|\frac{g(t)}{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}\bigr|\le\frac t2$. Corollary~\ref{cor:dct} applies: the convergence is uniform, and the difference is at most $\frac x2$. Thus
$\int_0^1\frac{g(t)}{\sqrt{x^2+t^2}}\dd t\to\int_0^1\frac{g(t)}t\dd t$. That integral equals $\bigl[-\operatorname{arsinh}\frac1t-\ln t\bigr]_0^1=\bigl[-\ln\bigl(1+\sqrt{1+t^2}\bigr)\bigr]_0^1=\ln2-\ln(1+\sqrt2)$.

Add the three limits. Then $I(1,x)=\ln\frac2x+\ln2-\ln(1+\sqrt2)+\ln(1+\sqrt2)+o(1)=\ln\frac4x+o(1)$. The error $o(1)$ is $6.8\cdot10^{-3}$ at $x=0.1$, $1.25\cdot10^{-4}$
at $x=0.01$ and $3.6\cdot10^{-12}$ at $x=10^{-6}$, in line with the known bound $O(x^2\ln\frac1x)$ (Borwein and Borwein, Chapter~7). The choice $x=2^{-m}$ gives $(m+2)\ln2\approx\frac\pi{2M(1,2^{-m})}$, with an error of order $m4^{-m}$. Feed in $\pi$ from Brent--Salamin, and this computes $\ln2$ to about $0.6m$ digits.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:cyclewalk}
\idea After $n$ steps the token sits at $S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n$, a sum of independent $\pm1$, read modulo $k$. The roots of unity filter picks out
$S_n\equiv0$. Independence factors the expectation of $\omega^{jS_n}$.

\writeup (a) Let $\omega=e^{2i\pi/k}$. For an integer $s$, the average $\frac1k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^{js}$ equals $1$ when $k\mid s$ and equals $0$
otherwise (Volume~1, Chapter~5). Therefore
\[
q_n=\mathbb E\Bigl[\frac1k\sum_j\omega^{jS_n}\Bigr]=\frac1k\sum_j\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbb E\bigl[\omega^{jX_i}\bigr]
=\frac1k\sum_j\Bigl(\frac{\omega^j+\omega^{-j}}2\Bigr)^n=\frac1k\sum_j\cos^n\frac{2\pi j}k,
\]
by independence (Volume~4, Chapter~2).

(b) When $k=3$ the cosines are $1,-\frac12,-\frac12$, so $q_n=\frac13\bigl(1+2(-\frac12)^n\bigr)$. When $k=4$ they are
$1,0,-1,0$, so $q_n=\frac14\bigl(1+(-1)^n\bigr)$ for $n\ge1$ (and $q_0=1$). When $k=6$ they are $1,\frac12,-\frac12,-1,-\frac12,
\frac12$, so $q_n=\frac16\bigl(1+2\cdot2^{-n}+2(-\frac12)^n+(-1)^n\bigr)$.

(c) The cosine $\cos\frac{2\pi j}k$ equals $\pm1$ only at $j=0$, and also at $j=\frac k2$ when $k$ is even. Every other term tends to $0$. Thus $q_n\to\frac1k$
when $k$ is odd. When $k$ is even, $q_n\to\frac2k$ along even $n$, and $q_n=0$ for odd $n$. An even polygon is bipartite.
\tools Volume~1, Chapter~5 (roots of unity filter); Volume~4, Chapter~2 (independence).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:detsquare}
\idea A determinant is a signed sum of $n!$ products. The square of that sum is a sum over pairs of products. Expectation kills every
pair in which some entry appears to an odd power. The $n!$ pairs with $\sigma=\tau$ each contribute $1$. A random
variable whose mean square is $n!$ must sometimes have absolute value at least $\sqrt{n!}$.

\writeup (a) Write $\det A=\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_ia_{i\sigma(i)}$. Each product has mean $0$: the factors are
independent and each has mean $0$. Next,
\[
(\det A)^2=\sum_{\sigma,\tau}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau)\prod_ia_{i\sigma(i)}a_{i\tau(i)}.
\]
When $\sigma=\tau$ the product is $\prod_ia_{i\sigma(i)}^2=1$. When $\sigma\ne\tau$, choose $i$ with $\sigma(i)\ne\tau(i)$. The entry
$a_{i\sigma(i)}$ appears once: row $i$ contributes $a_{i\sigma(i)}a_{i\tau(i)}$, and every other row
contributes entries from that row. The product is therefore $a_{i\sigma(i)}$ times an independent factor, and the mean is $0$. Hence
$\mathbb E[(\det A)^2]=n!$ (Volume~4, Chapter~2).

(b) If every $\pm1$ matrix satisfied $|\det A|<\sqrt{n!}$, then $\mathbb E[(\det A)^2]<n!$.

(c) Let $r_1,\dots,r_n$ be the rows. Dependent rows give $\det A=0$. Otherwise Gram--Schmidt produces
$r_i^*=r_i-\sum_{j<i}c_{ij}r_j^*$ with $r_1^*,\dots,r_n^*$ orthogonal (Volume~1, Chapter~7). Subtracting a combination
of earlier rows from a later row leaves the determinant unchanged, so $\det A=\det A^*$, where $A^*$ has rows $r_i^*$. Then
$(\det A^*)^2=\det(A^*A^{*\mathsf T})=\prod_i\|r_i^*\|^2$. The vector $r_i^*$ is the projection of $r_i$ onto the orthogonal complement
of the earlier rows, so $\|r_i^*\|\le\|r_i\|\le\sqrt n$. Therefore $|\det A|\le n^{n/2}$. Stirling gives $\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{n^{n/2}}\sim
(2\pi n)^{1/4}e^{-n/2}$. A typical random determinant is exponentially smaller than the maximum.
\tools Volume~4, Chapter~2 (expectation, independence); Volume~1, Chapter~7 (determinants, Gram--Schmidt) and Chapter~8 (Stirling's formula, with the constant $\sqrt{2\pi}$).
\leads Tao and Vu (2006) proved that $|\det A|=\sqrt{n!}\,e^{O(\sqrt{n\log n})}$ with probability tending to $1$. Nguyen and
Vu (2014) proved that $\log|\det A|$ is asymptotically normal. Equality in Hadamard's bound is Problem~\ref{pb:hadamard}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:hadamard}
\idea A Hadamard matrix is $n$ pairwise orthogonal vectors with entries $\pm1$. Two such vectors agree
in exactly half their coordinates, and three rows force the columns into quarters. A construction needs $\pm1$
vectors that are nearly uncorrelated. Quadratic residues modulo $p$ are the classical source: shift the Legendre
symbol by $d$ and it stays almost orthogonal to itself (Jacobsthal's sum). A border of ones, and a diagonal, repair the ``almost''.

\writeup (a) Multiplying a column by $-1$ preserves the Hadamard property, because $HH^{\mathsf T}=\sum_c(\text{column }c)(\text{column
}c)^{\mathsf T}$. So row $1$ may be taken to be all ones. Let $n\ge3$, and let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ count the
columns in which rows $2$ and $3$ carry $(+,+)$, $(+,-)$, $(-,+)$, $(-,-)$. Orthogonality of rows $1,2,3$ gives
\[
\alpha+\beta-\gamma-\delta=0,\qquad\alpha-\beta+\gamma-\delta=0,\qquad\alpha-\beta-\gamma+\delta=0,
\]
together with $\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=n$. Add the four equations: $4\alpha=n$. The same argument gives $\beta=\gamma=\delta=\frac n4$. So $4\mid n$.

(b) Block multiplication gives
\[
\begin{pmatrix}H&H\\H&-H\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}H^{\mathsf T}&H^{\mathsf T}\\H^{\mathsf T}&-H^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}2HH^{\mathsf T}&0\\0&2HH^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}=2nI_{2n}.
\]

(c) Since $p\equiv3\pmod4$, one has $\chi(-1)=-1$ (Volume~2, Chapter~6), so $Q_{ba}=\chi(a-b)=-\chi(b-a)=-Q_{ab}$. Each row sums to
$\sum_b\chi(b-a)=\sum_c\chi(c)=0$, because residues and non-residues are equally numerous. On the diagonal of $QQ^{\mathsf T}$,
$\sum_c\chi(c-a)^2=p-1$. Off the diagonal, set $d=a-b\ne0$ and replace $c$ by $c+b$:
\[
(QQ^{\mathsf T})_{ab}=\sum_c\chi(c-a)\chi(c-b)=\sum_c\chi(c)\chi(c+d).
\]
For $c\ne0$, $\chi(c)\chi(c+d)=\chi(c^2)\chi(1+dc^{-1})=\chi(1+dc^{-1})$. As $c$ runs through $\mathbb F_p^*$, the value $1+dc^{-1}$
runs through $\mathbb F_p\setminus\{1\}$. The sum is $\sum_{u\ne1}\chi(u)=-\chi(1)=-1$. Hence $QQ^{\mathsf T}=pI-J$.

Now set $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\\-\mathbf1&Q\end{pmatrix}$, of order $p+1$, and $H=I+S$. The diagonal of
$Q$ is $\chi(0)=0$, so the entries of $H$ are $\pm1$. Also $S^{\mathsf T}=-S$, and
\[
SS^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\\-\mathbf1&Q\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}\\
\mathbf1&Q^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}p&(Q\mathbf1)^{\mathsf T}\\Q\mathbf1&J+QQ^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}=pI_{p+1},
\]
because $Q\mathbf1=0$. Therefore $HH^{\mathsf T}=(I+S)(I-S)=I-S^2=I+SS^{\mathsf T}=(p+1)I_{p+1}$.

(d) Paley's construction gives the orders $4$, $8$, $12$, $20$, $24$, $32$, $44$, $48$, $60$ (from $p=3,7,11,19,23,31,43,47,59$). Sylvester's doubling
of $1$ gives $1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, $16$, $32$, $64$. Doubling the Paley orders gives $24,40,48,64$, and then $48$ again. Up to $64$ one obtains
every multiple of $4$ except $28$, $36$, $52$ and $56$. (Those four exist as well, by other constructions of Paley and of Williamson.)
\tools Volume~2, Chapter~6 (Legendre symbol, $\chi(-1)$, residue counts); Volume~1, Chapter~7 (block matrices); Volume~3,
Chapter~9 (the field $\mathbb F_p$).
\leads The \emph{Hadamard conjecture} asserts a Hadamard matrix for every multiple of $4$. It is open. The
smallest unknown order is $668$ (the order $428$ was found by Kharaghani and Tayfeh-Rezaie in 2005). In computer science the
Sylvester matrices are the Walsh--Hadamard transform, and their rows form the Hadamard code.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:rank}
\idea The element $g$ fixes a pair $(x,y)$ exactly when it fixes both points. So $F^2$ counts fixed points of $g$ on $X\times X$, and
Burnside turns the mean of $F^2$ into a count of orbits. The diagonal is one orbit. There is at least one more.

\writeup (a) Burnside says $\mathbb E[F]$ equals the number of orbits of $G$ on $X$, which is $1$. The fixed points of $g$ on $X\times X$
are the pairs of fixed points, and there are $F(g)^2$ of them, so $\mathbb E[F^2]=r$. The diagonal $\{(x,x)\}$ is an orbit, because $G$ is
transitive. Its complement is non-empty, since $n\ge2$. So $r\ge2$.

(b) One has $r=2$ precisely when the off-diagonal pairs form a single orbit, which is $2$-transitivity. And $\operatorname{Var}F=r-1$, so
the same condition is $\operatorname{Var}F=1$. The character $\pi$ is real-valued, so $\langle\pi,\pi\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum\pi(g)^2=r$ and
$\langle\pi,1\rangle=\mathbb E[F]=1$. Set $\psi=\pi-1$. Then $\langle\psi,1\rangle=0$ and $\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=r-2\langle\psi,1\rangle-1=r-1$.
A character $\psi=\sum m_i\chi_i$ satisfies $\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\sum m_i^2$, so it is irreducible precisely when
$\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=1$ (Volume~3, Chapter~11), that is, when $r=2$.

(c) A rotation or a reflection of the $n$-gon preserves the distance between two vertices along the polygon. That distance takes the
values $0,1,\dots,\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor$. Conversely, if $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ lie at the same distance, a rotation carries $x$ to $x'$
and then carries $y$ either to $y'$ or to the mirror image of $y'$ across the axis through $x'$. The reflection in that axis finishes the job. So
the orbits on pairs are the distance classes, and $r=\lfloor\frac n2\rfloor+1$. For $n=3$ this rank is $2$: $D_3=\mathfrak S_3$ is
$2$-transitive on the triangle.
\tools Volume~3, Chapters~3 and~11 (Burnside, inner products of characters); Volume~4, Chapter~2 (variance).
\leads Problem~\ref{pb:fixmoments} is the case $G=\mathfrak S_n$, in which every moment is a Bell number. The higher moments
$\mathbb E[F^j]$ count orbits on $X^j$. A group is $j$-transitive exactly when those moments agree with the moments of $\mathfrak S_n$ through
order $j$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:derangements}
\idea From the previous problem, $\mathbb E[F]=1$ and $\mathbb E[F^2]=r\ge2$. Look for a quadratic in $F$ that is $\le0$ on $F\ge1$ and
positive at $F=0$. The product $(F-1)(F-n)$ does this, because $1\le F\le n$ whenever $F\ge1$. Its mean is $r-1\ge1$, and only the
derangements can supply that positive mean.

\writeup (a) Let $D$ be the set of $g$ with $F(g)=0$. If $g\notin D$, then $1\le F(g)\le n$, so $(F-1)(F-n)\le0$. If $g\in D$, the product equals $n$.
Hence
\[
n\,P(D)\ge\mathbb E[(F-1)(F-n)]=\mathbb E[F^2]-(n+1)\mathbb E[F]+n=r-1\ge1,
\]
by Problem~\ref{pb:rank}. Therefore $P(D)\ge\frac1n$.

(b) Equality requires $r=2$, and $(F-1)(F-n)=0$ for every $g\notin D$. That is, $F(g)\in\{0,1,n\}$ for every $g$. Faithfulness of the action
forces $F(g)=n$ only for $g=e$. So equality holds precisely when $G$ is $2$-transitive and every $g\ne e$ fixes at most one
point. In that case the stabiliser of two distinct points is trivial, so there is exactly one $g$ with $gx=x'$ and $gy=y'$. The group is sharply
$2$-transitive. Conversely, a sharply $2$-transitive group is $2$-transitive and has trivial two-point stabilisers, so every $g\ne e$
fixes at most one point.

Check $t\mapsto at+b$ on $\mathbb F_p$. Given $x\ne y$ and $x'\ne y'$, the system $ax+b=x'$, $ay+b=y'$ has the unique solution
$a=\frac{x'-y'}{x-y}\ne0$, $b=x'-ax$. The group has order $p(p-1)$ and is sharply $2$-transitive. Its derangements are the
$p-1$ translations $t\mapsto t+b$ with $b\ne0$, a proportion $\frac{p-1}{p(p-1)}=\frac1p$.

(c) Let $H<G$ be proper, and suppose $G=\bigcup_{g\in G}gHg^{-1}$. Then $G$ acts transitively on the
$n=[G:H]\ge2$ cosets $gH$. An element $x$ fixes the coset $gH$ if and only if $g^{-1}xg\in H$, that is $x\in gHg^{-1}$. Every element would then fix a
coset. The action need not be faithful. Part (a) applies to the faithful action of $G/K$, where $K$ is the kernel, and produces an element
with no fixed point. That is a contradiction.
\tools Volume~3, Chapter~3 (actions on cosets); Volume~4, Chapter~2 (expectations).
\leads Jordan's theorem (1872) has a famous arithmetic consequence. An irreducible polynomial of degree at least $2$ in
$\Z[X]$ has no root modulo $p$ for infinitely many primes $p$, a set of positive density by Chebotarev's theorem (see Serre's
article below). Fulman and Guralnick proved a conjecture of Boston and Shalev: for simple groups the proportion of
derangements is bounded below by an absolute constant.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:noroots}
\idea Evaluation at the $q$ points of $\mathbb F_q$ is a linear map. It is onto once the degree is high enough for interpolation. The roots then
behave like $q$ independent coins of bias $\frac1q$, exactly when $n\ge q$. When $n<q$ they match the first terms of inclusion--exclusion.

\writeup Write $f=x^n+g$, with $g$ uniform among polynomials of degree $<n$. Take a set $E\subset\mathbb F_q$ with $|E|\le n$. The linear map
$g\mapsto(g(a))_{a\in E}$ is onto, by Lagrange interpolation with polynomials of degree $<|E|$. It therefore hits every value equally often.
The same is true of $f\mapsto(f(a))_{a\in E}$, which differs from the map on $g$ by a translation.

(a) If $n\ge q$, take $E=\mathbb F_q$. Then $(f(a))_a$ is uniform on $\mathbb F_q^q$. The coordinates are independent and uniform. The events
$f(a)=0$ are independent with probability $\frac1q$, and $R\sim\mathrm{Bin}(q,\frac1q)$. Hence $P(R=0)=(1-\frac1q)^q$.

(b) Inclusion--exclusion gives
\[
P(R=0)=\sum_{E\subset\mathbb F_q}(-1)^{|E|}P(f=0\text{ on }E).
\]
When $|E|\le n$ this probability is $q^{-|E|}$, by the
remark above. When $|E|>n$ it is $0$: a monic polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots. So
$P(R=0)=\sum_{k\le n}(-1)^k\binom qkq^{-k}$. And
\[
\binom qkq^{-k}=\frac1{k!}\prod_{i<k}\Bigl(1-\frac iq\Bigr)\longrightarrow\frac1{k!},
\]
so as $q\to\infty$ the probability tends to $\sum_{k\le n}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}$, the derangement probability. A random polynomial has no root as often as a random
permutation has no fixed point. That agrees with Problem~\ref{pb:randpoly}(c): a root is a factor of degree $1$.

\tools Volume~1, Chapter~6 (Lagrange interpolation); Volume~1, Proposition~2.3 (derangements); Volume~4, Chapter~1 (inclusion--exclusion for probabilities); Volume~4, Chapter~2 (the binomial law);
Chapter~\ref{ch:generating} (Problem~\ref{pb:randpoly}, for the comparison only).
\leads For $n\ge q$ the probability does not depend on $n$. Over $\Z$ the analogue is the number of roots modulo $p$ of a random
polynomial. For one fixed polynomial, the Chebotarev density theorem gives the law of the number of roots modulo a random prime.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:gaussmeasure}
\idea The preimage of $[0,t[$ is a union of intervals, one for each value of the first partial quotient. Their Gauss measures
telescope back to $\log_2(1+t)$.

\writeup (a) The condition $Tx<t$ with $\lfloor1/x\rfloor=k$ means $k\le\frac1x<k+t$, that is $x\in\,]\frac1{k+t},\frac1k]$. So
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(T^{-1}[0,t[)&=\frac1{\log2}\sum_{k\ge1}\Bigl(\log\Bigl(1+\frac1k\Bigr)-\log\Bigl(1+\frac1{k+t}\Bigr)\Bigr)\\
&=\frac1{\log2}\sum_{k\ge1}\Bigl(\log\frac{k+1}k-\log\frac{k+1+t}{k+t}\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
The partial sum up to $K$ equals $\log(K+1)-\log\frac{K+1+t}{1+t}$, which tends to $\log(1+t)$. The value is therefore $\mu([0,t[)$. Two probability measures that
agree on every interval $[0,t[$ agree on every Borel set, so $\mu\circ T^{-1}=\mu$.

(b) One has $a_n(x)=a_1(T^{n-1}x)$, and $T^{n-1}x$ has law $\mu$ by (a) and induction. So
$P(a_n=k)=\mu(]\frac1{k+1},\frac1k])=\log_2\frac{1+1/k}{1+1/(k+1)}=\log_2\frac{(k+1)^2}{k(k+2)}$.

(c) Here $P(a_1=k)=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}=\frac1{k(k+1)}$. For $k=1$ the uniform law gives $0.5$, and the Gauss measure gives $\log_2\frac43=0.415$.

\tools Volume~2, Chapter~9 (continued fractions); Volume~4, Chapter~5 (laws determined by distribution functions).
\leads Under both measures $\mathbb E[a_1]=\infty$, because the probabilities decay like $\frac1{k^2}$. Khinchin proved that nevertheless
$(a_1\cdots a_n)^{1/n}\to2.6854\ldots$ for almost every $x$. The proof uses the ergodic theorem, which this series has not proved yet. Exercise~\ref{pb:gausskuzmin} of Chapter~\ref{ch:zeta} shows how fast a uniform $x$ forgets its law and takes up $\mu$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:digitsclt}
\idea When $N=2^k$, the bits of a uniform $n$ are independent fair coins. A general $N$ is a union of a few dyadic blocks. Each block is a shifted
copy of the power-of-two case, and almost all the mass sits in the large blocks.

\writeup (a) The map sending $n$ to its $k$ bits is a bijection from $\{0,\dots,2^k-1\}$ to $\{0,1\}^k$. The bits are therefore independent and fair, and
$s(n)$ is their sum.

(b) Write $N=2^{k_1}+\dots+2^{k_r}$ with $k_1>\dots>k_r$, and $M_i=2^{k_1}+\dots+2^{k_{i-1}}$. The interval $[0,N[$ is the disjoint union of
the blocks $B_i=[M_i,M_i+2^{k_i}[$. For $n\in B_i$, $s(n)=(i-1)+s(n-M_i)$, and $n-M_i$ is uniform on $[0,2^{k_i}[$ when $n$ is uniform
on $B_i$. The law of $s(n)$ is therefore the mixture, with weights $w_i=2^{k_i}/N$, of the laws of $(i-1)+\mathrm{Bin}(k_i,\frac12)$.

Let $k=k_1$, so $\log_2N\in[k,k+1[$, and fix $h\ge1$. The blocks with $i>h$ have total weight at most $2^{k-h+1}/N\le2^{1-h}$. For
$i\le h$, $k_i\in[k-h,k]$ and the shift is at most $h$; by the de Moivre--Laplace theorem (Volume~4, Chapter~6),
$\bigl((i-1)+\mathrm{Bin}(k_i,\frac12)-\frac12\log_2N\bigr)/\sqrt{\frac14\log_2N}\to N(0,1)$ in law, uniformly in $i\le h$. The added constant is at most $h$, and the standard deviation is $\frac12\sqrt{k_i}$, so the shift moves the normalised variable by at most $2h/\sqrt k$. For each fixed $h$ that shift tends to $0$, and the change of variance is $O(h/k)$. Hence for every $x$, the distribution
function of the normalised $s(n)$ at $x$ is within $2^{1-h}+o(1)$ of $\Phi(x)$. Let $N\to\infty$, then $h\to\infty$.

\tools Volume~4, Chapter~6 (central limit theorem); Volume~4, Chapter~2 (binomial law).
\leads The mean is $\frac1N\sum_{n<N}s(n)=\frac12\log_2N+F(\log_2N)$, where $F$ is continuous, periodic, and nowhere differentiable
(Trollope 1968, Delange 1975). The blocks make the mean oscillate. Gelfond (1968) proved that $s(n)\bmod2$ is equidistributed in
arithmetic progressions. Mauduit and Rivat (2010) proved that it is equidistributed along the primes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:kolberg}
\idea If the parity of $p(n)$ settled down, the partition series would be rational over $\mathbb F_2$, and so would the pentagonal series. The pentagonal series has gaps that grow without bound. A rational series over a finite field does not.

\writeup Work in $\mathbb F_2[[x]]$ and set $\bar P=\sum_n(p(n)\bmod2)x^n$. If $p(n)$ is even for every $n\ge N$, then $\bar P$ is a polynomial.
If $p(n)$ is odd for every $n\ge N$, then $\bar P$ is a polynomial plus $\frac{x^N}{1-x}$. Either way $\bar P=\frac UV$ for polynomials
$U,V$ with $U(0)=V(0)=1$, because $p(0)=1$. From $P(x)E(x)=1$ in $\Z[[x]]$, the reduction $\bar E$ of $E$ equals $\frac VU$, so it is rational as well.

Take a rational series $\frac VU=\sum e_nx^n$ over $\mathbb F_2$ with $U=1+u_1x+\dots+u_dx^d$. Then $e_n=u_1e_{n-1}+\dots+u_de_{n-d}$ for
$n>\max(d,\deg V)$. The vector $(e_{n-1},\dots,e_{n-d})$ has at most $2^d$ possible values, so a value repeats. From that index on, the sequence
is periodic. An eventually periodic sequence with infinitely many non-zero terms has bounded gaps between those terms.

The pentagonal number theorem says $e_n\ne0$ exactly when $n=\frac{j(3j\mp1)}2$ for some $j\ge0$. The gap between
$\frac{j(3j+1)}2$ and $\frac{(j+1)(3j+2)}2$ is $2j+1$, and that gap is unbounded. Neither parity can hold from some index on.

\tools Theorem~\ref{thm:pentagonal} (pentagonal number theorem); Theorem~\ref{thm:rational} (rational series and recurrences).
\leads The argument gives no count. Nicolas, Ruzsa and Sárközy (1998) and Ahlgren (1999) showed that each
parity occurs, up to logarithmic factors, at least $\sqrt N$ times below $N$. That is still far from the expected $\frac N2$. Every arithmetic
progression contains infinitely many $n$ with $p(n)$ even (Ono, 1996) and infinitely many with $p(n)$ odd (Radu, 2012).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:kummer}
\idea Legendre's formula reads the power of $p$ in $n!$ off the digit sum. Each carry in base $p$ costs exactly $p-1$ in that sum.

\writeup (a) \emph{Legendre's formula.} Among $1,\dots,n$ there are $\lfloor n/p^i\rfloor$ multiples of $p^i$. An integer with $v_p=v$ is counted once for each
$i=1,\dots,v$, so $v_p(n!)=\sum_{i\ge1}\lfloor n/p^i\rfloor$. Write $n=\sum_jn_jp^j$ in base $p$. Then $\lfloor n/p^i\rfloor=\sum_{j\ge i}n_jp^{j-i}$. Summing over $i\ge1$ gives
$\sum_jn_j(1+p+\dots+p^{j-1})=\sum_jn_j\frac{p^j-1}{p-1}=\frac{n-s_p(n)}{p-1}$, where $s_p(n)=\sum_jn_j$. Hence
\[
v_p\binom{m+n}m=\frac{s_p(m)+s_p(n)-s_p(m+n)}{p-1}.
\]
Add $m$ and $n$ one digit at a time. In a position with incoming carry $c_{i-1}\in\{0,1\}$, the digit of the sum is
$m_i+n_i+c_{i-1}-pc_i$, where $c_i$ is the outgoing carry. Sum over $i$, taking $c_{-1}=0$ and allowing no carry past the last digit. Then
$s_p(m+n)=s_p(m)+s_p(n)+\sum_ic_{i-1}-p\sum_ic_i=s_p(m)+s_p(n)-(p-1)\sum_ic_i$. So $v_p\binom{m+n}m=\sum_ic_i$.

(b) Add $k$ and $n-k$. If $n$ has $L+1$ digits ($p^L\le n<p^{L+1}$), a carry out of position $L$ would make the sum at least
$p^{L+1}$. Carries can leave only the positions $0,\dots,L-1$, so there are at most $L$ of them. Hence $p^{v_p\binom nk}\le p^L\le n$.

(c) By (b), for each prime $p$ the power $p^{v_p\binom nk}$ is at most $n$. It therefore divides the highest power of $p$ that is $\le n$,
and that power is the power of $p$ in $\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n)$. So $\binom nk$ divides the lcm. Among the $n+1$ numbers
$\binom nk$, the largest is at least their average $\frac{2^n}{n+1}$.

\tools Volume~2, Chapters~1 and~2 (valuations, lcm and prime powers). Legendre's formula is proved in Volume~2, Chapter~1.
\leads Part (c) is where Chebyshev-type estimates begin. Since $\log\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,n)=\psi(n)$, the bound gives
$\psi(n)\ge n\log2-\log(n+1)$. Volume~6, Chapter~1 takes the argument on from there. Kummer found (a) in 1852, in a long paper on reciprocity laws.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:pascalfour}
\idea An entry is $2\bmod4$ when $2$ divides it exactly once, which means the addition has exactly one carry. A single carry can sit
only where $n$ has the block $10$.

\writeup (a) Problem~\ref{pb:kummer} says $\binom nk\equiv2\pmod4$ if and only if adding $k$ and $m=n-k$ in binary produces exactly one carry,
say out of position $i$. Position $i$ has no incoming carry, and $k_i+m_i=n_i+2$, so $k_i=m_i=1$ and $n_i=0$. Position
$i+1$ has an incoming carry and no outgoing carry, so $k_{i+1}+m_{i+1}+1=n_{i+1}\le1$. That forces $k_{i+1}=m_{i+1}=0$ and
$n_{i+1}=1$. Every other position has no carry, so $k_j+m_j=n_j$. Where $n_j=0$ both digits are $0$. Where $n_j=1$
either $k_j$ or $m_j$ may be the digit $1$. The integers $k$ with exactly one carry are therefore a choice of one block $10$ in $n$ (positions
$i+1$ and $i$) together with a free choice at each of the other $s(n)-1$ positions where $n$ has a digit $1$. There are $B(n)2^{s(n)-1}$ of them.

(b) The rows with $B(n)=0$ are the binary expansions with no $1$ immediately followed by a $0$, that is $n=11\cdots1_2=2^a-1$, together with $n=0$.

\tools Problem~\ref{pb:kummer}.
\leads The same kind of rule describes $\binom nk$ modulo any prime power, in terms of blocks of digits. Granville's survey, listed
below, explains the rules, and explains how the counts become the ``fractal'' pictures of Figure~\ref{fig:pascal3}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:centralp}
\idea The series satisfies $f^2(1-4x)=1$. In characteristic $p$, the map $f\mapsto f^p$ is the substitution $x\mapsto x^p$. That turns the equation into a rule that reads the coefficients one digit at a time.

\writeup (a) If $g$ is a power series with integer coefficients, then $g(x)^p\equiv g(x^p)\pmod p$. The multinomial coefficients in the
$p$-th power are divisible by $p$ except the pure powers, and $a^p\equiv a$. Since $f^2=\frac1{1-4x}$,
\[
(1-4x)^{\frac{p-1}2}f(x^p)\equiv(1-4x)^{\frac{p-1}2}f(x)^p=(1-4x)^{\frac{p-1}2}f(x)\bigl(f(x)^2\bigr)^{\frac{p-1}2}=f(x).
\]

(b) Let
\[
Q(x)=(1-4x)^{(p-1)/2}=\sum_{j\le(p-1)/2}\binom{(p-1)/2}j(-4)^jx^j.
\]
Now $\frac{p-1}2\equiv-\frac12\pmod p$, and
$\binom{-1/2}j(-4)^j=\binom{2j}j$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:binomcoef} with $\alpha=\frac12$: both are the coefficient of $x^j$ in $(1-4x)^{-1/2}$). So the coefficient of $x^j$ in $Q$ is $\equiv\binom{2j}j$ for $j\le\frac{p-1}2$. For
$\frac{p-1}2<j<p$, the binomial $\binom{2j}j=\frac{(2j)!}{j!^2}$ is divisible by $p$, because $p\le2j<2p$. Thus $Q(x)\equiv\sum_{j<p}\binom{2j}jx^j$. Write
$n=n_0+pn'$ with $0\le n_0<p$. The coefficient of $x^n$ in $Q(x)f(x^p)$ is $\binom{2n_0}{n_0}\binom{2n'}{n'}$ modulo $p$. Only the term
$x^{n_0}$ of $Q$ can meet a power of $x^p$, since $\deg Q<p$. Induction on the number of digits gives the product
formula.

(c) A digit $n_i\le\frac{p-1}2$ gives $\binom{2n_i}{n_i}=\frac{(2n_i)!}{n_i!^2}$ with $2n_i<p$, so $p$ does not divide it. A digit $n_i>\frac{p-1}2$ gives a
multiple of $p$, as in (b). Below $p^k$ the good $n$ number $\bigl(\frac{p+1}2\bigr)^k$ out of $p^k$. The proportion
$\bigl(\frac{p+1}{2p}\bigr)^k$ tends to $0$.

\tools Proposition~\ref{prop:binomcoef} (the series of $(1-4x)^{-1/2}$); Volume~2, Chapter~3 (Fermat's little theorem).
\leads The same device works for any power series $f$ with $f^d\in\Q(x)$, and more generally for diagonals of rational functions.
It produces ``Lucas congruences'' for many combinatorial sequences, among them the Apéry numbers from the irrationality proof of $\zeta(3)$
(Gessel, 1982). In base $p$
these series are \emph{$p$-automatic}. That is the theme of Problem~\ref{pb:thuemorse}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:sternfib}
\idea A hyperbinary representation is a pair of binary numbers with no digit $1$ in common. Lucas's theorem detects exactly those pairs.
The bound is the Fibonacci recursion written as an inequality, because one of any two consecutive integers is even.

\writeup (a) Start from a hyperbinary representation of $n$. Set $k=\sum_{e_i=2}2^i$ and $j=\sum_{e_i=1}2^i$. Then $n=2k+j$, and $k$ and $j$
share no binary digit $1$. Conversely, any such pair $(k,j)$ rebuilds the digits $e_i$. So $s(n+1)$ counts the integers $k\ge0$
with $2k\le n$ for which $k$ and $j=n-2k$ have disjoint binary supports. Lucas's theorem (Volume~2, Chapter~3) says $\binom{k+j}k$ is odd if
and only if every binary digit of $k$ is at most the digit of $k+j$ in the same place. That is the same as: there is no carry when adding
$k$ and $j$, which is the same as: $k$ and $j$ have disjoint supports. Since $k+j=n-k$, this is the oddness of $\binom{n-k}k$.

(b) Let $M_k=\max\{s(n):2^k\le n\le2^{k+1}\}$. Then $M_0=1=F_2$ and $M_1=\max(s(2),s(3),s(4))=2=F_3$. Take $n$ in $[2^{k+1},2^{k+2}]$. If
$n=2m$ is even, then $s(n)=s(m)\le M_k$. If $n=2m+1$, then $m$ and $m+1$ lie in $[2^k,2^{k+1}]$, and one of them is even, say $2r$
with $r\in[2^{k-1},2^k]$. So $s(n)=s(m)+s(m+1)\le M_k+M_{k-1}$. Hence $M_{k+1}\le M_k+M_{k-1}$, and induction gives $M_k\le F_{k+2}$.

(c) Let $a_k=\frac{2^{k+2}-(-1)^k}3$. Then $a_1=3$, $a_2=5$, $a_3=11$, and $a_{k+1}=2a_k+\varepsilon_k$ with $\varepsilon_k=(-1)^k$. Check:
$2a_k+(-1)^k=\frac{2^{k+3}-2(-1)^k+3(-1)^k}3=a_{k+1}$. Then $s(a_{k+1})=s(a_k)+s(a_k+\varepsilon_k)$, and
$a_k+\varepsilon_k=2a_{k-1}+\varepsilon_{k-1}+\varepsilon_k=2a_{k-1}$, so $s(a_k+\varepsilon_k)=s(a_{k-1})$. Thus $s(a_{k+1})=s(a_k)+s(a_{k-1})$, with
$s(a_1)=2$ and $s(a_2)=3$. Therefore $s(a_k)=F_{k+2}$. Finally $2^k\le a_k\le2^{k+1}$.

\tools Chapter~\ref{ch:generating} (Problem~\ref{pb:stern}); Volume~2, Chapter~3 (Lucas's theorem); Volume~1 (Fibonacci numbers).
\leads Part (a) and the identity $\sum_k\binom{n-k}k=F_{n+1}$ say that $s(n+1)$ counts the odd entries on the $n$-th ``shallow diagonal''
of Pascal's triangle. That diagonal sums to $F_{n+1}$. The second maximum in each row, $\frac{5\cdot2^k+(-1)^k}3$, is the
mirror image: $s(2^{k+1}+2^k-n)=s(n)$ for $2^k\le n\le2^{k+1}$. This follows from the reflection symmetry of the Stern--Brocot tree.
Lehmer's paper and Northshield's paper, both listed below, give the details.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:thuemorse}
\idea The rules $t_{2n}=t_n$ and $t_{2n+1}=1+t_n$ write $T(x)$ in terms of $T(x^2)$. Modulo $2$, that is $T(x)^2$. A period cannot survive these rules.
An even period would halve, and an odd period fights the alternation $t_{2n+1}\ne t_{2n}$.

\writeup (a) Since $s(2n)=s(n)$ and $s(2n+1)=s(n)+1$,
\[
T(x)=\sum_nt_nx^{2n}+\sum_n(1+t_n)x^{2n+1}=(1+x)T(x^2)+\frac x{1-x^2}.
\]
In $\mathbb F_2[[x]]$ one has $T(x^2)=T(x)^2$ and $1-x^2=(1+x)^2$. So $T=(1+x)T^2+\frac x{(1+x)^2}$. Multiply through by $(1+x)^2$.

(b) Suppose $t_{n+P}=t_n$ for all $n\ge N$, with $P\ge1$ as small as possible. If $P$ is even, then for $n\ge N$ one has $t_{n+P/2}=t_{2n+P}=t_{2n}=t_n$, so $P/2$ is a
period from $N$ on. That contradicts minimality. So $P$ is odd. For $n\ge N$,
\[
t_{2n+P}=t_{2n}=t_n\quad\text{and}\quad t_{2n+P}=t_{2(n+\frac{P-1}2)+1}=1+t_{n+\frac{P-1}2},
\]
hence $t_{n+\frac{P-1}2}=1+t_n$. The same comparison gives $t_{2n+1+P}=t_{2n+1}=1+t_n$ and $t_{2n+1+P}=t_{2(n+\frac{P+1}2)}=t_{n+\frac{P+1}2}$, so $t_{n+\frac{P+1}2}=1+t_n$.
Therefore $t_{m+1}=t_m$ for all large $m$: the sequence is eventually constant. But $t_{2n+1}=1+t_{2n}$ for every $n$. That is a contradiction.

(c) Part (a) says $T$ is a root of $(1+x)^3Y^2+(1+x)^2Y+x$, so $T$ is algebraic of degree at most $2$. If $T$ were rational, its coefficients
would obey a linear recurrence from some index on, and would be eventually periodic, as in Problem~\ref{pb:kolberg}. That contradicts
(b). The degree is exactly $2$.

\tools Theorem~\ref{thm:rational} (rational series and recurrences); Volume~3, Chapter~9 (the Frobenius map in characteristic $p$).
\leads \emph{Christol's theorem} (1979) says that a power series over $\mathbb F_p$ is algebraic over $\mathbb F_p(x)$ if and only if the sequence
of coefficients is \emph{$p$-automatic}: a finite automaton, reading the base-$p$ digits of $n$, computes $a_n$. Thue--Morse,
the Catalan numbers modulo $2$, and the central binomial coefficients modulo $p$ (Problem~\ref{pb:centralp}) are examples. Allouche and Shallit's book is the reference.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:hermite}
\idea Take a polynomial $f$ whose derivatives at $0$ and at $1$ are integers, and build an antiderivative of $e^{kx}f(x)$ of the shape $e^{kx}F(x)$.
The integral $\int_0^1e^{kx}f$ then becomes an integer combination of $1$ and $e^k$. The factor $\frac1{n!}$ in $f$ makes that integer smaller than $1$.

\writeup (a) Let $f(x)=\frac{x^n(1-x)^n}{n!}=\frac1{n!}\sum_{j=n}^{2n}c_jx^j$ with $c_j\in\Z$. Then $f^{(j)}(0)=\frac{j!}{n!}c_j\in\Z$ for $n\le j\le2n$, and $f^{(j)}(0)=0$ for
every other $j$. Since $f(1-x)=f(x)$, one also has $f^{(j)}(1)\in\Z$. Let $F=\sum_{j=0}^{2n}(-1)^jk^{2n-j}f^{(j)}$. Then
\[
kF+F'=\sum_{j=0}^{2n}(-1)^jk^{2n+1-j}f^{(j)}+\sum_{j=0}^{2n}(-1)^jk^{2n-j}f^{(j+1)}=k^{2n+1}f,
\]
because the sums telescope and $f^{(2n+1)}=0$. So $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\bigl(e^{kx}F(x)\bigr)=k^{2n+1}e^{kx}f(x)$, and
\[
k^{2n+1}\int_0^1e^{kx}f(x)\dd x=e^kF(1)-F(0).
\]
Suppose $e^k=\frac ab$ with integers $a,b\ge1$. Then
\[
M_n=bk^{2n+1}\int_0^1e^{kx}f(x)\dd x=aF(1)-bF(0)
\]
is an integer. It is positive, because
$f>0$ on $]0,1[$. But $0<f\le\frac{4^{-n}}{n!}$, so $M_n\le\frac{bk e^k(k^2/4)^n}{n!}\to0$. For large $n$ one has $0<M_n<1$, which is impossible.

(b) Write $r=\frac pq$ with $p\ne0$ and $q\ge1$. If $e^r$ were rational, then so would $(e^r)^q=e^p$ and $e^{-p}=\frac1{e^p}$. One of those is $e^k$
with $k=|p|\ge1$, which contradicts (a). If $\log r=\frac pq$ for a rational $r>0$ with $r\ne1$, then $p\ne0$ and $e^{p/q}=r$ would be
rational.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:piirrational} (the same method for $\pi^2$); Volume~1, Chapter~8 ($e$ is irrational).}
\leads Hermite (1873) carried the same idea further, using several functions $e^{kx}$ at once, and proved that $e$ is transcendental: it is not the root of
any non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients. Lindemann (1882) proved that $\pi$ is transcendental. The circle
cannot be squared with ruler and compass.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:centralsum}
\idea Write $\frac{(n-1)!\,n!}{(2n)!}$ as a Beta integral. Summing the integrands gives a geometric series in $t(1-t)$, and $t(1-t)\le\frac14$. The remaining rational function has denominator $1-t+t^2$, whose roots are primitive sixth roots of unity, so the value involves $\pi\sqrt3$.

\writeup (a) Theorem~\ref{thm:betagamma} gives $\int_0^1t^{n-1}(1-t)^n\dd t=B(n,n+1)=\frac{(n-1)!\,n!}{(2n)!}=\frac1{n\binom{2n}n}$. Sum the non-negative
integrands: $\sum_{n\ge1}t^{n-1}(1-t)^n=\frac{1-t}{1-t+t^2}$. The substitution $t\mapsto1-t$ fixes $1-t+t^2$, so the integral of the sum is half of
\[
\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dt}{1-t+t^2}=\frac2{\sqrt3}\Bigl[\arctan\frac{2t-1}{\sqrt3}\Bigr]_0^1=\frac2{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac\pi3=\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}.
\]
The sum is therefore $\frac\pi{3\sqrt3}=0.6046\ldots$

(b) One has $\frac1{\binom{2n}n}=(2n+1)B(n+1,n+1)=(2n+1)\int_0^1u^n\dd t$ with $u=t-t^2$, and $\sum_{n\ge0}(2n+1)u^n=\frac{1+u}{(1-u)^2}$ (differentiate the geometric
series). Set $q=1-t+t^2=1-u$. Then $\frac{1+u}{(1-u)^2}=\frac{2-q}{q^2}=\frac2{q^2}-\frac1q$. With $t-\frac12=\frac{\sqrt3}2\tan\theta$, one has $q=\frac34\sec^2\theta$ and
$\theta\in[-\frac\pi6,\frac\pi6]$:
\[
\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dt}{q^2}=\frac{8\sqrt3}9\int_{-\pi/6}^{\pi/6}\cos^2\theta\dd\theta=\frac{8\sqrt3}9\Bigl(\frac\pi6+\frac{\sqrt3}4\Bigr)=\frac{4\pi\sqrt3}{27}+\frac23.
\]
Hence $\sum_{n\ge0}\frac1{\binom{2n}n}=\frac{8\pi\sqrt3}{27}+\frac43-\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}=\frac43+\frac{2\pi\sqrt3}{27}=1.7364\ldots$

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:betagamma} (Beta and Gamma); Volume~1, Chapter~8 (integrals of rational functions).}
\leads D.~H.~Lehmer's 1985 article collects dozens of sums of this kind. The factor $\sqrt3$ is expected: these series are values
of $L$-functions of characters modulo $3$. The same technique, applied to $\frac1{n^3\binom{2n}n}$, produces Apéry's series for $\zeta(3)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:lehmer}
\idea The binomial series $\sum_nc_nx^n=\frac1{\sqrt{1-x}}$, for the exponent $-\frac12$, gives both sums in (a). Part (b) is a telescoping
sum. The right multiple of $nc_n^2$ produces it.

\writeup (a) For $|x|<1$, $\sum_nc_nx^{2n}=(1-x^2)^{-1/2}$. Integrate from $0$ to $x$: $\arcsin x=\sum_n\frac{c_n}{2n+1}x^{2n+1}$. The terms are
non-negative and $c_n\le1$, so the series converges at $x=1$. Monotone convergence says the sum is $\arcsin1=\frac\pi2$. Next,
$\sum_{n\ge1}c_nx^{n-1}=\frac{(1-x)^{-1/2}-1}x$. Integrate from $0$ to $1$ and set $v=\sqrt{1-x}$ ($x=1-v^2$, $\mathrm dx=-2v\dd v$):
\[
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{c_n}n=\int_0^1\frac{1/v-1}{1-v^2}\,2v\dd v=\int_0^1\frac{2\dd v}{1+v}=2\log2,
\]
again by monotone convergence at the endpoint.

(b) Proposition~\ref{prop:binomcoef} gives $c_n\sim\frac1{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, so $c_n^2\sim\frac1{\pi n}$ and $\sum c_n^2$ diverges like the harmonic series. Set $b_n=-4nc_n^2$. From
$c_{n+1}=\frac{2n+1}{2n+2}c_n$,
\[
b_n-b_{n+1}=4c_n^2\Bigl(\frac{(2n+1)^2}{4(n+1)}-n\Bigr)=4c_n^2\cdot\frac1{4(n+1)}=\frac{c_n^2}{n+1}.
\]
Therefore $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{c_n^2}{n+1}=b_0-b_N=4Nc_N^2\to\frac4\pi$.

\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~8 (Taylor series); Proposition~\ref{prop:binomcoef} (central binomial coefficient).}
\leads One has $\sum c_n^2x^n=\frac2\pi K(\sqrt x)$, a complete elliptic integral. Its blow-up as $x\to1$ is the divergence (this is the $K$ of the chapter, and $K(k)\to\infty$ as $k\to1$). Ramanujan's series for $\frac1\pi$, such as $\sum_n\binom{2n}n^3\frac{42n+5}{2^{12n+4}}=\frac1\pi$, sit in the same family.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:catalanmoments}
\idea The substitution $x=2\sin\theta$ turns the moment into a Wallis integral. The Beta function evaluates it.

\writeup (a) Odd moments vanish by symmetry. Set $x=2\sin\theta$. Then $\sqrt{4-x^2}=2\cos\theta$ and $\mathrm dx=2\cos\theta\dd\theta$:
\[
\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-2}^2x^{2n}\sqrt{4-x^2}\dd x=\frac{4^{n+1}}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2n}\theta\cos^2\theta\dd\theta=\frac{4^{n+1}}{2\pi}B\Bigl(n+\frac12,\frac32\Bigr)
\]
by Exercise~\ref{ex:sincos}. Now $B(n+\frac12,\frac32)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\frac12)\Gamma(\frac32)}{\Gamma(n+2)}$, with $\Gamma(n+\frac12)=\frac{(2n)!}{4^nn!}\sqrt\pi$ and $\Gamma(\frac32)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$. The
moment equals $\frac{4^{n+1}}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{(2n)!\,\pi}{2\cdot4^nn!\,(n+1)!}=\frac{(2n)!}{n!\,(n+1)!}=C_n$.

(b) Set $x=y^2$. Then $\mathrm dx=2y\dd y$ and $\sqrt{\frac{4-x}x}\,\mathrm dx=2\sqrt{4-y^2}\dd y$. The integral becomes $\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^22y^{2n}\sqrt{4-y^2}\dd y$, which is (a).

(c) Set $x=4(1-u)$. Then $C_n=\frac{2\cdot4^n}\pi\int_0^1(1-u)^{n-1/2}\sqrt u\dd u$. The factor $(1-u)^{n}=e^{n\log(1-u)}$ concentrates near $u=0$. Laplace's method
(Theorem~\ref{thm:laplace}; the integral is in fact exact, equal to $B(\frac32,n+\frac12)$) gives $\int_0^1(1-u)^{n-1/2}\sqrt u\dd u\sim\int_0^\infty e^{-nu}\sqrt u\dd u=\Gamma(\frac32)n^{-3/2}=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2n^{-3/2}$.
Hence $C_n\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2}}$.

\tools{Exercise~\ref{ex:sincos}, Proposition~\ref{prop:gammahalf} ($\Gamma(\frac12)$), Theorem~\ref{thm:laplace} (Laplace).}
\leads Part (a) is the moment form of Wigner's semicircle law (1955). The eigenvalues of a large random symmetric matrix, scaled by
$\sqrt n$, follow the density $\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}$. The proof computes $\frac1n\operatorname{tr}(A^{2k})$ by counting closed walks, and those walks
are Dyck paths, which Catalan numbers count. Part (b) is the Marchenko--Pastur law for $AA^{\mathsf T}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:apcount}
\idea A progression is a solution of $x+z=2y$, so the count is a Fourier sum. The frequency $r=0$ gives the count for a random set of the same density. The other frequencies are bounded by the largest non-trivial coefficient. Small coefficients mean the set looks random, and then it has about the expected number of progressions.

\writeup (a) The map $(x,d)\mapsto(x,x+d,x+2d)$ is a bijection from $\mathbb F_p^2$ onto the triples $(x,y,z)$ with $x+z=2y$. Given such a triple,
$d=y-x$ and then $z=2y-x=x+2d$. So
\[
T(A)=\sum_{x,y,z\in A}\frac1p\sum_re\Bigl(\frac{r(x+z-2y)}p\Bigr)=\frac1p\sum_r\hat A(r)^2\hat A(-2r).
\]

(b) The term $r=0$ equals $\frac{|A|^3}p$. For $r\ne0$, one has $-2r\ne0$ because $p$ is odd. The rest is at most
$\frac1p\max_{s\ne0}|\hat A(s)|\sum_r|\hat A(r)|^2=|A|\max_{s\ne0}|\hat A(s)|$, by Parseval.

(c) Let $\chi$ be the Legendre symbol, with $\chi(0)=0$. For $x\ne0$, $1_Q(x)=\frac12(1+\chi(x))$, so for $r\ne0$,
\[
\hat Q(r)=\frac12\sum_{x\ne0}e\Bigl(\frac{rx}p\Bigr)+\frac12\sum_x\chi(x)e\Bigl(\frac{rx}p\Bigr)=\frac{-1+\chi(r)g}2,
\]
where $g=\sum_x\chi(x)e(\frac xp)$ is the Gauss sum (the substitution is $x\mapsto\bar rx$), and $|g|=\sqrt p$. Hence $|\hat Q(r)|\le\frac{\sqrt p+1}2$. Part (b) with
$|Q|=\frac{p-1}2$ is the claim.

(d) Part (b) gives $T(A)\ge\delta^3p^2-\delta p\cdot\frac12\delta^2p=\frac12\delta^3p^2$. The pairs with $d=0$ number $|A|=\delta p$, and $\frac12\delta^3p^2>\delta p$ because
$\delta^2p>2$. Some pair therefore has $d\ne0$.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion} and Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussfourier} (Parseval, the Gauss sum); Volume~2, Chapter~6 (the Legendre symbol).}
\leads Part~(d) is the first half of Roth's proof (1953) that a subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ of density $\delta$ contains a three-term
progression once $N$ is large. Either every non-trivial coefficient is small, and (d) finds a progression, or one coefficient is large, and
the set is denser on a long subprogression, where the argument starts again. Meshulam's version over $\mathbb F_3^n$ is in Volume~8. Behrend (1946)
built progression-free sets of size $Ne^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$. Kelley and Meka (2023) proved a nearly matching upper bound of the
shape $Ne^{-c(\log N)^\beta}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:dftspectrum}
\idea $F^4=I$ allows only four eigenvalues. Two traces, one of them a Gauss sum, fix how often each eigenvalue occurs.

\writeup (a) Orthogonality gives
\[
(F^2x)_j=\frac1n\sum_le\Bigl(-\frac{jl}n\Bigr)\sum_kx_ke\Bigl(-\frac{lk}n\Bigr)=\sum_kx_k\cdot\frac1n\sum_le\Bigl(-\frac{l(j+k)}n\Bigr)=x_{-j}.
\]
So $F^2=R$, the reversal, and $F^4=R^2=I$. The polynomial $X^4-1$ annihilates $F$ and has four simple roots, so $F$ is diagonalisable. Its
minimal polynomial divides $X^4-1$ and therefore has simple roots. The eigenvalues lie among $1,-1,-i,i$.

(b) In an eigenbasis,
\[
\operatorname{tr}F=m_1-m_{-1}-im_{-i}+im_i,\qquad\operatorname{tr}F^2=m_1+m_{-1}-m_{-i}-m_i.
\]
Now $\operatorname{tr}F^2=\operatorname{tr}R$ counts the $k\in\Z/n\Z$ with $k=-k$: the count is $1$ if $n$ is odd and $2$ if $n$ is even. And
$\operatorname{tr}F=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_ke(-\frac{k^2}n)=\frac{\overline{G(n)}}{\sqrt n}$, where $G(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e(\frac{k^2}n)$. Gauss's theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:gausssign}) says $\operatorname{tr}F$ is $1-i$, $1$, $0$, $-i$ for
$n\equiv0,1,2,3\pmod4$.

(c) Let $\sigma=m_1+m_{-1}$ and $\tau=m_{-i}+m_i$. Then $\sigma+\tau=n$ and $\sigma-\tau=\operatorname{tr}F^2$, while $m_1-m_{-1}=\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{tr}F$ and $m_i-m_{-i}=\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{tr}F$. For
$n=4m$: $\sigma=2m+1$, $\tau=2m-1$, $m_1-m_{-1}=1$, $m_i-m_{-i}=-1$, so $(m_1,m_{-1},m_{-i},m_i)=(m+1,m,m,m-1)$. For $n=4m+1$: $\sigma=2m+1$, $\tau=2m$,
and the differences are $1$ and $0$, so $(m+1,m,m,m)$. For $n=4m+2$: $\sigma=2m+2$, $\tau=2m$, and the differences are $0$, $0$, so $(m+1,m+1,m,m)$. For $n=4m+3$: $\sigma=2m+2$,
$\tau=2m+1$, and the differences are $0$ and $-1$, so $(m+1,m+1,m+1,m)$.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion} (DFT inversion) and Theorem~\ref{thm:gausssign} (Gauss's evaluation); Volume~1, Chapter~7 (diagonalisation).}
\leads The argument runs backwards as well. Schur (1921) found the multiplicities independently, from the determinant of the DFT
matrix (a Vandermonde determinant), and deduced the value of $G(n)$, sign included. That sign had cost Gauss years of work. On $L^2(\R)$ the
Fourier transform also satisfies $\mathcal F^4=I$. Its eigenfunctions are the Hermite functions $H_k(\sqrt{2\pi}\,x)e^{-\pi x^2}$, with eigenvalue $(-i)^k$.
McClellan and Parks (1972) studied discrete analogues of those eigenfunctions for the DFT.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:erdosturan}
\idea The Fourier coefficients of an interval indicator decay slowly. Sandwich the indicator between continuous trapezoids, whose
coefficients decay like $h^{-2}$. The slopes have width $\delta$. What you gain is an absolutely convergent expansion, and that
turns a count of points into exponential sums.

\writeup (a) Write $k=\frac1\delta1_{[0,\delta]}$, extended to be $1$-periodic, just as $1_J$ is. Then $t(x)=\frac1\delta\int_0^\delta1_J(x-u)\dd u$ averages values in
$\{0,1\}$, so $0\le t\le1$. Fubini gives $\int_0^1t=\int_0^11_J=L$. Fourier coefficients multiply under a periodic convolution (Fubini
again): $\hat t(h)=\hat1_J(h)\hat k(h)$. For $h\ne0$,
\[
|\hat1_J(h)|=\Bigl|\int_Je(-hx)\dd x\Bigr|=\frac{|\sin\pi hL|}{\pi|h|}\le\frac1{\pi|h|},\qquad|\hat k(h)|=\frac{|\sin\pi h\delta|}{\pi|h|\delta}\le\min\Bigl(1,\frac1{\pi|h|\delta}\Bigr),
\]
and the bound follows.

(b) Part (a) gives $\sum_h|\hat t(h)|<\infty$, so $\sum_h\hat t(h)e(hx)$ converges uniformly to a continuous function with the same coefficients as $t$. That function is
$t$ itself (Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer}(b), after the change of variable $x\mapsto\frac x{2\pi}$). Hence
\[
\frac1N\sum_nt(x_n)=L+\frac1N\sum_{h\ne0}\hat t(h)S(h),
\]
and, since $S(-h)=\overline{S(h)}$, the error is at most
\[
E=\frac2N\sum_{h\ge1}\min\Bigl(\frac1{\pi h},\frac1{\pi^2h^2\delta}\Bigr)|S(h)|.
\]
Let $I$ have endpoints $a<b$ and length $\ell$,
and write $\nu=\frac1N\#\{n:x_n\in I\}$.

Upper bound. If $\ell\le1-2\delta$, take $J=[a-\delta,b]$, of length $L=\ell+\delta\le1-\delta$. For $x\in I$, the interval $[x-\delta,x]$ sits inside $J$, so $t(x)=1$. Thus $t\ge1_I$
and $\nu\le\frac1N\sum t(x_n)\le\ell+\delta+E$. If $\ell>1-2\delta$, then $\nu\le1$ and $\ell+2\delta>1$, so $\nu\le\ell+2\delta$.

Lower bound. If $\ell\ge\delta$, take $J=[a,b-\delta]$, of length $\ell-\delta$. If $t(x)>0$, then $x-u\in J$ for a set of $u\in[0,\delta]$ of positive
length, which forces $a<x<b$. So $t\le1_I$ and $\nu\ge\ell-\delta-E$. If $\ell<\delta$, then $\nu\ge0$ and $\ell-\delta<0$, so $\nu\ge\ell-\delta$.

Every case gives $|\nu-\ell|\le2\delta+E$, which is the claim.

(c) If $p\nmid h$, then $S(h)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}e(\frac{hk^2}p)=\sum_y(1+\chi(y))e(\frac{hy}p)=\chi(h)g$, where $\chi$ is the Legendre symbol and $g$ is the Gauss sum. So
$|S(h)|=\sqrt p$. If $p\mid h$, then $S(h)=p$. Two elementary bounds, for $X\ge1$: $\sum_{h\le X}\frac1h\le1+\log X$, and
$\sum_{h>X}\frac1{h^2}\le\sum_{h>\lfloor X\rfloor}\frac1{h(h-1)}=\frac1{\lfloor X\rfloor}\le\frac2X$. Take $\delta=p^{-1/2}$. The terms with $p\nmid h$ contribute at most
\[
\frac{2\sqrt p}p\Bigl(\sum_{h\le\sqrt p}\frac1{\pi h}+\sum_{h>\sqrt p}\frac1{\pi^2h^2\delta}\Bigr)\le\frac2{\sqrt p}\Bigl(\frac{1+\frac12\log p}\pi+\frac2{\pi^2}\Bigr),
\]
and the terms with $h=mp$ contribute at most $\frac2p\sum_{m\ge1}\frac p{\pi^2m^2p^2\delta}=\frac1{3p^{3/2}}$. Altogether
\[
D\le\frac{\log p+2+2\pi+\frac4\pi}{\pi\sqrt p}+\frac1{3p^{3/2}}\le\frac{\log p+10}{\pi\sqrt p},
\]
because $2+2\pi+\frac4\pi=9.55\ldots$ and $\frac1{3p^{3/2}}\le\frac{0.44}{\pi\sqrt p}$ for $p\ge3$.

\tools{Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer} (uniqueness of Fourier coefficients), Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussfourier} (Gauss sums).}
\leads Erdős and Turán (1948) proved the general form $D\le\frac C H+C\sum_{h\le H}\frac{|S(h)|}{hN}$ for every $H$. Vaaler (1985) found the optimal
smoothing functions, which give the inequality with small explicit constants. For the squares modulo $p$, a computation gives $D\sqrt p/\log p\approx0.14$
at $p=10007$, inside the bound of (c).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:circlewalk}
\idea The Fourier coefficients of the law of $X_n$ are the powers $\cos^n(2\pi h\alpha)$. They tend to $0$ unless the cosine is $\pm1$.
Which frequencies survive is the question of when $2h\alpha$ is an integer.

\writeup One has $e(hX_n)=e(h\alpha(\varepsilon_1+\cdots+\varepsilon_n))$, and the signs are independent, so
\[
\mathbb E[e(hX_n)]=\prod_{k=1}^n\mathbb E[e(h\alpha\varepsilon_k)]=\cos^n(2\pi h\alpha),
\]
and $|\cos(2\pi h\alpha)|=1$ exactly when $2h\alpha\in\Z$.

(a) If $\alpha$ is irrational and $h\ne0$, then $2h\alpha\notin\Z$, so $\mathbb E[e(hX_n)]\to0=\int_0^1e(hx)\dd x$. Linearity gives $\mathbb E[P(X_n)]\to\int_0^1P$ for every
trigonometric polynomial $P$. Take a continuous $1$-periodic $g$ and an $\varepsilon>0$. Fejér's theorem (Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer}(a)) supplies a
trigonometric polynomial $P$ with $|g-P|\le\varepsilon$ everywhere, so $\limsup_n|\mathbb E[g(X_n)]-\int_0^1g|\le2\varepsilon$.

(b) Now $X_n$ takes values in the finite set $\frac1q\Z/\Z$. The law of a variable on this set is determined by the $q$ numbers $\mathbb E[e(hX)]$,
$0\le h<q$ (inversion of the DFT, Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}). It is enough to find the limits of these numbers. Since $\gcd(a,q)=1$, one has $2h\alpha\in\Z$ if and only if
$q\mid2h$.

If $q$ is odd, this means $q\mid h$, and then $\cos(2\pi h\alpha)=1$. So $\mathbb E[e(hX_n)]\to1_{q\mid h}=\frac1q\sum_{j<q}e(\frac{hj}q)$, the coefficient of the uniform law on
$\frac1q\Z/\Z$.

If $q$ is even, then $a$ is odd, and $q\mid2h$ means $h=l\frac q2$. Then $\cos(2\pi h\alpha)=\cos(\pi la)=(-1)^l$. So $\mathbb E[e(hX_n)]$ tends to $0$ unless $h=l\frac q2$,
in which case it equals $(-1)^{ln}$. For even $n$ these are the coefficients of the uniform law on $\{0,\frac2q,\frac4q,\dots\}$. For odd $n$ they are the coefficients of the
uniform law on $\{\frac1q,\frac3q,\dots\}$. Indeed $\frac2q\sum_je(h(\frac1q+\frac{2j}q))=e(\frac hq)1_{\frac q2\mid h}=(-1)^l$ for $h=l\frac q2$. The two sets are disjoint, so
$X_n$ has no limit. This is the parity obstruction of Problem~\ref{pb:cyclewalk}, with $\alpha=\frac1k$.

\tools{Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer} and Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}; Volume~4, Chapter~2 (independence).}
\leads Compare Weyl's theorem that $(\{n\alpha\})$ is equidistributed (Corollary~\ref{cor:nalpha}). The deterministic walk and the random walk
are both governed by whether $\alpha$ is irrational. The speed of convergence in (a) is $\cos^n(2\pi h\alpha)\approx e^{-2\pi^2n\|2h\alpha\|^2}$, where $\|\cdot\|$ is the
distance to the nearest integer. It depends on how well $2\alpha$ is approximated by rationals, that is, on the continued fraction of $2\alpha$ (Volume~2,
Chapter~9).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:fejer}
\idea The polynomial $z^nT(z)$ is self-reciprocal, so its roots come in pairs $\alpha$, $1/\bar\alpha$. On the circle the two members of a pair
are at proportional distances from $z$. Keeping one member of each pair produces a square root of $T$. Once $T=|P|^2$, an extremal problem
for $T$ becomes a quadratic-form problem for the coefficients of $P$.

\writeup (a) If $T$ is constant, take $P=\sqrt{c_0}$. Otherwise reduce $n$ until $c_n\ne0$, and let $Q(z)=\sum_{k=-n}^nc_kz^{k+n}$, of degree $2n$ with $Q(0)=c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}\ne0$.
Since $c_{-k}=\overline{c_k}$, one has $z^{2n}\overline{Q(1/\bar z)}=Q(z)$ for $z\ne0$, so $\alpha\mapsto1/\bar\alpha$ permutes the roots of $Q$ and preserves multiplicities. A root
$e^{i\theta_0}$ on the circle has even multiplicity: $T(\theta)=e^{-in\theta}Q(e^{i\theta})$ vanishes at $\theta_0$ to that order and does not change
sign. List the roots as $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n,1/\bar\alpha_1,\dots,1/\bar\alpha_n$ with $0<|\alpha_j|\le1$. Then $Q(z)=c_n\prod_j(z-\alpha_j)(z-1/\bar\alpha_j)$. For $|z|=1$,
$|z-1/\bar\alpha|=\frac{|\bar\alpha z-1|}{|\alpha|}=\frac{|z|\,|\bar\alpha-\bar z|}{|\alpha|}=\frac{|z-\alpha|}{|\alpha|}$. Since $T\ge0$,
\[
T(\theta)=|Q(e^{i\theta})|=\frac{|c_n|}{\prod_j|\alpha_j|}\prod_j|e^{i\theta}-\alpha_j|^2=|P(e^{i\theta})|^2,\qquad P(z)=\Bigl(\frac{|c_n|}{\prod_j|\alpha_j|}\Bigr)^{1/2}\prod_j(z-\alpha_j).
\]

(b) Write $P=\sum_{k=0}^np_kz^k$. Orthogonality gives $c_0=\frac1{2\pi}\int|P(e^{i\theta})|^2\dd\theta=\sum|p_k|^2=1$. Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$T(0)=|\sum p_k|^2\le(n+1)\sum|p_k|^2=n+1$, with equality if and only if all the $p_k$ are equal, that is $T=\frac1{n+1}|\sum_{k=0}^ne^{ik\theta}|^2$, the Fejér kernel $F_{n+1}$ of
Lemma~\ref{lem:fejerkernel}.

(c) Now $c_k=\frac{a_k}2$ for $k\ge1$ and $c_0=1$. With $T=|P|^2$ as in (b), $c_1=\frac1{2\pi}\int|P|^2e^{-i\theta}\dd\theta=\sum_kp_{k+1}\overline{p_k}$. Set $u_k=|p_k|$. Then
\[
a_1=2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}p_{k+1}\overline{p_k}\le2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}u_ku_{k+1}=u^{\mathsf T}Au,\qquad|u|=1,
\]
where $A$ is the adjacency matrix of the path on $n+1$ vertices. So $a_1\le\lambda_{\max}(A)$. The vectors $v^{(j)}_k=\sin\frac{(k+1)j\pi}{n+2}$, $0\le k\le n$, satisfy
$Av^{(j)}=2\cos\frac{j\pi}{n+2}v^{(j)}$ (use $\sin(x-y)+\sin(x+y)=2\sin x\cos y$, with $v_{-1}=v_{n+1}=0$), for $j=1,\dots,n+1$. These are $n+1$ distinct eigenvalues,
so they are all of them, and the largest is $2\cos\frac\pi{n+2}$. For equality take $p_k=v^{(1)}_k/|v^{(1)}|>0$. Then $T=|P|^2$ has real coefficients $c_k=c_{-k}$, so it is
a cosine polynomial $1+\sum a_k\cos k\theta$, and $a_1=2\sum p_kp_{k+1}=2\cos\frac\pi{n+2}$.

\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~6 (roots of polynomials) and Chapter~7 (symmetric matrices); Lemma~\ref{lem:fejerkernel} (the Fejér kernel).}
\leads Non-negative trigonometric polynomials turn up often. Mertens's identity $3+4\cos\theta+\cos2\theta=2(1+\cos\theta)^2\ge0$ is the heart of the zero-free
region for $\zeta$ on the line $\operatorname{Re}s=1$ (Volume~6). Delsarte's linear programming bound for codes, and the Cohn--Elkies bounds for sphere packings,
which Viazovska used in dimension $8$, optimise over non-negative or positive-definite functions. Engineers use the Fejér--Riesz
factorisation, under the name spectral factorisation, to design filters.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bernstein}
\idea A trigonometric polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $2n$ zeros in a period. If $T$ climbed faster than the pure oscillation
$\cos(n(\theta-\beta))$ that matches $T$ at one point, the difference would have too many zeros.

\writeup (a) First suppose $\|T\|<1$, and suppose $T'(\theta_0)^2+n^2T(\theta_0)^2>n^2$ for some $\theta_0$. Then $T'(\theta_0)\ne0$, because $|T(\theta_0)|<1$. Let $\gamma\in\left]0,\pi\right[$ with
$\cos\gamma=T(\theta_0)$, and choose $\beta$ so that $n(\theta_0-\beta)=\pm\gamma$. Choose the sign so that $C(\theta)=\cos(n(\theta-\beta))$ has $C(\theta_0)=T(\theta_0)$ and $C'(\theta_0)$ of the
same sign as $T'(\theta_0)$. Then $|C'(\theta_0)|=n\sin\gamma=n\sqrt{1-T(\theta_0)^2}<|T'(\theta_0)|$. The difference $D=C-T$ is a trigonometric polynomial of degree at most
$n$, and $D$ is not $0$ because $C$ reaches $1$. At the $2n$ points $\theta_j=\beta+\frac{j\pi}n$ of a period, $C=(-1)^j$, so $D(\theta_j)$ has the sign of $(-1)^j$, and $D$ vanishes in
each of the $2n$ arcs $]\theta_j,\theta_{j+1}[$. Look at the arc that contains $\theta_0$, where $C$ is monotone. Say $C$ decreases there. The other case
is symmetric. Then $D(\theta_j)>0>D(\theta_{j+1})$, and $T'(\theta_0)<C'(\theta_0)<0$, so $D'(\theta_0)>0$. Thus $D$ is negative just before $\theta_0$ and positive just after. So $D$
has a zero in $]\theta_j,\theta_0[$, the zero $\theta_0$, and a zero in $]\theta_0,\theta_{j+1}[$. That is at least $2n+2$ zeros in a period, which is impossible. Hence
$T'^2+n^2T^2\le n^2$ when $\|T\|<1$. Apply the same bound to $(1-\varepsilon)T$ and let $\varepsilon\to0$: the inequality also holds when $\|T\|\le1$. In particular $|T'|\le n$, and by
homogeneity $\|T'\|\le n\|T\|$. For complex $T$ and a given $\theta_0$, choose $\beta$ with $e^{i\beta}T'(\theta_0)=|T'(\theta_0)|$. The real polynomial $U=\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\beta}T)$ has
$\|U\|\le\|T\|$ and $U'(\theta_0)=|T'(\theta_0)|$, so $|T'(\theta_0)|\le n\|T\|$.

(b) The function $T(\theta)=P(\cos\theta)$ is a real trigonometric polynomial of degree $n$ with $\|T\|\le1$, and $T'(\theta)=-\sin\theta\,P'(\cos\theta)$. For $x=\cos\theta$ with
$0<\theta<\pi$, part (a) gives $|P'(x)|\sin\theta\le n$, that is $|P'(x)|\le\frac n{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.

(c) The polynomial $T=\sin n\theta$ has $\|T\|=1$ and $T'(0)=n$. For the Chebyshev polynomial, $T_n(\cos\theta)=\cos n\theta$, so $T_n'(\cos\theta)=\frac{n\sin n\theta}{\sin\theta}$. At $\theta=\frac\pi{2n}$,
$|T_n'(x)|=\frac n{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.

\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~5 (the multiple-angle form of cosine and sine) and Chapter~6 (a polynomial of degree $2n$ has at most $2n$ roots, applied to $z^nD$ on the unit circle; Chebyshev polynomials).}
\leads Bernstein proved the inequality in 1912. A function that trigonometric polynomials
of degree $n$ approximate within $n^{-\alpha}$ must be Hölder of order $\alpha$, for $0<\alpha<1$. That is the converse of Jackson's theorem. The sharper form
in (a) is the Bernstein--Szegő inequality. Near the ends of $[-1,1]$ the bound of (b) blows up. The truth there is Markov's
inequality (1889): $|P'|\le n^2$ on $[-1,1]$, with equality for $T_n$ at $\pm1$. Problem~\ref{pb:bernsteinabs} uses (a) to build a function.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bernsteinabs}
\idea Hölder continuity controls the $L^2$ size of differences. Parseval turns that into $\ell^2$ control of each dyadic block of
coefficients. Cauchy--Schwarz turns $\ell^2$ into $\ell^1$, at the cost of the square root of the block length. The two sides balance exactly
when $\alpha=\frac12$. Rudin--Shapiro polynomials show that at $\alpha=\frac12$ the $\ell^1$ sum can still diverge.

\writeup (a) The function $f$ is continuous, and $g(x)=f(x+h)-f(x-h)$ has coefficients $(e^{ikh}-e^{-ikh})c_k=2i\sin(kh)c_k$. Apply Parseval (Theorem~\ref{thm:parseval}).

(b) The left side of (a) is at most $C^2(2h)^{2\alpha}$. For $j\ge0$ let $B_j=\{k:2^{j-1}<|k|\le2^j\}$, a set of $2^j$ integers, and set $h=\frac\pi{3\cdot2^j}$. For
$k\in B_j$, $\frac\pi6<|k|h\le\frac\pi3$, so $\sin^2(kh)\ge\frac14$ and
\[
\sum_{k\in B_j}|c_k|^2\le\sum_k4\sin^2(kh)|c_k|^2\le C^2\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{3\cdot2^j}\Bigr)^{2\alpha}.
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
\sum_{k\in B_j}|c_k|\le2^{j/2}C\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}3\Bigr)^\alpha2^{-j\alpha}=C\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}3\Bigr)^\alpha2^{j(1/2-\alpha)}.
\]
This is a geometric series in $j$, because $\alpha>\frac12$. So
$\sum_k|c_k|=|c_0|+\sum_j\sum_{B_j}|c_k|<\infty$. Then $\sum_kc_ke^{ikx}$ converges uniformly to a continuous function with the coefficients of $f$, and that function is $f$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer}(b)).

(c) Let $g_j(x)=e^{i2^jx}P_j(e^{ix})$. Problem~\ref{pb:rudin} says the frequencies are $2^j,\dots,2^{j+1}-1$, the coefficients are $\pm1$, and $\|g_j\|\le\sqrt{2^{j+1}}$. So
$f=\sum_j2^{-j}g_j$ converges uniformly. Integrating term by term, $c_k(f)=\pm2^{-j}$ for $2^j\le k<2^{j+1}$, and $\sum_k|c_k(f)|=\sum_j2^{-j}2^j=\infty$. For the Hölder
bound, let $0<|h|\le1$ and choose $m\ge0$ with $2^{-m-1}<|h|\le2^{-m}$. The polynomial $g_j$ has degree less than $2^{j+1}$, so Bernstein's inequality (Problem~\ref{pb:bernstein})
gives $\|g_j'\|\le2^{j+1}\sqrt{2^{j+1}}$. Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}|f(x+h)-f(x)|&\le\sum_{j\le m}2^{-j}|h|\,2^{3(j+1)/2}+\sum_{j>m}2^{-j}\cdot2\sqrt{2^{j+1}}\\&\le\frac{2^{3/2}|h|2^{m/2}+2^{3/2}2^{-(m+1)/2}}{1-2^{-1/2}}\le20|h|^{1/2},\end{aligned}
\]
because $2^{m/2}\le|h|^{-1/2}$ and $2^{-(m+1)/2}<|h|^{1/2}$. For $|h|>1$, $|f(x+h)-f(x)|\le2\|f\|\le2\|f\||h|^{1/2}$. So $f$ is Hölder of order $\frac12$.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:parseval} and Corollary~\ref{cor:fejer}; Problems~\ref{pb:rudin} and~\ref{pb:bernstein}.}
\leads The space of absolutely convergent Fourier series is an algebra, the Wiener algebra of Volume~8. Wiener characterised its invertible elements
in 1932. Bernstein's theorem says the algebra contains every Hölder function of order greater than $\frac12$. The example in (c) is the one Rudin gave in 1959, to show that Hölder order $\frac12$ need not give an absolutely convergent Fourier series. Hardy and Littlewood had reached the same threshold earlier, with the series
$\sum_k\frac{e^{ick\log k}}ke^{ikx}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:fantaussky}
\idea Write the polygon as a sum of regular polygons, one for each frequency $m$. Those are its discrete Fourier modes. Differences and areas
are both diagonal in this decomposition. Each mode is compared with its own constant. The extreme mode is $m=1$, the regular
polygon traversed once.

\writeup (a) Write $x_k=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}c_m\omega^{mk}$ (DFT inversion, Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion}), so $c_0=\frac1n\sum x_k=0$. Then $x_{k+1}-x_k=\sum_mc_m(\omega^m-1)\omega^{mk}$. Parseval gives
\[
\begin{aligned}\sum_k|x_{k+1}-x_k|^2&=n\sum_m|c_m|^2|\omega^m-1|^2=n\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}4\sin^2\frac{\pi m}n|c_m|^2\\&\ge4\sin^2\frac\pi n\cdot n\sum_m|c_m|^2=4\sin^2\frac\pi n\sum_k|x_k|^2,\end{aligned}
\]
because $\sin^2\frac{\pi m}n\ge\sin^2\frac\pi n$ for $1\le m\le n-1$, with equality only for $m=1$ and $m=n-1$. Equality holds if and only if $c_m=0$ for every other $m$, that is
$x_k=a\omega^k+b\omega^{-k}$.

(b) With $z_k=\sum_mc_m\omega^{mk}$, orthogonality gives $\sum_k\overline{z_k}z_{k+1}=n\sum_m|c_m|^2\omega^m$, so $2A=n\sum_m|c_m|^2\sin\frac{2\pi m}n$, while
$\sum_k|z_{k+1}-z_k|^2=n\sum_m|c_m|^2\,4\sin^2\frac{\pi m}n$. Set $\theta_m=\frac{\pi m}n$. Then
\[
\sum_k|z_{k+1}-z_k|^2-4\tan\frac\pi n\cdot A=4n\sum_m|c_m|^2\sin\theta_m\Bigl(\sin\theta_m-\tan\frac\pi n\cos\theta_m\Bigr).
\]
The term $m=0$ vanishes. For $1\le m\le n-1$, one has $\sin\theta_m>0$. The bracket is positive if $\cos\theta_m\le0$, and it equals $\cos\theta_m(\tan\theta_m-\tan\frac\pi n)\ge0$ if
$\cos\theta_m>0$, with equality only for $m=1$. So the difference is $\ge0$, with equality if and only if $z_k=c_0+c_1\omega^k$: a positively oriented regular $n$-gon
with centre $c_0$, or a single point if $c_1=0$.

(c) If every side has length $s$, then $L=ns$ and $\sum|z_{k+1}-z_k|^2=ns^2=\frac{L^2}n$. Part (b) gives $A\le\frac{L^2}{4n\tan(\pi/n)}$, with equality for the regular
$n$-gon. Orient the polygon positively, so that $A$ is its area.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:dftinversion} (DFT inversion and Parseval); Volume~1, Chapter~5 (roots of unity).}
\leads Ky Fan, Olga Taussky and John Todd (1955) proved (a) as a discrete analogue of Wirtinger's inequality. Part (b) is the discrete
form of Hurwitz's Fourier proof of the isoperimetric inequality (Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}). The full polygonal isoperimetric inequality,
$L^2\ge4n\tan\frac\pi n\cdot A$ for every $n$-gon, needs one further step: equalising the sides. The same decomposition into regular
modes is the key to Problems~\ref{pb:midpoint} and~\ref{pb:napoleon}.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:involasym}
\idea On the circle $|z|=r$, the quantity $|T(z)|r^{-n}$ is smallest on the positive axis. There the integrand is a sharp Gaussian bump around $\theta=0$. Laplace's method
finishes the estimate, and Stirling turns it into a closed form.

\writeup (a) The function $T$ is entire. Cauchy's formula on $|z|=r$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:cauchyformula}) gives $\frac{t_n}{n!}=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{T(z)}{z^{n+1}}\dd z$. Parametrise $z=re^{i\theta}$.

(b) One has $|T(re^{i\theta})|=\exp\operatorname{Re}\bigl(re^{i\theta}+\frac12r^2e^{2i\theta}\bigr)=\exp\bigl(r\cos\theta+\frac12r^2\cos2\theta\bigr)$, and
$r(\cos\theta-1)+\frac12r^2(\cos2\theta-1)=-2r\sin^2\frac\theta2-r^2\sin^2\theta$.

(c) Let $\delta=r^{-4/5}$. \emph{Tails.} For $\delta\le|\theta|\le\frac\pi2$, one has $\sin^2\theta\ge\frac{4\theta^2}{\pi^2}$. Part (b) says the integrand has modulus at most
$T(r)r^{-n}e^{-4r^2\delta^2/\pi^2}=T(r)r^{-n}e^{-4r^{2/5}/\pi^2}$. For $\frac\pi2\le|\theta|\le\pi$, one has $\sin^2\frac\theta2\ge\frac12$, so the modulus is at most $T(r)r^{-n}e^{-r}$. Both tails are
$o\bigl(T(r)r^{-n}/\sqrt b\bigr)$, because $b\le3r^2$.

\emph{Centre.} Let $G(\theta)=re^{i\theta}+\frac12r^2e^{2i\theta}-in\theta$. Then $G(0)=\ln T(r)$, $G'(0)=i(r+r^2)-in=0$, $G''(0)=-(r+2r^2)=-b$, and $|G'''|\le r+4r^2\le5r^2$. Taylor's formula gives
$G(\theta)=\ln T(r)-\frac b2\theta^2+E(\theta)$ with $|E(\theta)|\le r^2|\theta|^3\le r^{-2/5}$ for $|\theta|\le\delta$. Hence
\[
\int_{-\delta}^\delta e^{G(\theta)}\dd\theta=T(r)\bigl(1+O(r^{-2/5})\bigr)\int_{-\delta}^\delta e^{-b\theta^2/2}\dd\theta=T(r)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}b}\,\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),
\]
because $b\delta^2\ge2r^{2/5}\to\infty$, so the Gaussian tails beyond $|\theta|=\delta$ are negligible. Divide by $2\pi r^n$. That is the claim.

(d) One has $r=\frac{\sqrt{4n+1}-1}2=\sqrt n-\frac12+\frac1{8\sqrt n}+O(n^{-3/2})$. Since $r^2=n-r$, one has $\ln T(r)=r+\frac{r^2}2=\frac{n+r}2$. Next $\frac r{\sqrt n}=1+\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon=-\frac1{2\sqrt n}+\frac1{8n}+O(n^{-3/2})$, and $\ln(1+\varepsilon)=\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon^2}2+O(\varepsilon^3)=-\frac1{2\sqrt n}+O(n^{-3/2})$, so $n\ln r=\frac n2\ln n-\frac{\sqrt n}2+O(n^{-1/2})$. Hence
\[
\ln\frac{T(r)}{r^n}=\frac n2+\frac{\sqrt n}2-\frac14-\frac n2\ln n+\frac{\sqrt n}2+o(1)=\frac n2+\sqrt n-\frac14-\frac n2\ln n+o(1).
\]
With $b\sim2n$ and $n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,n^ne^{-n}$,
\[
t_n\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,n^ne^{-n}\cdot\frac{e^{n/2+\sqrt n-1/4}n^{-n/2}}{\sqrt{4\pi n}}=\frac1{\sqrt2}n^{n/2}e^{-n/2+\sqrt n-1/4}.
\]

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:cauchyformula} (Cauchy's formula), the cutting argument of Theorem~\ref{thm:laplace} and Example~\ref{ex:centralsaddle}; Volume~1, Chapter~8 (Stirling, with the constant $\sqrt{2\pi}$).}
\leads This is the saddle-point method of Chapter~\ref{ch:asymptotics}, applied to a function that is not a pure power. Hayman (1956) axiomatised that setting. The asymptotic for
involutions is due to Chowla, Herstein and Moore (1951) and to Moser and Wyman (1955). The ratio of $t_n$ to the approximation
in (d) is $1.0091$ for $n=1000$. The error is of relative order $\frac1{\sqrt n}$. That is why the approximation in (c), whose ratio is $0.99983$ at $n=1000$, is much better.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:distinctcycles}
\idea The generating function is $\frac1{1-z}$ times a function that stays tame at $z=1$. The partial sums of its coefficients converge to the value at $1$, and that value
is $e^{-\gamma}$.

\writeup (a) A permutation is a set of cycles, and a given $k$-set carries $(k-1)!$ cycles. The exponential generating function of one cycle of length $k$
is $\frac{z^k}k$. Allow each length at most once. The exponential formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:expformula}) gives $\sum_n(\text{number}/n!)z^n=\prod_k(1+\frac{z^k}k)$, and the number divided
by $n!$ is $d_n$. Expanding: $d_1=1$, $d_2=\frac12$ (only $1+\frac{z^2}2$ contributes, not the product $z\cdot z$), $d_3=\frac13+\frac12=\frac56$, $d_4=\frac14+\frac13=\frac7{12}$, $d_5=\frac15+\frac14+\frac16=\frac{37}{60}$.

(b) For $|z|<1$, $\prod_ke^{z^k/k}=e^{-\ln(1-z)}=\frac1{1-z}$. Every product converges absolutely, so $\prod(1+\frac{z^k}k)=\frac{h(z)}{1-z}$. At $z=1$,
$\prod_{k\le N}(1+\frac1k)e^{-1/k}=(N+1)e^{-H_N}\to e^{-\gamma}$.

(c) The bound $\|fg\|\le\|f\|\,\|g\|$ is the triangle inequality on the Cauchy product. Next, $(1+w)e^{-w}=\sum_j\frac{(-w)^j}{j!}+\sum_j\frac{(-1)^jw^{j+1}}{j!}=1-\sum_{j\ge2}(-1)^j\frac{j-1}{j!}w^j$.
With $w=\frac{z^k}k$,
\[
\Bigl\|\Bigl(1+\frac{z^k}k\Bigr)e^{-z^k/k}-1\Bigr\|\le\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{j-1}{j!}k^{-j}\le\frac1{k^2}\sum_{j\ge2}\frac{j-1}{j!}=\frac1{k^2}.
\]
Let $P_N$ be the product of the first $N$ factors. Then $\|P_N\|\le\prod_k(1+k^{-2})=:C<\infty$, and $\|P_{N+1}-P_N\|\le\|P_N\|\cdot(N+1)^{-2}\le C(N+1)^{-2}$. So $(P_N)$ is a Cauchy
sequence for $\|\cdot\|$ and converges to a series $\tilde h$ with $\|\tilde h\|<\infty$. Convergence in this norm gives convergence of each coefficient, and uniform convergence on
$|z|\le1$. Thus $\tilde h=h$ on $|z|<1$ and $\tilde h(1)=\lim P_N(1)=h(1)$. Hence $\sum_j|h_j|<\infty$ and $\sum_jh_j=h(1)$.

(d) From $\sum d_nz^n=h(z)\sum z^n$ one has $d_n=h_0+\dots+h_n\to\sum_jh_j=e^{-\gamma}$.

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:expformula} (exponential formula) and Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic} ($H_N-\ln N\to\gamma$); Volume~1, Chapter~3 (absolute convergence, products).}
\leads The convergence is slow: $d_{300}=0.5633$, and the error is of order $\frac1n$. A permutation with no cycle shorter than $m$ has probability
about $\frac{e^{-\gamma}}m$. An integer with no prime factor below $m$ has density about $\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\ln m}$ (Mertens). The constant $e^{-\gamma}$ appears whenever
cycles or primes are forbidden to repeat, or forbidden to be small. The norm $\|\cdot\|$ is the norm of the Wiener algebra, which returns with Wiener's theorem in Volume~8.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ramanujan}
\idea Both $Q$ and $R$ are Laplace integrals of one function, taken on the two sides of its peak. The peak is not symmetric. The skewness of $s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}$, read through
$g(v)=1-\frac v3+\cdots$, contributes $\mp\frac13$. Ramanujan's $\frac13$ is half of $\frac13+\frac13$.

\writeup (a) One has $\frac{n^{n-j}}{(n-j)!}=\frac{n^n}{n!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-j+1)}{n^j}$. Sum over $j=1,\dots,n$ to get the first identity. Likewise $\frac{n^{n+j}}{(n+j)!}=\frac{n^n}{n!}\cdot\frac{n^j}{(n+1)\cdots(n+j)}$, summed
over $j\ge0$. Adding the two series gives $e^n$. Then $\theta_n\frac{n^n}{n!}=\frac{e^n}2-\frac{n^n}{n!}Q=\frac{n^n}{n!}\bigl(\frac{Q+R}2-Q\bigr)$.

(b) The function $F(x)=\sum_{k\ge n}e^{-x}\frac{x^k}{k!}$ satisfies $F(0)=0$ and $F'(x)=\sum_{k\ge n}e^{-x}\bigl(\frac{x^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}-\frac{x^k}{k!}\bigr)=e^{-x}\frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}$, a telescoping sum. So
$\sum_{k\ge n}\frac{n^k}{k!}=\frac{e^n}{(n-1)!}\int_0^nt^{n-1}e^{-t}\dd t$. Set $t=ns$ and use (a):
\[
R(n)=\frac{n!}{n^n}\cdot\frac{e^n}{(n-1)!}\,n^n\int_0^1s^{n-1}e^{-ns}\dd s=n\int_0^1s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}\dd s.
\]
The same computation with $\int_0^\infty t^{n-1}e^{-t}\dd t=(n-1)!$ gives
\[
Q+R=n\int_0^\infty s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}\dd s,
\]
and subtracting $R$ gives the formula for $Q$.

(c) The function $\varphi$ is convex and has minimum $\varphi(1)=0$, so $v$ is an increasing bijection. It is smooth, with $v'(1)=1$ (because $\varphi(s)=\frac{(s-1)^2}2+O((s-1)^3)$), and the inverse is smooth. Since
$s^{n-1}e^{n(1-s)}=\frac{e^{-n\varphi(s)}}s$ and $v\,\mathrm dv=\mathrm d\varphi=\frac{s-1}s\mathrm ds$, one gets $\frac{\mathrm ds}s=g(v)\,\mathrm dv$. That is the pair of integrals. Also $g>0$, because $v$ and $s-1$ have the same sign.
For $s>1$, $\ln s\ge(s-1)-\frac{(s-1)^2}2$, so $\varphi\le\frac{(s-1)^2}2$, $v\le s-1$ and $g\le1$. For $s<1$ the needed bound is $|v|\le(1-s)(1+|v|)$, that is $|v|\le\frac{1-s}s=:w$. Set $s=\frac1{1+w}$. Then
$2\varphi=2\ln(1+w)-\frac{2w}{1+w}$, and $k(w)=w^2-2\ln(1+w)+\frac{2w}{1+w}$ has $k(0)=0$ and $k'(w)=2w-\frac{2w}{(1+w)^2}\ge0$, so $2\varphi\le w^2$. Next, with $x=s-1$, $\varphi=\frac{x^2}2-\frac{x^3}3+O(x^4)$,
so $v=x(1-\frac x3+O(x^2))$, $x=v+\frac{v^2}3+O(v^3)$ and $g=\frac vx=1-\frac v3+O(v^2)$ near $0$. Since $g$ is smooth, $|g(v)-1+\frac v3|\le Cv^2$ on $[-1,1]$. For $|v|\ge1$,
$|g(v)-1+\frac v3|\le2+2|v|\le4v^2$.

(d) Set $I_j=n\int_0^\infty v^je^{-nv^2/2}\dd v$. Then $I_0=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}$, $I_1=1$, $I_2=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2\sqrt n}$. So $Q=I_0-\frac13I_1+O(I_2)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}-\frac13+O(n^{-1/2})$. The same computation on
$\left]-\infty,0\right]$ gives $R=\sqrt{\frac{\pi n}2}+\frac13+O(n^{-1/2})$. Hence $\theta_n=\frac{R-Q}2=\frac13+O(n^{-1/2})$.

(e) One has $e^{-n}\sum_{k\le n}\frac{n^k}{k!}=e^{-n}\frac{n^n}{n!}(Q+1)=\frac{Q+1}{Q+R}$. Now $Q+R=\frac{n!e^n}{n^n}=\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(1+O(\frac1n))$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:stirlingseries}) and $Q+1=\frac12\sqrt{2\pi n}+\frac23+O(n^{-1/2})$, so the ratio is
$\frac12+\frac2{3\sqrt{2\pi n}}+O(\frac1n)$.

\tools{Problem~\ref{pb:poissonise} (the sum $Q$); Theorem~\ref{thm:stirlingseries} (Stirling's series) and the idea of Theorem~\ref{thm:laplace} (Laplace's method); Volume~1, Chapter~8 (inverse functions).}
\leads The limit $\frac12$ in (e) is also what the central limit theorem gives for a Poisson variable of mean $n$ (Volume~4, Chapter~6). Computation shows $n(\theta_n-\frac13)\to0.029630$, which is $\frac4{135}$. That is the next term, and it comes from the $v^2$ term of $g$. The same Laplace
integral, cut at other places, is Knuth's analysis of linear probing. With a table of $m$ cells and $n$ keys, the cost of a search involves $Q$-like
sums at the ratio $\frac nm$. A full table is exactly Ramanujan's case.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:mappings}
\idea Following $x,f(x),f^2(x),\dots$ is sampling with replacement until a repeat, so the birthday sums return. The number of cycles weights the same terms
by $\frac1k$, and that weight turns $\sqrt n$ into a logarithm.

\writeup (a) The point $x$ lies on a cycle of length exactly $k$ if and only if $x,f(x),\dots,f^{k-1}(x)$ are distinct and $f^k(x)=x$. The values $f(x),f(f(x)),\dots$ are fresh uniform draws until
a repeat occurs, so the probability is $\frac{n-1}n\cdots\frac{n-k+1}n\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n^{\underline k}}{n^{k+1}}$. Sum over $k$ and over the $n$ points: $\mathbb E[\#\text{cyclic}]=\sum_k\frac{n^{\underline k}}{n^k}=Q(n)$,
and $Q(n)\sim\sqrt{\pi n/2}$ by Problem~\ref{pb:ramanujan}.

(b) Each cycle is counted once for every point on it, divided by its length: $\sum_x\frac{[x\text{ cyclic}]}{\text{length of its cycle}}$. Therefore $C_n=n\sum_k\frac1k\cdot\frac{n^{\underline k}}{n^{k+1}}$.

(c) Let $w_k=\frac{n^{\underline k}}{n^k}$. \emph{Comparison.} $\ln w_k=\sum_{j<k}\ln(1-\frac jn)$. For $k\le n^{2/3}$ and $n\ge8$, one has $\frac jn\le\frac12$, and $-x-x^2\le\ln(1-x)\le-x$ gives
$\bigl|\ln w_k+\frac{k^2}{2n}\bigr|\le\frac k{2n}+\frac{k^3}{n^2}\le2$, hence $|w_k-e^{-k^2/(2n)}|\le Ce^{-k^2/(2n)}(\frac kn+\frac{k^3}{n^2})$. Then
$\sum_k\frac1ke^{-k^2/(2n)}(\frac kn+\frac{k^3}{n^2})=O(\frac{\sqrt n}n)+O(\frac{n^{3/2}}{n^2})\to0$. For $k>n^{2/3}$, $w_k\le w_{\lceil n^{2/3}\rceil}\le e^{-n^{1/3}/3}$ and $e^{-k^2/(2n)}\le e^{-n^{1/3}/2}$, so these terms total
at most $2ne^{-n^{1/3}/3}\to0$. Therefore $C_n=S_n+o(1)$ with $S_n=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1ke^{-k^2/(2n)}$.

\emph{The sum.} With $a=n^{-1/2}$ and $\chi(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/2}-[x\le1]}x$, one has $S_n=\sum_{k\le\sqrt n}\frac1k+\sum_{k\ge1}a\chi(ka)$. The first sum is $\frac12\ln n+\gamma+o(1)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic}). The function
$\chi$ is bounded on $\left]0,1\right]$, monotone on $\left]0,1\right]$ and on $\left]1,\infty\right[$, and integrable, so the Riemann sums converge: $\sum_ka\chi(ka)\to\int_0^\infty\chi$. With $u=\frac{x^2}2$,
\[
\int_0^\infty\chi=\frac12\int_0^\infty\bigl(e^{-u}-[u\le\tfrac12]\bigr)\frac{\mathrm du}u=\frac12\Bigl(\int_0^\infty\bigl(e^{-u}-[u\le1]\bigr)\frac{\mathrm du}u+\int_{1/2}^1\frac{\mathrm du}u\Bigr)=\frac12(-\gamma+\ln2).
\]
The value $-\gamma$ comes from an integration by parts with $\ln u$, which turns the integral into $\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\ln u\dd u=\Gamma'(1)=-\gamma$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:digamma}). So
$C_n=\frac12\ln n+\gamma+\frac12(\ln2-\gamma)+o(1)=\frac12(\ln2n+\gamma)+o(1)$.

\tools{Volume~4, Chapter~1 (birthdays); Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic} (harmonic numbers) and Exercise~\ref{ex:digamma} ($\Gamma'(1)=-\gamma$); Problem~\ref{pb:ramanujan}.}
\leads Kruskal (1954) found (c), and Harris (1960) found the distributions. The rho shape of the path $x,f(x),f^2(x),\dots$, a tail and then a cycle, each of length about
$\sqrt n$, is what Pollard's factoring method uses. Computation suggests $C_n-\frac12(\ln2n+\gamma)\approx\frac{0.42}{\sqrt n}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:xplusinv}
\idea Square the recursion and it becomes linear. Then $x_n^2$ grows by $2$, plus a small term $\frac1{x_n^2}$. That term is about $\frac1{2n}$, so it sums to a logarithm. Feed the crude bound back in,
and the constant appears.

\writeup Let $y_n=x_n^2$. Then $y_{n+1}=y_n+2+\frac1{y_n}$ and $y_0=1$.

(a) From $y_{n+1}\ge y_n+2$ one gets $y_n\ge2n+1$. Then $y_n=2n+1+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{y_k}\le2n+1+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{2k+1}\le2n+2+\int_0^{n-1}\frac{\mathrm dx}{2x+1}=2n+2+\frac12\ln(2n-1)$, which is at most
$2n+2+\ln n$ for $n\ge1$.

(b) Let $\delta_k=\frac1{y_k}-\frac1{2k+1}=\frac{2k+1-y_k}{y_k(2k+1)}$. Part (a) gives $-\frac{1+\ln k}{(2k+1)^2}\le\delta_k\le0$ for $k\ge1$, and $\delta_0=0$, so $\sum_k\delta_k$ converges absolutely to some $\Delta$, and
$\sum_{k<n}\delta_k=\Delta+o(1)$. Also $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac1{2k+1}=H_{2n}-\frac12H_n=\ln2n+\gamma-\frac12(\ln n+\gamma)+o(1)=\frac12\ln n+\ln2+\frac\gamma2+o(1)$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic}). So
\[
y_n=2n+\frac12\ln n+C+o(1),\qq C=1+\ln2+\frac\gamma2+\Delta.
\]

(c) With $a_n=\frac12\ln n+C+o(1)=O(\ln n)$, one has $\sqrt{2n+a_n}=\sqrt{2n}\bigl(1+\frac{a_n}{4n}+O(\frac{a_n^2}{n^2})\bigr)=\sqrt{2n}+\frac{a_n}{2\sqrt{2n}}+O\bigl(\frac{\ln^2n}{n^{3/2}}\bigr)$, and $\frac{a_n}{2\sqrt{2n}}=\frac{\ln n+2C}{4\sqrt{2n}}+o(\frac1{\sqrt n})$.

\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~3 (sequences, comparison with integrals); Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic} (harmonic numbers).}
\leads Here $\Delta=-0.2586\ldots$, so $C=1.72314\ldots$. The constant depends on $x_0$, and no closed form for it is known. The same squaring handles $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac1{x_n^p}$: raise to the
power $p+1$. De Bruijn's \emph{Asymptotic Methods in Analysis} (1958) develops this bootstrapping for iterations in general.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:hyperfactorial}
\idea One has $\ln H(N)=\sum k\ln k$, which is minus the derivative of $\sum k^{-s}$ at $s=-1$. Differentiate the Euler--Maclaurin identity for $\zeta$, and the expansion of $\ln H$ falls out, with $\zeta'(-1)$ as
the constant.

\writeup (a) Take $M=3$ in the identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:zetaem} (it is valid for $\operatorname{Re}s>-5$) and differentiate in $s$. Both sides are holomorphic there, and the integral may be differentiated under the
integral sign. At $s=-1$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\sum_{k\le N}k^{-s}=-\sum k^{-s}\ln k$, which equals $-\ln H(N)$;
\item $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1}=-\frac{N^{1-s}\ln N}{s-1}-\frac{N^{1-s}}{(s-1)^2}$, which equals $\frac{N^2}2\ln N-\frac{N^2}4$;
\item $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\bigl(-\frac{N^{-s}}2\bigr)=\frac{N^{-s}\ln N}2$, which equals $\frac N2\ln N$;
\item $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\bigl(\frac{B_2}2sN^{-1-s}\bigr)=\frac1{12}N^{-1-s}(1-s\ln N)$, which equals $\frac1{12}(1+\ln N)$;
\item for $j=2,3$ the factor $(s)_{2j-1}$ vanishes at $s=-1$, so only its derivative remains: $(s)_3'(-1)=(-1)\cdot1=-1$, giving $\frac{B_4}{24}(-1)N^{-2}=\frac1{720N^2}$, and the $j=3$ term gives
$O(N^{-4})$;
\item likewise $(s)_6$ vanishes at $-1$, so the derivative of the remainder is
\[
-\frac{(s)_6'(-1)}{6!}\int_N^\infty\bar B_6(x)x^{-5}\dd x=O(N^{-4}).
\]
\end{itemize}
Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}\zeta'(-1)&=-\ln H(N)+\Bigl(\frac{N^2}2+\frac N2+\frac1{12}\Bigr)\ln N\\&\quad-\frac{N^2}4+\frac1{12}+\frac1{720N^2}+O(N^{-4}),\end{aligned}
\]
which is the claim, with $\ln A=\frac1{12}-\zeta'(-1)$.

(b) One has $\zeta'(-1)=-0.1654211\ldots$, so $\ln A=0.2487545$ and $A=1.2824271$. For $n=10$, $\ln H(10)=\sum_{k\le10}k\ln k=102.0828\ldots$, and the formula without the $\frac1{720n^2}$ term misses by $1.39\cdot10^{-5}$, which is $\frac1{72000}$ to
three digits.

\tools{Proposition~\ref{prop:zetaem} and Theorem~\ref{thm:holoparam} (differentiating holomorphic functions and integrals).}
\leads Kinkelin (1860) and Glaisher (1878) found this constant. Glaisher's name stayed with it. The functional equation gives $\zeta'(2)=\frac{\pi^2}6(\gamma+\ln2\pi-12\ln A)$, so $A$ also
measures how $\zeta$ moves at $2$. The constant $A$ stands to $\prod k^k$ as $\sqrt{2\pi}$ stands to $\prod k$: indeed $\ln\sqrt{2\pi}=-\zeta'(0)$, and the same differentiation at $s=0$ gives Stirling's formula.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:banach}
\idea Finding a box empty means choosing it one time too many. The recursion between consecutive probabilities sums to an equation for the mean, and what remains is a central
binomial coefficient.

\writeup (a) Suppose the left box is the empty one. That happens when the left pocket is chosen for the $(N+1)$-th time, after the right pocket has been chosen $N-r$ times: $2N-r+1$
choices, and the last one is left. The probability is $\binom{2N-r}N2^{-(2N-r+1)}$. Double it to include the right box, and the formula follows.

(b) One has $\frac{a_{r+1}}{a_r}=2\binom{2N-r-1}N\binom{2N-r}N^{-1}=\frac{2(N-r)}{2N-r}$. That is the relation, and it also holds at $r=N$ with $a_{N+1}=0$. Rewrite it as $2Na_{r+1}-ra_{r+1}=2Na_r-2ra_r$ and sum over
$0\le r\le N$. Set $E=\mathbb E[R]=\sum ra_r$, and use $\sum a_r=1$. The left side is $2N(1-a_0)-\sum_{r'\ge1}(r'-1)a_{r'}=2N(1-a_0)-E+(1-a_0)$. The right side is $2N-2E$. So $E=(2N+1)a_0-1$, with
$a_0=\binom{2N}N4^{-N}$.

(c) One has $a_0=\frac1{\sqrt{\pi N}}(1+O(\frac1N))$, so $E=\frac{2N+1}{\sqrt{\pi N}}-1+O(N^{-1/2})=2\sqrt{\frac N\pi}-1+O(N^{-1/2})$. For $N=50$, one gets $E=7.04$, against $2\sqrt{50/\pi}-1=6.98$.

\tools{Volume~4, Chapter~2 (expectations); Proposition~\ref{prop:binomcoef} (central binomial coefficients).}
\leads Feller's textbook made this ``Banach match box problem'' famous. The answer $2\sqrt{N/\pi}$ is the expected distance of a simple random walk from the origin after about $2N$ steps.
That is what $R$ is: the difference between the two boxes when one of them runs out.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:primezeta}
\idea The logarithm of the Euler product is a sum of prime zeta values at the multiples of $s$. Möbius inversion over those multiples solves for $P(s)$. Each $\zeta(ks)$ sits
extremely close to $1$, so for $s=2$ the series converges like $4^{-k}$.

\writeup (a) For $s>1$, $\zeta(s)=\prod_p(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:eulerproduct}), so $\log\zeta(s)=\sum_p\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{p^{-js}}j=\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{P(js)}j$. Every term is positive.

(b) Apply (a) at $ks$:
\[
\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\mu(k)}k\log\zeta(ks)=\sum_{k\ge1}\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{\mu(k)}{kj}P(jks)=\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{P(ms)}m\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(k)=P(s).
\]
The rearrangement is allowed because $\sum_{k,j}\frac{P(jks)}{kj}=\sum_m\frac{\tau(m)P(ms)}m$, and $P(ms)\le\zeta(ms)-1\le2^{1-ms}$ for $ms\ge3$, so the series converges.

(c) For $\sigma\ge3$, $\log\zeta(\sigma)\le\zeta(\sigma)-1\le2^{-\sigma}+\int_2^\infty t^{-\sigma}\dd t=2^{-\sigma}\bigl(1+\frac2{\sigma-1}\bigr)\le2^{1-\sigma}$. For $k\ge K+1$ and $s\ge2$,
one has $ks\ge4$, so the error is at most
\[
\sum_{k>K}\frac{2^{1-ks}}k\le\frac{2^{1-(K+1)s}}{(K+1)(1-2^{-s})}\le\frac83\cdot\frac{2^{-(K+1)s}}{K+1}\le\frac4K2^{-(K+1)s}.
\]
For $s=2$ and $K=20$ this is $0.2\cdot2^{-42}<5\cdot10^{-14}$.

(d) A bound available here: $\sum_{p>x}p^{-2}<\sum_{n>x}n^{-2}<\frac1{x-1}$, which equals $1.0000001\cdot10^{-7}$ at $x=10^7$. The two numbers differ by
$0.452247420041-0.452247414181=5.86\cdot10^{-9}$, inside that bound. For the true size of the tail we quote the prime number theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~5), which
nothing else in this solution uses. It gives $\sum_{p>x}p^{-2}\sim\int_x^\infty\frac{\mathrm dt}{t^2\log t}\sim\frac1{x\log x}$, which is $6.2\cdot10^{-9}$ at $x=10^7$. (The slightly smaller
true value comes from the next term of $\frac1{x\log x}(1-\frac1{\log x}+\dots)$.)

\tools{Theorem~\ref{thm:eulerproduct} (Euler product) and Volume~2, Chapter~4 ($\sum_{k\mid m}\mu(k)=[m=1]$). The prime number theorem is quoted in (d) for comparison only.}
\leads The same inversion computes any sum $\sum_pf(p)$ once $\sum_nf(n)$ has an Euler product. Mertens's constant and the twin prime constant are computed this way to hundreds of digits.
Adding the primes one by one stalls after a few digits: the tail decays only like $\frac1{x\log x}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:dickmanint}
\idea The delay equation $u\rho'(u)=-\rho(u-1)$ becomes an ordinary differential equation for the Laplace transform. Multiplication by $u$ is differentiation in $s$, and the delay is
multiplication by $e^{-s}$. The constant of integration is fixed at $s=\infty$ by $\rho(0)=1$. Euler's constant appears from the asymptotics of the exponential integral.

\writeup (a) For $u>1$, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}(u\rho(u))=\rho(u)+u\rho'(u)=\rho(u)-\rho(u-1)=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\int_{u-1}^u\rho$, and both sides equal $1$ at $u=1$. Suppose $\rho$ had a zero.
Let $u_0>1$ be the first one. Then $\rho>0$ on $[u_0-1,u_0[$ and $u_0\rho(u_0)=\int_{u_0-1}^{u_0}\rho>0$, a contradiction. So $\rho>0$, and $\rho'(u)=-\frac{\rho(u-1)}u<0$ for $u>1$. For
$u\ge2$, $\rho$ decreases on $[u-1,u]$, so $u\rho(u)\le\rho(u-1)$ and $\rho(u)\le\frac12\rho(u-1)$. In particular $\rho(u)\le2^{2-u}$, so $\hat\rho(s)$ is finite and smooth for
$s>-\log2$. The derivatives come from differentiating under the integral sign.

(b) On $]0,1[$, the function $u\rho(u)=u$ has derivative $1=\rho(u)-\rho(u-1)$, because $\rho(u-1)=0$. On $]1,\infty[$ this is (a). Since $u\rho(u)$ is continuous and decays exponentially,
\[
\int_0^\infty e^{-su}(u\rho(u))'\dd u=\Bigl[e^{-su}u\rho(u)\Bigr]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty ue^{-su}\rho(u)\dd u=-s\hat\rho'(s),
\]
while the left side equals $\hat\rho(s)-\int_1^\infty e^{-su}\rho(u-1)\dd u=(1-e^{-s})\hat\rho(s)$.

(c) One has $\hat\rho>0$, and $(\log\hat\rho)'(s)=-\frac{1-e^{-s}}s$ for $s>0$. The right side extends continuously to $s=0$, and $\hat\rho$ is continuous on $[0,\infty[$, so integrating from $0$ gives the formula.

(d) Write $s\hat\rho(s)=s\int_0^1e^{-su}\dd u+s\int_1^\infty e^{-su}\rho(u)\dd u=1-e^{-s}+\theta$ with $0\le\theta\le s\int_1^\infty e^{-su}\dd u=e^{-s}$, so $s\hat\rho(s)\to1$. Next,
\[
\int_0^s\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t=\int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t+\log s-\int_1^s\frac{e^{-t}}t\dd t\to\log s+\gamma+o(1),
\]
because $\int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t-\int_1^\infty\frac{e^{-t}}t\dd t=\gamma$. To see this, integrate by parts in $\Gamma'(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\log t\dd t=-\gamma$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:digamma}):
$\int_0^1e^{-t}\log t\dd t=\bigl[(1-e^{-t})\log t\bigr]_0^1-\int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t=-\int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t$ and $\int_1^\infty e^{-t}\log t\dd t=\int_1^\infty\frac{e^{-t}}t\dd t$. Finally, by (c),
\[
\int_0^\infty\rho=\hat\rho(0)=s\hat\rho(s)\cdot\exp\Bigl(\int_0^s\frac{1-e^{-t}}t\dd t-\log s\Bigr)\to1\cdot e^\gamma.
\]

(e) Abel summation gives $\sum_{n\ y\text{-smooth}}\frac1n=\int_{1^-}^\infty\frac{\mathrm d\Psi(t,y)}t=\int_1^\infty\frac{\Psi(t,y)}{t^2}\dd t$, since $\Psi(t,y)/t\to0$. With $\Psi(t,y)\approx t\rho(\frac{\log t}{\log y})$ and
$t=y^u$, one has $\frac{\mathrm dt}t=\log y\dd u$, so the sum is $\approx\log y\int_0^\infty\rho(u)\dd u=e^\gamma\log y$, which is Mertens's third theorem. Thus $\int\rho=e^\gamma$ is the
consistency condition between Dickman's function and Mertens's theorem.

\tools{Exercise~\ref{ex:digamma} ($\Gamma'(1)=-\gamma$); differentiation under the integral sign (Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals}). Mertens's third theorem (Volume~6, Chapter~1) is admitted in (e), which is a consistency check and not a proof.}
\leads The formula in (c) starts de Bruijn's asymptotic analysis of $\rho$. A saddle point on the inverse Laplace transform gives $\rho(u)=e^{-u\log u-u\log\log u+u+o(u)}$.
The counterpart for permutations is the probability that a random permutation has no cycle longer than $\frac nu$, and that probability also tends to $\rho(u)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:polymertens}
\idea In $\mathbb F_q[x]$ the prime number theorem is an identity, $\sum_{k\mid m}kN_k=q^m$. The logarithm of Mertens's product is therefore exactly the harmonic sum, up to a tiny
error. Euler's constant enters through $H_n-\log n\to\gamma$, the polynomial form of the integral $\int_0^1\frac{1-e^{-t}}t-\int_1^\infty\frac{e^{-t}}t$ from Problem~\ref{pb:dickmanint}.

\writeup (a) One has $-\log E_n=\sum_{k\le n}N_k\sum_{j\ge1}\frac{q^{-jk}}j$. The terms with $jk=m\le n$ contribute, for each $m$, $\frac{q^{-m}}m\sum_{k\mid m}kN_k=\frac1m$. Together they are $H_n$.
The other terms have $k\le n<jk$, so $j\ge2$. Using $N_k\le\frac{q^k}k$, each is at most $\frac{q^{-(j-1)k}}{jk}$. For fixed $k$ the smallest admissible $j$, call it $j_0$, satisfies $(j_0-1)k\ge\frac n2$:
if $k\le\frac n2$ then $(j_0-1)k=\lfloor\frac nk\rfloor k>n-k\ge\frac n2$, and if $k>\frac n2$ then $j_0=2$ and $(j_0-1)k=k>\frac n2$. The series in $j$ has ratio at most $q^{-k}\le\frac12$, so
\[
0\le\varepsilon_n\le\sum_{k\le n}\frac{2q^{-n/2}}{j_0k}\le q^{-n/2}H_n\le(1+\log n)q^{-n/2}.
\]
Hence $E_n=e^{-H_n-\varepsilon_n}\sim e^{-\log n-\gamma}=\frac{e^{-\gamma}}n$.

(b) A reducible polynomial of degree $n$ has a factor of degree at most $\frac n2$. The polynomials with no such factor are the irreducible ones, a proportion $\frac1n+O(\frac{q^{-n/2}}n)$
(Problem~\ref{pb:randpoly}). The prediction is $E_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sim\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{n/2}=\frac{2e^{-\gamma}}n$. The ratio of prediction to truth tends to $2e^{-\gamma}$.

(c) One has $H_n=\log n+\gamma+\frac1{2n}-\frac1{12n^2}+O(\frac1{n^4})$ (Euler--Maclaurin, Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic}), so $ne^\gamma E_n=\exp\bigl(-\frac1{2n}+\frac1{12n^2}+O(\frac1{n^4})-\varepsilon_n\bigr)=1-\frac1{2n}+O(\frac1{n^2})$.
At $n=10$, $\exp(-\frac1{20}+\frac1{1200})=0.952022\ldots$. For $q=5$, the error $\varepsilon_{10}\le(1+\log10)5^{-5}$ is small, and the exact value $0.952015$ agrees to within $10^{-5}$.

\tools{Counting irreducible polynomials (Volume~3, Chapter~9); Corollary~\ref{cor:harmonic}; Problem~\ref{pb:randpoly}.}
\leads The same factor $2e^{-\gamma}$ on both sides shows what Legendre's discrepancy is. It is what happens whenever one sieves an interval by the ``primes'' up to the
square root of its length. The analogy between $\mathbb Z$ and $\mathbb F_q[x]$, with $\log x$ in the role of the degree, runs through number theory. Rosen's book develops it.
\end{solution}
