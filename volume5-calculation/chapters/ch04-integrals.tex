\begin{center}\itshape
$\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{x-1}{\ln x}\,\dd x$ has no antiderivative you can write down.\\
A parameter in the exponent makes the value $\ln 2\approx 0.693147$.
\end{center}

For $t\ge0$ set $F(t)=\int_0^1\frac{x^t-1}{\ln x}\dd x$. Differentiate in $t$ under the integral sign, and the logarithm cancels: $F'(t)=\int_0^1 x^t\dd x=\frac1{t+1}$. So $F(t)=\ln(1+t)+C$. At $t=0$ the integrand is $0$, so $F(0)=0$ and $C=0$. The integral we wanted is $F(1)=\ln 2$. Example~\ref{ex:logint} is the place where this differentiation is proved.

Richard Feynman learned integrals from a book his high-school physics teacher gave him, and contour integration was not in that book. The parameter above was his working tool. He tells the story in \emph{Surely You're Joking, Mr.~Feynman!} (Norton, 1985), in the chapter ``A Different Box of Tools.'' Leibniz had already differentiated under an integral sign, and Euler used the move often. The swap can fail. On a closed bounded rectangle, continuity is uniform, and that is enough to pass a derivative under the integral. On an unbounded interval, one integrable function has to sit above the whole family. A continuous function on a rectangle may be integrated in either order. With those three facts we compute the Gaussian integral, Frullani's integrals, and Dirichlet's $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x\dd x=\frac\pi2$. Contour integration is Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}.

\section{Parameters on a compact interval}

Volume~1 integrated continuous functions on closed bounded intervals. Add a parameter, and continuity on a closed bounded rectangle is uniform: one $\delta$ works at every point. That is Heine's lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Heine]\label{lem:heine}
A continuous function $f$ on a closed bounded rectangle $K=[a,b]\times[c,d]$ is uniformly continuous: for every
$\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $|f(p)-f(q)|\le\varepsilon$ whenever $p,q\in K$ and $|p-q|\le\delta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose not. Then some $\varepsilon>0$ has pairs $p_n,q_n\in K$ with $|p_n-q_n|\le\frac1n$ and $|f(p_n)-f(q_n)|>\varepsilon$. Apply
Bolzano--Weierstrass (Volume~1, Chapter~3) to the first coordinates, then to the second coordinates along the extracted
subsequence. Some subsequence satisfies $p_{n_k}\to p\in K$, and then $q_{n_k}\to p$ as well. Continuity forces
$f(p_{n_k})-f(q_{n_k})\to f(p)-f(p)=0$, which contradicts the gap $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:paramcompact}
Let $J$ be an interval and $f$ continuous on $[a,b]\times J$. Then $F(t)=\int_a^bf(x,t)\dd x$ is continuous on $J$. If
moreover $\partial_tf$ exists and is continuous on $[a,b]\times J$, then $F$ is of class $C^1$ and
\[
F'(t)=\int_a^b\partial_tf(x,t)\dd x.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Continuity.}
Let $t_0\in J$ and let $[c,d]\subseteq J$ be a closed bounded interval that is a neighbourhood of $t_0$ in $J$. Given $\varepsilon>0$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:heine} on $[a,b]\times[c,d]$ gives $\delta$ with $|f(x,t)-f(x,t_0)|\le\varepsilon$ for $|t-t_0|\le\delta$, $t\in[c,d]$, so
$|F(t)-F(t_0)|\le(b-a)\varepsilon$.
\emph{The difference quotient.}
For the derivative, let $q(x,t)=\frac{f(x,t)-f(x,t_0)}{t-t_0}$. By the mean value theorem, for each $x$
there is $\tau$ between $t_0$ and $t$ with
\[
q(x,t)-\partial_tf(x,t_0)=\partial_tf(x,\tau)-\partial_tf(x,t_0),
\]
which is at most $\varepsilon$ in absolute value when $|t-t_0|\le\delta$, now with $\delta$ given by the uniform continuity of $\partial_tf$
on $[a,b]\times[c,d]$. Integrating, $\bigl|\frac{F(t)-F(t_0)}{t-t_0}-\int_a^b\partial_tf(x,t_0)\dd x\bigr|\le(b-a)\varepsilon$. So $F'(t_0)$ exists and is the
integral of $\partial_tf(\cdot,t_0)$.
\emph{The derivative is continuous.}
That integral is continuous in $t_0$ by the first part of the theorem.
\end{proof}

The bound $(b-a)\varepsilon$ is why the interval of integration has to be finite here. An infinite interval needs a different hypothesis, and that is the next section.

\begin{theorem}[Fubini on a rectangle]\label{thm:fubinirect}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]\times[c,d]$, then
\[
\int_a^b\Bigl(\int_c^df(x,y)\dd y\Bigr)\dd x=\int_c^d\Bigl(\int_a^bf(x,y)\dd x\Bigr)\dd y.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Two antiderivatives.}
For $s\in[a,b]$ let
\[
\begin{gathered}\varphi(s)=\int_a^s\Bigl(\int_c^df(x,y)\dd y\Bigr)\dd x,\qq\psi(s)=\int_c^d\Bigl(\int_a^sf(x,y)\dd x\Bigr)\dd y.\end{gathered}
\]
The inner
integral in $\varphi$ is a continuous function of $x$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact}, so $\varphi'(s)=\int_c^df(s,y)\dd y$ by the
fundamental theorem of calculus. In $\psi$, the function $g(s,y)=\int_a^sf(x,y)\dd x$ is continuous on $[a,b]\times[c,d]$ (it is
$\int_a^bf(x,y)\mathbf 1_{x\le s}\dd x$; directly, $|g(s,y)-g(s',y')|\le|s-s'|\max|f|+(b-a)\max_x|f(x,y)-f(x,y')|$) and has the
continuous partial derivative $\partial_sg(s,y)=f(s,y)$.
\emph{The same derivative.}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact}, $\psi'(s)=\int_c^df(s,y)\dd y=\varphi'(s)$.
Since $\varphi(a)=\psi(a)=0$, $\varphi=\psi$, and $s=b$ gives the theorem.
\end{proof}

Try the theorem on $f=1$ on the unit square. Both orders give area $1$. The content of the theorem is that the same equality holds for every continuous $f$, not only for products.

\section{Improper integrals with a parameter}

Let $I$ be an interval of any kind, with endpoints $\alpha<\beta$ in $[-\infty,+\infty]$, and $g$ continuous on $I$. The
\emph{improper integral} $\int_Ig$ converges if $\int_u^vg$ has a limit as $u\to\alpha$ and $v\to\beta$ independently (with $u$, $v$ in
$I$); by the Cauchy criterion this holds if $\int_I|g|<\infty$, and then $\bigl|\int_Ig\bigr|\le\int_I|g|$: we say $g$ is
\emph{integrable} on $I$. A non-negative continuous $g$ is integrable if and only if the integrals over closed bounded
subintervals are bounded, and a continuous $f$ with $|f|\le g$, $g$ integrable, is integrable (comparison). The main
example: $x^{-s}$ is integrable on $[1,\infty[$ if and only if $s>1$, and on $]0,1]$ if and only if $s<1$.

For a family of integrands, the comparison function has to be chosen once, and it has to work for every value of the parameter.

\begin{theorem}[domination]\label{thm:domination}
Let $I$, $J$ be intervals and $f$ continuous on $I\times J$, with $|f(x,t)|\le g(x)$ for all $(x,t)$, where $g$ is continuous and
integrable on $I$. Then $F(t)=\int_If(x,t)\dd x$ is continuous on $J$. If moreover $\partial_tf$ is continuous on $I\times J$ and
$|\partial_tf(x,t)|\le h(x)$ with $h$ continuous and integrable on $I$, then $F$ is of class $C^1$ and $F'(t)=\int_I\partial_tf(x,t)\dd x$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Cut off a small tail.}
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose a closed bounded $[u,v]\subseteq I$ with $\int_{I\setminus[u,v]}g\le\varepsilon$ and $\int_{I\setminus[u,v]}h\le\varepsilon$. Then
for $t,t_0\in J$, $|F(t)-F(t_0)|\le2\varepsilon+\bigl|\int_u^v(f(x,t)-f(x,t_0))\dd x\bigr|$, and the last term is at most $\varepsilon$ for $t$ close to
$t_0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact} on $[u,v]$.
\emph{The difference quotient.}
For the derivative, the difference quotient minus $\int_I\partial_tf(x,t_0)\dd x$
is, by the mean value theorem as before, at most $2\varepsilon$ outside $[u,v]$ (each term is bounded by $h$) plus a term over $[u,v]$
that tends to $0$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact}. Continuity of $F'$ follows from the first part applied to $\partial_tf$.
\end{proof}

The same cut gives the version for sequences used throughout this volume. The integrands stay continuous, and the integral is over an interval.

\begin{corollary}[dominated convergence for continuous functions]\label{cor:dct}
Let $f_n$ and $f$ be continuous on $I$, with $f_n\to f$ uniformly on every closed bounded subinterval, and $|f_n|\le g$ with $g$
continuous and integrable on $I$. Then $\int_If_n\to\int_If$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$|f|\le g$ too, and $\bigl|\int_I(f_n-f)\bigr|\le2\int_{I\setminus[u,v]}g+(v-u)\sup_{[u,v]}|f_n-f|$; choose $[u,v]$ first, then $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Volume~4, Chapter~5, proved the same dominated convergence theorem for continuous integrands. The continuous versions above are all this volume needs, and they are elementary. The
bound $g$ cannot be dropped: Exercise~\ref{ex:swapfail} shows a sequence of integrals that does not converge to the integral of
the limit.
\end{remark}

\section{Feynman's trick}

The pattern is fixed. Put the parameter where the differentiated integrand is elementary. Compute $F'$. Read the constant from a value of $t$ where $F$ is obvious, often $t=0$ or $t\to\infty$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:logint}
For $t\ge0$ let $F(t)=\int_0^1\frac{x^t-1}{\ln x}\dd x$. On $I=\left]0,1\right[$ and $J=[0,\infty[$ the integrand is continuous, with
$\partial_t\frac{x^t-1}{\ln x}=x^t$. For $t\in[0,T]$ the bounds are $\bigl|\frac{x^t-1}{\ln x}\bigr|\le t\le T$, since $1-e^{-u}\le u$ for
$u=t|\ln x|\ge0$, and $|x^t|\le1$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:domination} (on $J=[0,T]$, for every $T$), $F'(t)=\int_0^1x^t\dd x=\frac1{t+1}$. Since
$F(0)=0$, $F(t)=\ln(1+t)$; in particular
\[
\int_0^1\frac{x-1}{\ln x}\dd x=\ln2.
\]
\end{example}

At $t=1$ this is the opening integral, and $\ln 2\approx0.693147$. The bound $T$ is why the theorem is applied on each compact piece of the parameter, not on the whole half-line at once.

\begin{example}[the Gaussian integral]\label{ex:gaussfeynman}
Let $F(t)=\Bigl(\int_0^te^{-x^2}\dd x\Bigr)^2$ and $G(t)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{-t^2(1+y^2)}}{1+y^2}\dd y$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact},
\[
G'(t)=-2t\int_0^1e^{-t^2(1+y^2)}\dd y=-2te^{-t^2}\int_0^1e^{-(ty)^2}\dd y.
\]
Put $u=ty$, so $\dd u=t\,\dd y$. As $y$ runs from $0$ to $1$, $u$ runs from $0$ to $t$, and the factor $t$ cancels:
\[
\begin{aligned}G'(t)&=-2e^{-t^2}\int_0^te^{-u^2}\dd u=-F'(t),\end{aligned}
\]
since $F'(t)=2e^{-t^2}\int_0^te^{-x^2}\dd x$. So $F+G$ is constant, equal to $G(0)=\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dy}{1+y^2}=\frac\pi4$. As $t\to\infty$, $0\le G(t)\le e^{-t^2}\to0$, so
$\bigl(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\dd x\bigr)^2=\frac\pi4$, and $\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\dd x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2$. Volume~1, Chapter~8, found the same value from the Wallis integrals. This argument uses only a parameter. Volume~4, Chapter~5, takes the value from Volume~1 and uses polar rectangles to build two independent normal variables; it does not evaluate the integral.
\end{example}

The number to keep is $\frac{\sqrt\pi}2\approx0.886227$. Squaring it returns $\frac\pi4$.

\section{Frullani integrals and symmetry}

Frullani's integral compares the same function at two scales. The antiderivative never appears. Only the values at $0$ and at infinity survive.

\begin{theorem}[Frullani]\label{thm:frullani}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[0,\infty[$ with a limit $f(\infty)$ at infinity. For $a,b>0$,
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{f(ax)-f(bx)}x\dd x=\bigl(f(0)-f(\infty)\bigr)\ln\frac ba.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Cut the integral.}
For $0<\varepsilon<R$, let $I_{\varepsilon,R}=\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{f(ax)-f(bx)}x\dd x$. Substituting $u=ax$ and $u=bx$ in the two halves,
\[
\begin{aligned}I_{\varepsilon,R}&=\int_{a\varepsilon}^{aR}\frac{f(u)}u\dd u-\int_{b\varepsilon}^{bR}\frac{f(u)}u\dd u\\&=\int_{a\varepsilon}^{b\varepsilon}\frac{f(u)}u\dd u-\int_{aR}^{bR}\frac{f(u)}u\dd u.\end{aligned}
\]
(The difference of the two integrals over the common range cancels; if $a>b$, read $\int_p^q$ as $-\int_q^p$.)
\emph{The two ends.}
On
$[a\varepsilon,b\varepsilon]$, $f(u)=f(0)+o(1)$ as $\varepsilon\to0$, and $\int_{a\varepsilon}^{b\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm du}u=\ln\frac ba$, so the first term tends to
$f(0)\ln\frac ba$. Likewise the second tends to $f(\infty)\ln\frac ba$ as $R\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

With $f(x)=e^{-x}$, one has $f(0)=1$ and $f(\infty)=0$, so
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-ax}-e^{-bx}}x\dd x=\ln\frac ba.
\]
At $a=1$ and $b=2$ the value is $\ln 2\approx0.693147$. With $f(x)=\arctan x$, one has $f(0)=0$ and $f(\infty)=\frac\pi2$, so
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\arctan bx-\arctan ax}x\dd x=\frac\pi2\ln\frac ba.
\]

The substitution $x\mapsto a+b-x$ preserves $\int_a^b$. Sometimes the reflected integral is a multiple of the original plus a quantity you already know.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:logsin}
The integrals $\int_0^{\pi/2}\ln\sin x\dd x$ and $\int_0^{\pi/2}\ln\cos x\dd x$ are both equal to $-\frac\pi2\ln2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The integrals converge, since $\ln\sin x=\ln x+\ln\frac{\sin x}x$ and $\ln x$ is integrable at $0$. The substitution $x\mapsto\frac\pi2-x$
shows they are equal; call their value $L$. Then, using $\sin x\cos x=\frac{\sin2x}2$,
\[
\begin{aligned}2L&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\ln\frac{\sin2x}2\dd x\eqbr=\frac12\int_0^\pi\ln\sin u\dd u-\frac\pi2\ln2\eqbr=L-\frac\pi2\ln2,\end{aligned}
\]
because $\int_0^\pi\ln\sin u\dd u=2L$ by the symmetry $u\mapsto\pi-u$. Hence $L=-\frac\pi2\ln2$.
\end{proof}

So $\int_0^{\pi/2}\ln\sin x\dd x\approx-1.088$. The negative sign is the fact that $\sin x\le1$ on the interval, so the logarithm is not positive.

\section{Dirichlet's integral}

The function $\frac{\sin x}x$ is not integrable on $[0,\infty[$. Over $[k\pi,(k+1)\pi]$ its absolute value has integral at
least $\frac2{(k+1)\pi}$, and those lower bounds are a constant times the harmonic series, which diverges. The signed integral still converges. The positive arches and the negative arches cancel (Figure~\ref{fig:rw04-dirichlet}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\fig{ch04-rewrite-dirichlet}
\caption{The arches of $\sin x/x$ alternate. Each arch has absolute area at least $2/((k+1)\pi)$, and those lower bounds already diverge, so the absolute integral diverges. The signed integral converges.}
\label{fig:rw04-dirichlet}
\end{figure}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:secondmean}
Let $u$ be of class $C^1$, positive and non-increasing on $[A,B]$. Then $\bigl|\int_A^Bu(x)\sin x\dd x\bigr|\le2u(A)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Integrate by parts: $\int_A^Bu\sin=\bigl[-u\cos\bigr]_A^B+\int_A^Bu'\cos$, so the absolute value is at most
$u(A)+u(B)+\int_A^B|u'|=u(A)+u(B)+u(A)-u(B)=2u(A)$, since $u'\le0$.
\end{proof}

The bound depends only on the value of $u$ at the left endpoint. For $u(x)=1/x$ it says that the tail of Dirichlet's integral, from $A$ onward, is at most $2/A$.

\begin{theorem}[Dirichlet]\label{thm:dirichletint}
The improper integral $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x\dd x$ converges, and equals $\frac\pi2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{A uniform tail.}
For $t\ge0$ and $A>0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:secondmean} with $u(x)=\frac{e^{-tx}}x$ gives $\bigl|\int_A^B\frac{e^{-tx}\sin x}x\dd x\bigr|\le\frac2A$
for all $B>A$, uniformly in $t\ge0$. With $t=0$, the Cauchy criterion shows that the integral converges (near $0$ the integrand
extends continuously by $1$). Let $F(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}x\dd x$.

\emph{For $t>0$.} On $J=[t_0,\infty[$ with $t_0>0$, the integrand and its $t$-derivative $-e^{-tx}\sin x$ are bounded by $e^{-t_0x}$,
which is integrable. By Theorem~\ref{thm:domination}, $F'(t)=-\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\sin x\dd x=-\frac1{1+t^2}$ (integrate by parts twice, or take
the imaginary part of $\int_0^\infty e^{(-t+i)x}\dd x=\frac1{t-i}$). So $F(t)=C-\arctan t$ on $]0,\infty[$. Since $\bigl|\frac{\sin x}x\bigr|\le1$,
$|F(t)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\dd x=\frac1t\to0$. Also $\arctan t\to\frac\pi2$, so $C-\frac\pi2=0$ and $C=\frac\pi2$. Thus $F(t)=\frac\pi2-\arctan t$ for $t>0$. At $t=1$ this is $\frac\pi4\approx0.785398$.

\emph{At $t=0$.} For $A>0$ and $t>0$, let $D=\bigl|F(t)-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x\dd x\bigr|$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}D&\le\int_0^A(1-e^{-tx})\dd x+\frac2A+\frac2A\eqbr\le\frac{tA^2}2+\frac4A,\end{aligned}
\]
using $1-e^{-tx}\le tx$. Letting $t\to0$, then $A\to\infty$, gives $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x\dd x=\lim_{t\to0}F(t)=\frac\pi2$.
\end{proof}

The opening question now has an answer. For $t>0$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin tx}x\dd x=\frac\pi2$. For $t<0$ the same integral equals $-\frac\pi2$,
and for $t=0$ it equals $0$. The function of $t$ is a step. It is not differentiable at $0$. The differentiated integral
$\int_0^\infty\cos tx\dd x$ diverges for every $t$. No integrable bound $h$ exists, so Theorem~\ref{thm:domination} does not apply.

\section{What we proved, and what comes next}

A limit, a derivative, or a second integral may pass under the integral sign when the hypotheses above hold. On a
closed bounded interval, continuity is enough (Theorems~\ref{thm:paramcompact} and~\ref{thm:fubinirect}). On an unbounded
interval, one integrable function has to lie above the whole family (Theorem~\ref{thm:domination},
Corollary~\ref{cor:dct}). That licence gives the Gaussian integral, Frullani's formula, the symmetry for $\ln\sin x$, and Dirichlet's integral, which converges by cancellation. The damping $e^{-tx}$ is what makes the derivative legal for $t>0$. The value at $t=0$ is a limit.

The formula of Bailey, Borwein and Plouffe reaches one hexadecimal place of $\pi$ without the places before it. At $d=999999$ those digits are \texttt{26C65E52}. The Borwein integrals equal $\frac\pi2$ seven times and then miss it by about $2.31\times10^{-11}$, because a sum of lengths crosses $1$. A closed form is one half of the story. A pattern that breaks by a tiny probability is the other.

Two integrals are still waiting, as functions of their parameters: $\int_0^\infty t^{x-1}e^{-t}\dd t$ and $\int_0^1 t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\dd t$. Chapter~\ref{ch:gamma} asks which curve through the factorials has a convex logarithm, and the first of those integrals is the answer. The integrals that survive every trick of this chapter are taken up by contour integration, in Chapter~\ref{ch:complex}.

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:bricks}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item An $a\times b$ rectangle of unit squares is tiled by $1\times k$ bricks, each placed horizontally or vertically.
Show that $k$ divides $a$ or $b$. Can a $10\times10$ board be tiled by $1\times4$ bricks?

\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Journey]\label{pb:golomb}
\emph{The longest cycle.} Let $L_n$ be the length of the longest cycle of a uniformly random permutation of
$\{1,\dots,n\}$, and $E_1(u)=\int_u^\infty\frac{e^{-v}}v\,dv$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Fix $0<x<1$. Draw $N$ with $P(N=n)=(1-x)x^n$ for $n\ge0$, then a uniform permutation of $\{1,\dots,N\}$, and let
$C_j$ be its number of $j$-cycles. Show that the $C_j$, $j\ge1$, are independent Poisson variables of parameters $\frac{x^j}j$.
\item Deduce that
\[
\sum_{n\ge0}(1-x)x^n\,\mathbb E[L_n]=\sum_{m\ge0}\Bigl(1-\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{j>m}\frac{x^j}j\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\item Let $x=e^{-1/t}$. Show that the right-hand side is equivalent to $t\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-e^{-E_1(u)}\bigr)du$ as
$t\to\infty$.
\item Deduce that if $\frac{\mathbb E[L_n]}n$ has a limit $\lambda$, then
\[
\lambda=\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-e^{-E_1(u)}\bigr)du=\int_0^\infty e^{-u-E_1(u)}\,du=0.62433\ldots
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{pb:sondow}
\emph{Euler's constant as a double integral (Sondow, 2005).}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Show that
\[

\]
\item Show similarly that replacing $1-xy$ by $1+xy$ gives $\log\frac4\pi$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\end{enumerate}

\begin{solution}{ex:swapfail}
\idea The graphs are bumps of fixed area that slide towards $0$ and grow taller: no single integrable function lies above all
of them.
\writeup For $x>0$, $n^2xe^{-nx}\to0$ since the exponential beats $n^2$; at $x=0$ every term is $0$. With $u=nx$,
$\int_0^\infty n^2xe^{-nx}\dd x=\int_0^\infty ue^{-u}\dd u=1$. So $\int f_n=1\not\to0=\int\lim f_n$. Both hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{cor:dct} fail. On the closed interval $[0,1]$, $f_n(\frac1n)=\frac ne$ is unbounded, so the convergence is not uniform there. And there is no integrable dominant: $\sup_nf_n(x)\ge f_n(x)$ with $n\approx\frac1x$ gives about $\frac1{ex}$, which
is not integrable at $0$. Either failure puts the sequence outside the corollary. The integrals show what that costs.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:gausscos}
\idea Differentiating brings down $-2x\sin(2ax)$, and $-2xe^{-x^2}$ is the derivative of $e^{-x^2}$: one integration by parts returns
the original integral.
\writeup Let
\[
F(a)=\int_{\R}e^{-x^2}\cos(2ax)\dd x.
\]
The integrand and its $a$-derivative $-2xe^{-x^2}\sin(2ax)$ are bounded by $e^{-x^2}$
and $2|x|e^{-x^2}$, both integrable, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:domination}
\[
\begin{aligned}F'(a)&=\int_{\R}(-2xe^{-x^2})\sin(2ax)\dd x\eqbr=\Bigl[e^{-x^2}\sin2ax\Bigr]_{-\infty}^\infty-2a\int_{\R}e^{-x^2}\cos2ax\dd x\eqbr=-2aF(a).\end{aligned}
\]
So $(e^{a^2}F)'=0$ and $F(a)=F(0)e^{-a^2}=\sqrt\pi e^{-a^2}$, using Example~\ref{ex:gaussfeynman}. In the language of Chapter~\ref{ch:transform}: the
Gaussian is its own Fourier transform.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:sin2}
\idea Integrate by parts so as to differentiate $\sin^2x$, which brings back a sine over $x$.
\writeup On $[\varepsilon,R]$, $\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}\dd x=\Bigl[-\frac{\sin^2x}x\Bigr]_\varepsilon^R+\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sin2x}x\dd x$. The bracket tends to
$0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$ and $R\to\infty$, and with $u=2x$ the last integral tends to $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin u}u\dd u=\frac\pi2$. (This integral is
absolutely convergent, unlike Dirichlet's.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:xsinx}
\idea The factor $x$ is the only thing that breaks the symmetry $x\mapsto\pi-x$; averaging the integral with its reflection
replaces $x$ by the constant $\frac\pi2$.
\writeup Let $I$ be the integral. Since $\sin(\pi-x)=\sin x$ and $\cos^2(\pi-x)=\cos^2x$, the substitution gives
$I=\int_0^\pi\frac{(\pi-x)\sin x}{1+\cos^2x}\dd x$. Adding, $2I=\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin x}{1+\cos^2x}\dd x$, and with $u=\cos x$ this is
$\pi\int_{-1}^1\frac{\mathrm du}{1+u^2}=\pi\cdot\frac\pi2$. So $I=\frac{\pi^2}4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:loglog}
\idea Expand the logarithm that is regular at $0$ into its power series, integrate term by term against $\ln x$, and recognise
$\zeta(2)$ in what is left after partial fractions.
\writeup On $]0,1[$,
\[
\begin{aligned}\ln x\ln(1-x)&=(-\ln x)(-\ln(1-x))\eqbr=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x^n(-\ln x)}n,\end{aligned}
\]
a series of non-negative continuous functions. Its partial sums $S_N$ increase to the sum $S$, uniformly on every $[u,v]\subseteq\,]0,1[$ (the tail is at most
$|\ln u|\sum_{n>N}\frac{v^n}n$), and $0\le S_N\le S$ with $S$ continuous and integrable on $]0,1[$ (it is $O(x|\ln x|)$ at $0$ and $O(|\ln(1-x)|)$
at $1$). So Corollary~\ref{cor:dct} applies, and the integral is $\sum_n\frac1n\int_0^1x^n(-\ln x)\dd x=\sum_n\frac1{n(n+1)^2}$, using $\int_0^1x^n(-\ln x)\dd x=\frac1{(n+1)^2}$ (by parts).
With $\frac1{n(n+1)^2}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{(n+1)^2}$, the sum telescopes to $1-(\zeta(2)-1)=2-\frac{\pi^2}6$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:arctanparam}
\idea Differentiating in $a$ removes the logarithm and leaves a rational function of $x$, integrable by partial fractions.
\writeup Fix $A>0$. For $0\le a\le A$ the integrand is continuous, non-negative and at most $\frac{\ln(1+A^2x^2)}{1+x^2}$, which is
integrable (it is $O(\frac{\ln x}{x^2})$ at infinity); so $J$ is continuous on $[0,A]$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:domination}, and $J(0)=0$.
Its $a$-derivative is $\frac{2ax^2}{(1+a^2x^2)(1+x^2)}$. Near $a=0$ there is no good bound, so take $0<a_0\le a\le A$: then
$1+a^2x^2\ge a_0^2x^2$ and the derivative is at most $\frac{2A}{a_0^2(1+x^2)}$, integrable. So $J$ is $C^1$ on $]0,\infty[$ with
$J'(a)=\int_0^\infty g_a(x)\dd x$, where $g_a(x)=\frac{2ax^2}{(1+a^2x^2)(1+x^2)}$. For $a\ne1$,
\[
g_a(x)=\frac{2a}{a^2-1}\Bigl(\frac1{1+x^2}-\frac1{1+a^2x^2}\Bigr),
\]
so $J'(a)=\frac{2a}{a^2-1}\bigl(\frac\pi2-\frac\pi{2a}\bigr)=\frac\pi{1+a}$, and by continuity of $J'$ also at $a=1$. So $J(a)=\pi\ln(1+a)+C$ on
$]0,\infty[$; letting $a\to0$ and using the continuity of $J$ at $0$ gives $C=0$, so $J(a)=\pi\ln(1+a)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:bricks}
\idea A colouring argument where the colours are roots of unity. Each brick covers $k$ consecutive powers of
$\zeta=e^{2i\pi/k}$, whose sum is $0$, so the whole board has weight $0$. But the board's weight factorises. The same idea
works in the continuous case, with $e^{2i\pi x}$, whose integral over an interval vanishes exactly on intervals of integer
length.

\writeup (a) Give the cell $(i,j)$, $0\le i<a$, $0\le j<b$, the weight $\zeta^{i+j}$. A horizontal brick covers cells
$(i,j),\dots,(i,j+k-1)$, of total weight $\zeta^{i+j}(1+\zeta+\dots+\zeta^{k-1})=0$, and likewise for a vertical brick. So the
total weight is $0$:
\[
0=\sum_{i<a}\sum_{j<b}\zeta^{i+j}=\Bigl(\sum_{i<a}\zeta^i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{j<b}\zeta^j\Bigr).
\]
One factor vanishes. For $\zeta\ne1$, $\sum_{i<a}\zeta^i=\frac{\zeta^a-1}{\zeta-1}$ is $0$ if and only if $\zeta^a=1$, that is
$k\mid a$ (for $k=1$ there is nothing to prove). So $k\mid a$ or $k\mid b$. In particular a $10\times10$ board cannot be tiled
by $1\times4$ bricks, although $4$ divides its area.

(b) For a rectangle $R=[x_0,x_1]\times[y_0,y_1]$, write $\iint_R$ for the iterated integral, which does not depend on the order
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fubinirect}, applied to real and imaginary parts). The integrand is a product, so
\[
\iint_Re^{2i\pi(x+y)}\dd x\dd y=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}e^{2i\pi x}\dd x\int_{y_0}^{y_1}e^{2i\pi y}\dd y,
\]
and $\int_{x_0}^{x_1}e^{2i\pi x}\dd x=e^{2i\pi x_0}\frac{e^{2i\pi(x_1-x_0)}-1}{2i\pi}$ vanishes if and only if $x_1-x_0\in\Z$. So
the integral over $R$ is $0$ if and only if $R$ has a side of integer length. Each small rectangle gives $0$. Now extend the sides of
all the small rectangles to full lines. These lines cut the big rectangle, and each small one, into the cells of one grid.
An iterated integral over a rectangle is additive when the rectangle is cut by a vertical or a horizontal line, because an
integral over an interval is. So the integral over any of our rectangles is the sum of the integrals over its cells. The
integral over the big rectangle is therefore the sum of the integrals over the small ones, which is $0$. Hence the big
rectangle has an integer side.
\tools Volume~1, Chapter~5 (geometric sums of roots of unity); Theorem~\ref{thm:fubinirect}.
\leads De Bruijn (1969) used this to decide when an $a\times b\times c$ box can be filled with $1\times2\times4$ bricks.
Stan Wagon's paper on (b) gives fourteen proofs, from graph theory to the Euler characteristic, and the integral one is
the shortest.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:golomb}
\idea The obstacle is that the cycle counts of a permutation of fixed size are dependent, since $\sum jC_j=n$. Randomise the size
geometrically, and the dependence disappears: the counts become exactly independent Poisson variables. The longest cycle is then
easy to describe, and the average over $n$ is a generating function in $x$ that can be studied as $x\to1$.

\writeup (a) By Cauchy's formula there are $\frac{n!}{\prod j^{c_j}c_j!}$ permutations of type $c$, $\sum jc_j=n$. So
\[
P(C=c)=(1-x)x^n\prod_j\frac1{j^{c_j}c_j!}=(1-x)\prod_j\frac{(x^j/j)^{c_j}}{c_j!}=\prod_je^{-x^j/j}\frac{(x^j/j)^{c_j}}{c_j!},
\]
because $1-x=\exp(-\sum_jx^j/j)$. A joint law that factorises in this way is that of independent Poisson$(\frac{x^j}j)$
variables (summing over all $c$ gives $1$, so every $c$ with finitely many non-zero terms is accounted for).

(b) The longest cycle $L$ is at most $m$ if and only if $C_j=0$ for all $j>m$, so by (a)
$P(L\le m)=\prod_{j>m}e^{-x^j/j}=\exp(-\sum_{j>m}\frac{x^j}j)$. Then $\mathbb E[L]=\sum_{m\ge0}P(L>m)$, and conditioning on $N$
gives the left-hand side $\sum_n(1-x)x^n\mathbb E[L_n]$.

(c) Let $f(v)=\frac{e^{-v/t}}v$, decreasing on $]0,\infty[$, with $\int_m^\infty f=E_1(\frac mt)$. For $m\ge1$, comparing sum and
integral, $E_1(\frac mt)-f(m)\le\sum_{j>m}f(j)\le E_1(\frac mt)$. The function $u\mapsto1-e^{-u}$ is $1$-Lipschitz, so with
$g(u)=1-e^{-E_1(u)}$,
\[
\Bigl|\sum_{m\ge1}\Bigl(1-e^{-\sum_{j>m}f(j)}\Bigr)-\sum_{m\ge1}g\bigl(\tfrac mt\bigr)\Bigr|\le\sum_{m\ge1}f(m)=-\log(1-e^{-1/t})=O(\log t).
\]
The term $m=0$ is at most $1$. Finally $g$ is decreasing and integrable ($g\le1$, and $g(u)\le E_1(u)\le e^{-u}$ for $u\ge1$),
and since $g$ decreases, $\frac1t\sum_{m\ge1}g(\frac mt)$ lies between $\int_{1/t}^\infty g$ and $\int_0^\infty g$; it tends to $\int_0^\infty g$. So the right-hand side is
$t\int_0^\infty g+o(t)$.

(d) If $\mathbb E[L_n]=\lambda n+o(n)$, then $\sum_n(1-x)x^n\mathbb E[L_n]=\lambda\frac x{1-x}+o\bigl(\frac1{1-x}\bigr)$ (an
Abelian argument: split the $o(n)$ at a large $n_0$), and $\frac1{1-x}\sim t$. Comparing with (c), $\lambda=\int_0^\infty g$.
Integrating by parts, with $g'(u)=-\frac{e^{-u}}ue^{-E_1(u)}$ and $ug(u)\to0$ at $0$ and $\infty$:
\[
\int_0^\infty g(u)\,du=-\int_0^\infty u\,g'(u)\,du=\int_0^\infty e^{-u-E_1(u)}\,du=0.6243299885\ldots
\]
\tools Volume~3, Chapter~4 (Cauchy's formula); Volume~4, Chapters~2 and~10 (Poisson variables); this chapter (improper integrals).
\leads Golomb found the constant numerically in 1964, and Shepp and Lloyd (1966) proved that $\frac{\mathbb E[L_n]}n$ converges,
with this very Poissonisation. The same $\lambda$ is the limit of the mean of $\frac{\log P^+(n)}{\log n}$, $P^+(n)$ the largest prime
factor of $n$: Dickman had found it in 1930. Figure~\ref{fig:longest} shows the convergence.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:sondow}
\idea The factor $\frac1{-\log(xy)}$ is the integral of $(xy)^t$ over $t\ge0$. With it, everything becomes a sum of rational
functions of $t$, which integrate to logarithms; and $\gamma$ is the limit of $H_N-\log N$, a sum of $\frac1m-\log(1+\frac1m)$.

\writeup (a) For $0<u<1$, $\int_0^\infty u^t\dd t=\frac1{-\log u}$. All terms below are non-negative, so the sums and integrals may be
exchanged by Tonelli's theorem. Admitted here, proved in Volume~7, Theorem~9.5.
\[
\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\frac{1-x}{(1-xy)(-\log xy)}=\int_0^\infty\sum_{k\ge0}\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1(1-x)(xy)^{k+t}\dd x\dd y\dd t.
\]
With $a=k+t$, $\int_0^1(1-x)x^a\dd x=\frac1{(a+1)(a+2)}$ and $\int_0^1y^a\dd y=\frac1{a+1}$. So the integral is
$\sum_{k\ge0}\int_{k+1}^\infty\frac{\mathrm du}{u^2(u+1)}$ ($u=a+1$). Since $\frac1{u^2(u+1)}=\frac1{u^2}-\frac1u+\frac1{u+1}$,
\[
\int_m^\infty\frac{\mathrm du}{u^2(u+1)}=\frac1m-\log\Bigl(1+\frac1m\Bigr),
\]
and $\sum_{m=1}^M\bigl(\frac1m-\log(1+\frac1m)\bigr)=H_M-\log(M+1)\to\gamma$ (Volume~1, Chapter~3).

(b) Write $\frac1{1+xy}=\sum_{k<K}(-xy)^k+\frac{(-xy)^K}{1+xy}$. Each term $(-1)^k(xy)^k$ contributes, by the computation of (a),
$(-1)^k\bigl(\frac1{k+1}-\log(1+\frac1{k+1})\bigr)$. The remainder is at most $(xy)^K$ in absolute value, so it contributes at most
$\frac1{K+1}-\log(1+\frac1{K+1})$, which tends to $0$. So the integral is $\sum_{m\ge1}(-1)^{m-1}\bigl(\frac1m-\log(1+\frac1m)\bigr)$.
Now $\sum(-1)^{m-1}\frac1m=\log2$ (Volume~1, Chapter~3), and
\[
\sum_{m=1}^{2M}(-1)^{m-1}\log\frac{m+1}m=\log\prod_{j=1}^M\frac{2j}{2j-1}\cdot\frac{2j}{2j+1}\longrightarrow\log\frac\pi2
\]
by Wallis's product (Corollary~\ref{cor:wallis}). The result is $\log2-\log\frac\pi2=\log\frac4\pi$.

\tools{Volume~1, Chapter~3 (Euler's constant, the alternating harmonic series); Corollary~\ref{cor:wallis} (Wallis); Tonelli's theorem, admitted.}
\leads Sondow found these formulas while looking for a Beukers-type proof that $\gamma$ is irrational. Replacing $1-x$ by suitable
polynomials gives integrals equal to $a_n+b_n\gamma+c_n\log(\text{integer})$, and Sondow (2003) showed that $\gamma$ is irrational if and
only if a certain explicit sequence of such numbers is not eventually an integer. Nobody has been able to decide it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:fresnel}
\idea The computation of the Gaussian integral by a parameter goes through word for word with $i$ in place of $-1$. What changes is the end: the
auxiliary integral $G$ no longer dies by size, it dies by oscillation, and one integration by parts shows it. Then a square root has to be chosen, and
continuity chooses it.

\writeup Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact} applies to complex-valued functions, through their real and imaginary parts.

(a) $F'(t)=2e^{it^2}\Phi(t)$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact},
\[
G'(t)=\int_0^12it\,e^{it^2(1+y^2)}\dd y=2i\,e^{it^2}\int_0^te^{iu^2}\dd u=iF'(t),
\]
with $u=ty$. So $F+iG$ is constant. Its value at $0$ is $iG(0)=i\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dy}{1+y^2}=\frac{i\pi}4$, so $F(t)=i\bigl(\frac\pi4-G(t)\bigr)$.

(b) $G(t)=e^{it^2}\int_0^1e^{it^2y^2}w(y)\dd y$ with $w(y)=\frac1{1+y^2}$. Let $0<\delta<1$. The part of the integral over $[0,\delta]$ is at most $\delta$ in
modulus. On $[\delta,1]$, $e^{it^2y^2}=\frac1{2it^2y}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dy}e^{it^2y^2}$, so integrating by parts,
\[
\int_\delta^1e^{it^2y^2}w(y)\dd y=\Bigl[\frac{e^{it^2y^2}w(y)}{2it^2y}\Bigr]_\delta^1-\frac1{2it^2}\int_\delta^1e^{it^2y^2}\Bigl(\frac{w(y)}y\Bigr)'\dd y.
\]
The function $\frac{w(y)}y=\frac1{y(1+y^2)}$ is positive and decreasing, so $\int_\delta^1\bigl|(\frac wy)'\bigr|=\frac{w(\delta)}\delta-w(1)$, and the right side is at
most $\frac1{2t^2}\bigl(w(1)+\frac{w(\delta)}\delta+\frac{w(\delta)}\delta-w(1)\bigr)\le\frac1{t^2\delta}$ in modulus. So $|G(t)|\le\delta+\frac1{t^2\delta}$, and $\delta=\frac1t$ gives $|G(t)|\le\frac2t$ for $t>1$.

(c) Let $\omega=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2e^{i\pi/4}$, so that $\omega^2=\frac{i\pi}4$. By (a) and (b), $\Phi(t)^2\to\omega^2$, that is,
$\bigl(\Phi(t)-\omega\bigr)\bigl(\Phi(t)+\omega\bigr)\to0$. Choose $t_0$ such that this product is less than $\frac{|\omega|^2}4$ in modulus for $t\ge t_0$. Then for each
$t\ge t_0$ one of the two distances $|\Phi(t)-\omega|$, $|\Phi(t)+\omega|$ is less than $\frac{|\omega|}2$, and the other is more than $\frac{3|\omega|}2$, since they add up to at
least $2|\omega|$. The continuous function $d(t)=|\Phi(t)-\omega|-|\Phi(t)+\omega|$ therefore never vanishes on $[t_0,\infty[$, and by the intermediate value theorem it
keeps one sign there: $\Phi(t)$ stays near $\omega$ for all $t\ge t_0$, or near $-\omega$ for all $t\ge t_0$. Whichever it is, the small distance tends to $0$, because the
product tends to $0$ and the other factor is at least $\frac{3|\omega|}2$. So $\Phi(t)$ converges, to $\omega$ or to $-\omega$.

To decide, look at the imaginary part. With $u=x^2$, $\int_0^t\sin(x^2)\dd x=\int_0^{t^2}\frac{\sin u}{2\sqrt u}\dd u$. The arches of $\frac{\sin u}{2\sqrt u}$ over
$[k\pi,(k+1)\pi]$ alternate in sign, starting with a positive one, and their areas decrease in absolute value, because $\frac1{\sqrt u}$ decreases. So every partial
integral is positive, and the limit has imaginary part $\ge0$. Since $\operatorname{Im}(-\omega)<0$, the limit is $\omega=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2\cdot\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2}$. Its real
and imaginary parts are both $\frac12\sqrt{\frac\pi2}$.

\tools{Example~\ref{ex:gaussfeynman} (the model); Theorem~\ref{thm:paramcompact}; Volume~1, Chapter~3 (the intermediate value theorem).}
\leads These integrals carry the name of Fresnel, who met them in 1818 in his theory of the diffraction of light; Euler had found their value in 1781.
The integration by parts in (b) is the first appearance of an idea that returns in Chapter~\ref{ch:fourier}: a fast oscillation integrated against a smooth
function gives almost nothing. Lemma~\ref{lem:fresnel} of Chapter~\ref{ch:transform} proves the same value another way and uses it to evaluate Gauss sums.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw04a}
\idea The exponential integral is elementary. Its derivative in the parameter is the integral with an extra factor $-x$, once a dominant is in hand.
\writeup For $a>0$, $\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\dd x=\frac1a$. Fix $0<a_0\le a\le A$. Then $|xe^{-ax}|\le xe^{-a_0x}$, and $\int_0^\infty xe^{-a_0x}\dd x=\frac1{a_0^2}<\infty$. Theorem~\ref{thm:domination} gives
\[
F'(a)=\int_0^\infty -xe^{-ax}\dd x=-\frac1{a^2},
\]
so $\int_0^\infty xe^{-ax}\dd x=\frac1{a^2}$. At $a=3$ the integral equals $\frac19=0.111111111111$. The same value comes from one integration by parts, with no parameter: $\int_0^\infty xe^{-3x}\dd x=\bigl[- \frac x3 e^{-3x}\bigr]_0^\infty+\frac13\int_0^\infty e^{-3x}\dd x=\frac19$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw04b}
\idea Frullani needs only the values of $f$ at $0$ and at infinity. Differentiating in the second scale produces a Gaussian integral you can finish by a substitution.
\writeup The function $f(x)=e^{-x^2}$ is continuous on $[0,\infty[$, with $f(0)=1$ and $f(\infty)=0$. Theorem~\ref{thm:frullani} gives
\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{f(ax)-f(bx)}x\dd x=\ln\frac ba,
\]
and $f(ax)=e^{-a^2x^2}$. At $a=1$ and $b=2$ the integral equals $\ln 2=0.693147180560$. For the derivative, take $b\in[b_0,B]$ with $b_0>0$. Then
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\frac{e^{-a^2x^2}-e^{-b^2x^2}}x=2bx\,e^{-b^2x^2},
\]
and $|2bx\,e^{-b^2x^2}|\le 2Bx\,e^{-b_0^2x^2}$, which is integrable on $[0,\infty[$. Differentiating $\ln(b/a)$ therefore gives $\int_0^\infty 2bx\,e^{-b^2x^2}\dd x=\frac1b$. The substitution $w=b^2x^2$ has $\dd w=2b^2x\,\dd x$, so $2bx\,\dd x=\dd w/b$ and the integral is $\frac1b\int_0^\infty e^{-w}\dd w=\frac1b$. At $b=2$ both sides equal $\frac12=0.5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:rw04d}
\idea The difference of two cosines is an integral of sines. Fubini on a finite rectangle, then the Dirichlet limit, turns that into a length times $\pi/2$.
\writeup For $u>0$, the substitution $y=ux$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:dirichletint} gives $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ux)}x\dd x=\frac\pi2$. Also $\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\cos(ux)=-x\sin(ux)$, so
\[
\cos(ax)-\cos(bx)=\int_a^b x\sin(ux)\dd u
\]
when $0\le a<b$, and
\[
\frac{\cos(ax)-\cos(bx)}{x^2}=\int_a^b\frac{\sin(ux)}x\dd u.
\]
If $a>0$, integrate in $x$ from $0$ to $T$. The integrand $\sin(ux)/x$ extends continuously by the value $u$ at $x=0$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:fubinirect} on $[0,T]\times[a,b]$ gives
\[
\int_0^T\frac{\cos(ax)-\cos(bx)}{x^2}\dd x=\int_a^b D_T(u)\dd u,\qquad D_T(u)=\int_0^{uT}\frac{\sin y}y\dd y.
\]
The function $D_T$ is bounded by some $B$ independent of $T$ and $u>0$, by the same bound used for $\operatorname{Si}$ in the proof of Theorem~\ref{conjectures:thm:borwein}, and $D_T(u)\to\frac\pi2$ as $T\to\infty$, uniformly for $u\ge a$. So the integral from $0$ to $\infty$ equals $\frac\pi2(b-a)$. If $a=0$, the same identity holds on $[\delta,b]$, and the piece over $[0,\delta]$ is at most $B\delta+\frac\pi2\delta$. Let $T\to\infty$ and then $\delta\to0$. At $a=0$ and $b=2$ the value is $\pi=3.141592653590$. As a second computation of that case, integrate by parts: $\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos(2x)}{x^2}\dd x=\int_0^\infty\frac{2\sin(2x)}x\dd x=\int_0^\infty\frac{2\sin y}y\dd y=\pi$.
\end{solution}
