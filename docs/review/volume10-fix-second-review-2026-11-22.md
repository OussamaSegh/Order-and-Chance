**V10-002.** Proposition `prop:abuniv` still says that every homomorphism \(f:G\to A\) into an abelian group factors uniquely as \(\bar f\circ\pi_G\). The new proof shows \(G'\subseteq\ker f\), then: if \(gG'=hG'\), then \(h^{-1}g\in G'\subseteq\ker f\), so \(f(h)^{-1}f(g)=f(h^{-1}g)=1\) and \(f(g)=f(h)\). That is the representative check in the proof of Volume 3, Theorem 2.7. On current main, `thm:iso1` is that theorem: the shared counter in `volume3-symmetry/chapters/ch02-quotients.tex` gives Theorem 2.6 as `thm:quotient`, Theorem 2.7 as `thm:iso1`, Example 2.8 as `ex:iso1`, and Theorem 2.9 as `thm:iso23`. The displayed multiplication check shows that \(\bar f\) is a homomorphism for the quotient product \((gG')(hG')=ghG'\). The composite with \(\pi_G\) is \(f\), and any factorisation is fixed by the same formula, so it is the only one. Volume 3, Exercise 2.4 (`ex:commutator`) already shows that \(G'\) is normal; the chapter’s definition of \(G^{\mathrm{ab}}\) cites that exercise. The proposition’s statement is unchanged.

**V10-004.** Volume 1 sets the chapter counter to \(-1\) before *The Language of Mathematics*, so that chapter is Chapter 0 and *Differential and Integral Calculus* is Chapter 8. The Volume 1 index agrees: Gram–Schmidt is Theorem 7.8 and the Gram determinant is Theorem 7.10, both in the linear-algebra file, and Vandermonde is the fourth numbered statement of that file, Proposition 7.4. Inside the proof of `prop:gaussianintegral`, Chapter 8 records: for \(u\ge0\), \(\ln(1+u)\le u\); for \(0\le u<1\), \(\ln(1-u)\le -u\). The new paragraph cites that pair, puts \(u=t-1\) or \(u=1-t\), and gets \(\ln t\le t-1\) for \(t>0\). The displayed derivatives are \(1/(1+u)-1=-u/(1+u)\) and \(1-1/(1-u)=-u/(1-u)\). Dividing by \(\ln 2>0\) turns \(-\sum p_s\ln p_s\le\ln r\) into \(E(\psi)\le\log_2 r\). The Schmidt theorem already has \(r=\operatorname{rank}\Psi\le\min(m,n)\) and \(\sum\sigma_s^2=\|\psi\|^2\), so a unit state has \(\sum p_s=1\) and \(p_s=\sigma_s^2>0\). Equality in the summed inequality forces \(p_s=1/r\), and equality in the last step also forces \(r=\min(m,n)\). The Bell sentence is the case \(p=(1/2,1/2)\), whose entropy is one bit.

The value \(\varphi(X)=a\) is the value written in the definition of this particular \(\varphi\). The following uniqueness sentence said that multiplicativity forces \(\varphi(X^k)=a^k\), and it stopped there. A general polynomial is a sum of terms \(c_kX^k\), so additivity is the remaining step. The think box uses that uniqueness. The several-variable paragraph already said that the images of the indeterminates fix every monomial and every sum; the one-variable paragraph now says the same, in order: the formula gives \(\varphi(1)\) and \(\varphi(X)\), the initial-object rule fixes every integer, multiplicativity fixes the powers, and additivity fixes the sum. The displayed product identity was left as it stood.
