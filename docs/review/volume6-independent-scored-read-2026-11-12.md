### Justifications

**Chapter 1.** Theorems 1.3, 1.5 and 1.6, and the Abel lemma they rest on, were re-read line by line. The constant \(M+H=\gamma\) is derived from \(\zeta(s)=1/(s-1)+\gamma+O(s-1)\) (Volume 5, Proposition 10.6, which is that expansion) and \(\Gamma'(1)=-\gamma\) (Volume 5, Exercise 5.7, whose solution proves it). The passage of the integral down to \(u=0\) is not estimated (V6-18). Chebyshev’s input for the product of primes is Volume 2, Theorem 2.4(i), and that part says what this chapter uses. The sentence that Theorem 2.4 gives both sides of \(\pi(x)\asymp x/\log x\) is one step looser: the upper bound sits in the paragraph after the theorem (V6-21). Proposition 1.2(b) prints \(4\log 4\); the argument on the page gives \(2\log 4\) (V6-15). Recomputed: \(R(10^3)=-1.298245\), \(R(10^5)=-1.328631\), \(R(10^7)=-1.332395\), the claimed limit \(-1.33258\), \(\theta(3000)/3000=0.97745\), \(\psi(3000)/3000=1.00037\), and the product over \(p\le 1000\) is \(0.08097\) against \(e^{-\gamma}/\log 1000=0.08128\). The beauty box’s \(\approx 0.0813\) is a fair rounding of that pair. The solution of Exercise 1.2 says the primes to \(10^7\) already give ten digits of \(M\); they give eight (V6-01). The solution of Exercise 1.4 quotes \(3.0\) prime factors at \(10^9\), which is \(\log\log 10^9\), not the mean \(\log\log x+M\approx 3.29\) just proved (V6-17). The solution of Exercise 1.7 states the right expectation and multiplies two counts that still differ from it by \(n!\) (V6-16). The two figures match their captions, including the colour names after the series palette remaps `MidnightBlue` and `BurntOrange`.

**Chapter 2.** Wintner’s theorem, the three error terms, the hyperbola identity, and the algebra of the \(\tau_3\) main term were checked. The citations Volume 2, Theorem 4.6 and Exercise 4.8, and Volume 5, Corollary 10.4, Corollary 11.6 and Theorem 10.3, land on the statements used. Recomputed: the mean of \(\tau(n)\) for \(n\le 10^6\) is \(13.970\), the median is 8, the proportion with at least 14 divisors is \(0.343\), \(\tau(720720)=240\), 359 of the 576 lattice points in the \(24\times 24\) square are visible, and \(\tau(n)\le\sqrt{3n}\) holds for every \(n\le 10^6\) with equality only at \(n=12\). The comparison \(\sum_{m\le x}m^{-1/2}\le\int_0^x t^{-1/2}\,dt\) in the \(\tau_3\) proof is true but not the comparison that was proved. Walfisz’s exponent \((\log x)^{2/3}(\log\log x)^{4/3}\) matches the statement in the literature (Liu’s note, arXiv:1811.02556, records Walfisz’s bound in that form). The solution of Exercise 2.9(a) concludes \(v\mid\gcd(a,b)\) from \(v\mid ua\) and \(v\mid ub\) without naming Euclid’s lemma (V6-22). The conclusion is the right one.

**Chapter 3.** The conductor, the Gauss-sum evaluation \(|\tau(\chi)|=\sqrt q\), and both halves of \(L(1,\chi)\ne 0\) were checked, including the Taylor identity that turns \(\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)\) at \(s=1/2\) into \(\sum r(n)n^{-1/2}\). The Leibniz evaluation \(L(1,\chi_{-4})=\pi/4\) from \(\tau(\chi_{-4})=2i\) is correct. Volume 3, Proposition 11.15 is the Dirichlet orthogonality relation this book filters with, and Volume 5, Proposition 7.13 and Theorem 8.14 are the quadratic Gauss sum and its sign. The chapter is denser than Chapters 1 and 2; a reader who has only just met characters will feel it. The two figures were not recomputed from their plotted data.

**Chapter 4.** Dirichlet’s theorem (Theorem 4.1) is the filter plus \(L(1,\chi)\ne 0\), and the bookkeeping was checked. The race to \(60\,000\) was recomputed: the ten ties are at the primes 5, 17, 41, 461, 26833, 26849, 26863, 26881, 26893 and 26921, and \(4n+1\) leads only for \(26861\le x\le 26862\). The partial sums at \(10^6\) for the two classes modulo 3 are \(-1.1866\) and \(-0.5116\), which round to the caption’s \(-1.187\) and \(-0.512\). The limits are \(-1.18775\) and \(-0.51104\), so \(-1.188\) and \(-0.511\) are the right three decimals, and the gap \(0.6767\) rounds to the printed \(0.677\). The clause that calls \(0.308\) the prime powers in the single class \(p^k\equiv 1\) names the wrong sum (V6-19). The caption states \(99.59\%\) as a fact; the remark on the same page says the figure is conditional (V6-20). Volume 2, Exercise 2.1 is the infinitude of primes \(4n+3\), and Exercise 5.7 is the infinitude of primes \(1\pmod n\). In the solutions, the reciprocity step for \(\bigl(\frac{-3}{p}\bigr)\) hides a sign that cancels (V6-24), and the Euclidean-polynomial sentence gives Schur’s existence half to Murty (V6-23).

**Chapter 5.** Newman’s contour, the continuation \(G(z)=-\frac1s(Z'/Z+1)\), the value \(-1-\gamma\) of the integral, the passage to \(\psi(x)\sim x\), and the proof that \(\sum\mu(n)/n=0\) were checked sign by sign. Recomputed: \(\pi(10^8)=5761455\) is the value used, \(\mathrm{Li}(10^8)-\pi(10^8)=753.33\), \(\pi\log x/x=1.06130\), and \(\pi/\mathrm{Li}=0.999869\). The partial sums of \(\mu(n)/n\) are \(-0.002083\), \(0.000201\) and \(0.000102\) at \(10^4\), \(10^6\) and \(10^7\). Volume 5, Theorems 9.15 and 9.26 are the parameter-integral theorem and the residue theorem. The remark that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to \(M(x)=O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})\) states a converse this book does not prove (issue V6-02). The solution of the character series states the right limit \(-\frac{L'}{L}(1,\chi)\) and then equates the series to the positive value (V6-27).

**Chapter 6.** The constants in Lemma 6.6 were recomputed from the Borel–Carathéodory bounds the proof quotes: the extra Blaschke terms contribute less than \(\log M/R\) and less than \(\log M/R^2\), which is where 17 and 129 come from. The zero-free arithmetic was recomputed: with \(\delta=1/(2A')\) one gets \(1-\beta_0\ge 1/(14A'\log|t|)\), as written. \(\xi(0)=1/2\) is in Proposition 6.2 and is what Hadamard’s product uses. The opening calls the infinitude of zeros the Riemann hypothesis; Corollary 6.8 then proves infinitude, and the closing correctly names the hypothesis as the critical line (V6-25). The Riemann–von Mangoldt contour and the Stirling proof were not line-checked, so the rigor score stays below the chapters that were. The records “one third, two fifths, five twelfths” are explicitly quoted, which is the right way to handle a moving bound. The staircase caption’s \(29\) steps, \(\gamma=14.13\) and \(98.83\), and \(|S(T)|<0.83\) match the known ordinates; the caption does not say they are computed (V6-32). \(N(200)=79\) is consistent with the main term and was not checked zero by zero.

**Chapter 7.** Perron’s three cases, the residues of \(-\zeta'/\zeta\cdot x^s/s\), and the identity \(-\frac12\log(1-x^{-2})=\sum_{k\ge 1}x^{-2k}/(2k)\) were checked. The constant \(\zeta'/\zeta(0)=\log 2\pi\) follows from the value of \(B\) in Exercise 6.1, which is earlier in the book; the algebra \(B+1+\frac12\log\pi+\gamma/2=\log 2\pi\) is correct, and \(B=-0.0230957\ldots\) matches \(\frac12\log(4\pi)-1-\gamma/2\). Theorem 7.5 and Corollary 7.6 are the explicit formula for \(\psi_0\), with the symmetric limit. The introduction displays the same formula for \(\psi\), and the line after the proof says that display is what was proved (V6-26). At a prime the two functions differ by \(\frac12\log p\). The de la Vallée Poussin error balances at \(\log T=\sqrt{\log x}\). The zero-free input is written with \(\log T\), where Theorem 6.12 has \(\log(|t|+2)\); a slightly smaller \(c\) still works (V6-30). The functional equation of \(L(s,\chi_{-4})\) is labelled admitted in the remark after Proposition 7.11 and is not used in that proposition. The same remark attaches Littlewood’s unconditional theorem to the conditional wave formula (V6-29), and the sum over ordinates is not marked as two-sided (V6-31). Volume 13, Chapter 12 is Tate’s thesis, and its corollary `cor:dirichletFE` is the functional equation of a primitive Dirichlet \(L\)-function. The solution of Exercise 7.3 drops the quantifier “for every \(\varepsilon>0\)” and still concludes \(\beta\le\frac12\) (V6-28).

**Chapter 8.** Selberg’s diagonalisation and the bound \(|\lambda_d|\le 1\) were read. Brun–Titchmarsh really does produce the factor 2: \(G(y)\ge\frac12\log N-\log\log N\) gives \(\frac{2N}{\log N}(1+O(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}))\), and the text separates that short-interval ratio from the arithmetic-progression inequality whose constant 2 is tied to Siegel zeros. The twin argument produces a constant about 64, as the remark says. Recomputed: \(\pi_2(10^7)=58980\), and \(2C_2\int_2^{10^7}dt/\log^2 t=58753.8\), which rounds to the caption’s \(58\,754\). The product for \(C_2\) through the primes to \(10^7\) is already \(0.66016182\). Buchstab’s function is an exercise, not a theorem, and the crossings of \(e^{-\gamma}\) recomputed from the delay equation are \(1.781\), \(2.483\), \(3.227\) and \(4.002\), as the caption says.

**Chapter 9.** The moment comparison (Lemmas 9.4 and 9.5) and the truncation \(z=x^{1/L^{1/4}}\) were checked, including \(\mathbb E[S_z^k]=(k-1)!!\,\sigma_z^k+O_k(\sigma_z^{k-2})\) for even \(k\). At \(x=10^7\), \(1/\sqrt{\log\log x}=0.600\) and the proportion of \(n\) with \(\omega(n)\le\log\log x\) is \(0.320\); the model’s skewness on the primes to \(10^7\) is \(0.488\), against the text’s \(0.49\). The passage from moments to the distribution function (Lemma 9.6) calls on dominated convergence and Scheffé’s lemma. Volume 4’s dominated-convergence theorem needs local uniform convergence, which is not checked (issue V6-03). The theorem is the right one; the write-up is one step short of the hypothesis it cites.

**Chapter 10.** Hoeffding’s inequality in Volume 4 is Theorem 4.2, and Borel–Cantelli is Theorem 4.4; the two-sided bound \(2\exp(-2t^2/N)=2/N^2\) is a valid weakening of the finite-sum inequality. Theorem 10.4 states \(\limsup(q_{i+1}-q_i)/\log^2 q_i=1\) for the model, which is what later volumes mean by the prediction \(\limsup G(X)/\log^2 X=1\). The record gaps in the caption were checked against the tables of maximal gaps: 354 after \(4\,302\,407\,359\), 1132 after \(1\,693\,182\,318\,746\,371\) (Nyman, 1999), and 1476 after \(1\,425\,172\,824\,437\,699\,411\). The probability space for unequal probabilities \(1/\log n\) is asserted rather than built (issue V6-04). Maier’s theorem is a remark and is pointed at Volume 15. Buchstab is “the function of Problem 8.10”, not a theorem already proved.

**Chapter 11.** The geometric bound \(1/(2\|\beta\|)\) uses \(\sin\pi t\ge 2t\) on \([0,\frac12]\), which is the concavity of sine, and the proof of van der Corput’s inequality is the usual Cauchy–Schwarz count. Weyl’s criterion is Volume 5, Theorem 6.13 (the equidistribution chapter is Chapter 6 of that book; the number was not re-counted environment by environment). The derivative tests, Pólya–Vinogradov, and the exercises were sampled, not line-checked. The curlicue caption’s distance 36 against \(\sqrt{2000}\approx 44.7\) was not recomputed from the path.

**Chapter 12.** Both halves of the fundamental domain are proved (Theorem 12.2). \(\Delta=\eta^{24}\) is shown never zero on the upper half-plane before it is used to divide. The dimension recursion, the identity \(E_4^2=E_8\), and the coefficient arithmetic modulo 691 were checked: \(720\cdot 691-65520=432000\), \(65520\equiv -125\) and \(432000\equiv 125\pmod{691}\), and \(125\) is invertible. \(\sigma_7(2)=129=9+120\). The second proof of the transformation of \(\eta\) is Siegel’s contour; it was read through the residue calculation at 0 and not through the limit \(N\to\infty\). Volume 5, Theorem 12.4 is the transformation of \(\eta\) in the Hardy–Ramanujan chapter, which is that book’s Chapter 12. The closing sentence of the chapter uses the word “landscape” once (issue V6-05). After six exercises on the chapter, thirty-nine further problems begin, announced as closing problems. They are graded and solved, and several of them are substantial (Euler’s polynomial \(n^2+n+41\) really does take 261081 prime values for \(n<10^6\), as the text says; \(n^2+1\) takes 54110). They are not a graded path through modular forms, and they make this chapter the long one (issue V6-06).

## 3. Overall

**8.**

The book does the thing its preface promises. A reader who has Volumes 1–5 can be shown Mertens’s theorems, Dirichlet’s theorem, the prime number theorem, a classical zero-free region, the explicit formula, Selberg’s sieve, Brun’s theorem on twins, and the shape of Erdős–Kac and of Cramér’s model, with the proofs written out and the numbers in the captions checked against independent calculations. The two admitted facts are labelled, and they sit where the preface says they sit. I would give this book to the reader it was written for. I would attach a short errata list. I would not send it back for a rewrite.

The score is not 9, and it is not 7 either. Two framings are wrong and are corrected later in the same chapters: the opening of Chapter 6 calls the infinitude of zeros the Riemann hypothesis, and the opening of Chapter 7 writes the explicit formula for \(\psi\) where the theorem is for \(\psi_0\) (V6-25, V6-26). The theorem boxes — Newman, the zero-free region, von Mangoldt’s formula for \(\psi_0\) — are the right statements. The score stays at 8 for that reason, and because of the thinner justifications already listed: the ten digits of \(M\), the equivalence of the Riemann hypothesis with the size of \(M(x)\), the appeal to dominated convergence, the doubled Chebyshev constant, the probability space of Chapter 10, and the missing index.
