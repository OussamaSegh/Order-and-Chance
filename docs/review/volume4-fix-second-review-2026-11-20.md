**Euler's formula.** `lem:tree` is an induction on the number of vertices. A longest path in a finite tree has two distinct ends, each of degree one, and deleting one leaf leaves a tree. A connected graph has a spanning tree: a loop lies on no path, and any other cycle edge can be replaced by the rest of the cycle. For a plane tree the face count is an induction on that drawing. One vertex leaves one face. Restoring a leaf curve into that face does not split it, so every plane tree has \(v-e+f=2\). Each edge put back into a connected plane graph has its interior in one face, and the two shore arcs split that face, so \(e\) and \(f\) rise together. A bridge is an edge whose two shores lie on the same face, and the shore walk traverses it twice. Face degrees therefore add to \(2e\). A simple connected plane graph with \(v\ge 3\) has no face of degree less than 3, so \(2e\ge 3f\) and \(e\le 3v-6\). The disconnected arithmetic is the paragraph the review already accepted. The induction uses paths, cycles, and trees defined in the section, induction and the least-upper-bound facts from Volume 1, and no later theorem.

**Sectors.** `lem:sector` starts from the quarter-disk integral in Volume 1, Chapter 8. The unit disk is the two halves. A sector from the positive axis of angle less than \(\pi/2\) is a triangle plus a circular cap; the cap is \(\int\sin^2\), and the chain rule from Volume 1, Chapter 3, integrates \(\cos 2\phi\). A sector inside one quadrant is a difference of two such sectors. Sign changes move that difference to the other quadrants. A sector of angle at most \(2\pi\) splits at the axes into at most four of those pieces. Scaling by \(a\) multiplies the area by \(a^2\). The polar cell is the difference of two such sectors. The symmetric difference with the disk sits in the radii and the outer circle, and a neighbourhood of those curves can be made arbitrarily thin.

The bound \(R(k,k)>2^{k/2}\) for \(k\ge 3\) is the same conclusion, including \(2^{5/2}<6\).
