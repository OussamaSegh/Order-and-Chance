### Part (a), Minkowski for a centred closed rectangle: two proofs

**First general proof, the manuscript's continuous area-overlap route.** Let S be any *closed*, centred rectangle, in any orientation, with area(S)≥4. Set K=(1/2)S, so area(K)≥1. If area(K)>1, cut K by the integer unit-square tiling z+[0,1)². Translate each cut piece into [0,1)². Because the sum of their areas is area(K)>1, the translated pieces cannot be disjoint up to zero-area intersections: two contain common points. We can choose **distinct** x,y∈K, in different integer cells, with x−y∈Z² nonzero. As K is centrally symmetric and convex, x−y∈K−K=(1/2)S−(1/2)S=S. Therefore S contains a nonzero lattice point. Notice that the exact value 4 comes from the halving in each of two coordinates, which makes area(K)=area(S)/4.

**Exact equality (area(S)=4), first route.** For each integer m≥1 enlarge S to S_m=(1+1/m)S. Its area exceeds 4, so choose a nonzero lattice point z_m∈S_m. All lie in bounded 2S, which contains *finitely* many lattice points. At least one fixed z≠0 recurs along unbounded m. Since S is closed and S_m contracts to S, that fixed z belongs to S. **Closed is necessary** at equality: the open square (-1,1)² has area4 but contains no nonzero lattice point. This precise counterexample and boundary explanation were inserted into the book, commit `78d4aa99d22f9609dd726499862b97e77e5ef201`.

**Second general proof, finite-grid pigeonhole, independent of the continuous overlap.** For area(K)>1, choose integer q large and count the points of the rational grid (1/q)Z² lying in K. For a rectangle with finitely many straight edges, elementary square-mesh area counting gives
#(K∩(1/q)Z²)=q² area(K)+O(q).
A direct reason: after multiplying by q, count the full integer unit squares inside the expanded rectangle; only O(q) unit squares meet its four boundary segments (each has length O(q)), so the difference between area and the count of their grid vertices is O(q). Because area(K)>1, for sufficiently large q there are **more than q² rational grid points** in K. Among their integer coordinate numerators (i,j), only q² residue pairs modulo q exist. Two distinct grid points have the same pair of residues, hence their difference is an **integer** nonzero vector z. Since K−K=S by central symmetry, z∈S. **`thm:cyclic`.** Pass A: if exact order `d` occurs, one element's `d` powers exhaust the degree-`d` roots of `X^d-1`, so exactly `phi(d)` elements have order `d`.
