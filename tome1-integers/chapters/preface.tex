\chapter*{Preface}
The integers are the oldest objects of mathematics and still among the most mysterious. A child can ask their
open questions; the answers call on the whole of mathematics. This volume of \emph{Order and Chance}
studies them with elementary tools, and discovers at every turn that elementary does not mean easy.

Two gear wheels, a repeating decimal, a triangle with integer sides: these are invitations to the same adventure.
One starts by calculating, notices a pattern, makes a conjecture, breaks it with a counterexample, and eventually
finds a proof. A proof is the moment when the pattern stops being a coincidence. The jokes in the margins of
this journey are optional; the hypotheses of a theorem are not.

The book starts with divisibility and Euclid's algorithm, the primes and their infinitude, and congruences, the
arithmetic of remainders. Arithmetic functions and their Dirichlet convolution give a first glimpse of the
analytic theory of Tome~5. The non-zero remainders modulo a prime form a cyclic multiplicative group, and their
squares obey the law that Gauss called the \emph{theorema aureum}: quadratic reciprocity, the centrepiece of the
book. Its two proofs show different reasons for the same astonishing symmetry.

From there we follow questions that look innocent: which numbers are sums of two or four squares, how
continued fractions find good rational approximations and solve Pell's equation, and how to prove that an
integer equation has no solution. The questions force us to enlarge the integers, first to the Gaussian and
Eisenstein integers, then to quadratic fields and ideals, where unique factorisation is restored after it fails.
Finally we reach the $p$-adic numbers, in which divisibility by $p$ becomes nearness. The integers have acquired
geometry, algebra and analysis without ceasing to be the integers.

\section*{What to bring}
Chapters~1--7 require arithmetic with fractions, induction, elementary polynomial algebra and a willingness to
work through examples. We use the basic language of sets and functions. Groups, rings, units and ideals are
explained as they arise; a previous abstract-algebra course is not assumed. Later chapters use real limits,
geometric series and the completeness of $\R$; Chapter~10 also uses complex numbers and roots of unity.
Tome~0 supplies these foundations, including the polynomial root bound and Gauss' lemma for polynomials.
Calculus appears in a few estimates and comparisons, rather than as a prerequisite to the opening chapters.

Every numbered theorem, proposition, lemma and corollary has a proof, with earlier dependencies identified.
Deep results mentioned as destinations, such as the prime number theorem, the three-square theorem and the
Hasse--Minkowski theorem, are explicitly distinguished from results proved here. The point is to know exactly
what has been established, what is being used, and what remains beyond the horizon.

\section*{How to travel}
For a first reading, follow Chapters~1--6 in order: divisibility leads to congruences, congruences to multiplicative
orders, and orders to quadratic reciprocity. Chapters~7--9 apply the toolkit to representations, approximations
and equations. Chapter~10 supplies the ring arithmetic needed for Chapter~11; Chapter~12 returns to congruences
and builds a new kind of analysis. The final \emph{Arithmetic Field Guide} collects notation, proof strategies and
warnings for use while solving problems.

A shorter elementary route is Chapters~1--7 followed by the continued fractions and Pell sections of Chapter~8.
A computational route starts with Chapters~1, 3 and 5, and their notebooks, then returns to the explanations that
make the algorithms reliable. Neither route requires rushing: a page that takes an afternoon is sometimes a
very good page.

At each worked example, cover the next line and predict it. At a ``Pause and think'' box, make a guess before
reading on. Try a small case and an awkward case: $0$, $1$, the prime $2$, a repeated prime factor. Many false
proofs have excellent manners until they meet one of these guests. Diagrams help us conjecture, but their
captions say what they establish; a handsome picture is not an induction argument.

Exercises are graded \textbf{$\star$} for a warm-up, \textbf{$\star\star$} for a longer investigation and
\textbf{$\star\star\star$} for a substantial challenge. Each has a hint and a complete solution at the end of its
chapter. A solution begins with \emph{How to find it}, explaining what to notice, before the \emph{Write-up}.
Try the hint before the solution, and after reading a solution close the book and reconstruct the key step.
That last small act turns recognition into understanding.

Each chapter has a Python companion notebook: exact computations, plots, experiments and applications to
computing, including modular exponentiation, primality tests and RSA. Experiments test claims on finite data;
they do not replace proofs about all integers. Cryptographic examples are deliberately small demonstrations,
not systems suitable for protecting information. Historical portraits and photographs are credited where they
appear and in the illustration credits. The new mathematical diagrams are drawn for this volume and remain
readable in black and white.
