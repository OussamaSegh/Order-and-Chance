# Description

A child can state the open problems about whole numbers; their theorems call on the whole of mathematics. On Christmas Day 1640, Fermat wrote that every prime of the form 4n + 1 is a sum of two squares, and kept the proof to himself. A few weeks before his nineteenth birthday, Gauss proved the law of quadratic reciprocity, which he called the golden theorem. In 1847 a claimed proof of Fermat's Last Theorem collapsed in front of the Paris Academy, because unique factorisation can fail. These stories lead to the mathematics: concrete questions, careful definitions and proofs with their hypotheses made explicit.

Twelve chapters go from divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm, primes and congruences to arithmetic functions, primitive roots and the cyclic group of remainders modulo a prime, and quadratic reciprocity, proved more than once because each proof shows a different reason. Then come innocent questions: which numbers are sums of two or four squares, how continued fractions find the best approximations and solve Pell's equation (including Archimedes' cattle problem, whose answer has 206,545 digits), which Diophantine equations can be solved at all. To answer them the book enlarges the integers to Gaussian and Eisenstein integers, to ideals, where unique factorisation is restored, and to the p-adic numbers.

Every numbered theorem, proposition, lemma and corollary has a proof; advanced results quoted for perspective are clearly identified. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas, has exercises graded from warm-ups to gems, each with a hint and a complete solution that first explains how to find the idea, worked examples, mathematical diagrams, historical portraits and photographs, think boxes, and sources for further reading. An arithmetic field guide collects notation, problem-solving strategies and common pitfalls.

Every chapter comes with a Python notebook that follows the arithmetic into modern computing, from fast exponentiation to RSA.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
