# Description

In 1946 the best mathematicians feared that computers would drown in their own rounding errors. Within twenty years James Wilkinson had shown why the algorithms of linear algebra can be trusted, and the mathematics of data had begun. This book tells that story and the ones that followed it: how to compute with matrices, how to minimise functions as fast as is possible, what a sample of data can tell us, and why a machine that has seen finitely many examples can predict the next one. Every method in it comes with a proof that it works and a proof of how well.

Twelve chapters. Computing with matrices: floating point, condition numbers, backward stability of Householder reflections, the QR algorithm and the singular value decomposition. Krylov spaces and random sketches: conjugate gradients, random projections, spectral clustering. Convexity and duality. Gradient descent and Nesterov's acceleration, with the lower bound that proves it optimal, drawn on the cover. Sparsity, proximal methods and compressed sensing. Newton's method and interior points, which solve linear programs in polynomial time. Learning online: experts, bandits and stochastic gradients. Estimation: maximum likelihood, the Cramer-Rao bound and the paradox of James and Stein. Tests, confidence intervals, the false discovery rate and the bootstrap. Bayesian inference and Markov chain Monte Carlo, with conductance and coupling. Why learning generalises: PAC learning, the Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension and boosting. Kernels and neural networks, up to universal approximation and Barron's theorem.

Every theorem is proved in full. Every chapter opens with a story and a question to think about, and has exercises graded by difficulty, each with a hint and a solution that first explains how to find the idea and then writes it up. Figures show the constructions, research papers come with reading guides, and every chapter ends with its sources.

Each chapter comes with a substantial Python notebook that implements its methods from scratch, checks every number in the text, and measures the algorithms against the bounds proved for them.

The book is written for the pleasure of seeing why things are true. It is the fifth volume of the computing track of a series that goes from the foundations of number theory, algebra and probability to the edge of research.
