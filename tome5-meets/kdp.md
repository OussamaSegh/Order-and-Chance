# Description

The prime numbers look random: no formula produces them, twin primes keep appearing, and so do long gaps. Yet in bulk they obey precise laws. In 1792 the fifteen-year-old Gauss counted primes and guessed that near x they have density 1/log x. In 1853 Chebyshev noticed that primes of the form 4n + 3 seem to win their race against 4n + 1. In 1916 Ramanujan looked at the coefficients of an infinite product and saw patterns he could not prove. This book proves the laws behind all three, with the number theory, algebra, analysis and probability of the earlier volumes working together.

It starts with Mertens's theorems and the averages of arithmetic functions, builds Dirichlet's L-functions to find primes in every arithmetic progression, and proves the prime number theorem. It studies the zeros of the zeta function and Riemann's explicit formula, in which the primes are a superposition of waves, one for each zero, and explains why the 4n + 3 primes lead almost all the time. Then come sieves and twin primes, the Erdős–Kac theorem, which says that the number of prime factors of a typical integer follows the bell curve, Cramér's random model of the primes and what it predicts about gaps, exponential sums, and a first taste of modular forms, where Ramanujan's observations become theorems.

Every result is proved in full. Each chapter opens with the story behind its ideas and has graded exercises, each with a hint and a complete solution that explains how to find the idea, figures, and sources for further reading.

Every chapter has a Python notebook that turns the computer into a laboratory for primes.

Written for curious minds with a taste for rigour, who read mathematics for the pleasure of understanding it.
